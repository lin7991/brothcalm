Do your eyes feel gritty, heavy, or strained by late afternoon — especially after a long day of screens? You are in good company. Dry eyes, eye fatigue, and blurry vision have become the quiet background noise of modern life. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has been describing this exact feeling for more than two thousand years, and it has always pointed to the same place first: the Liver.

In TCM, the eyes are known as the "window" of the Liver. The ancient phrase gan kai qiao yu mu (肝开窍于目) — "the Liver opens to the eyes" — teaches that clear, comfortable vision depends on Liver Blood flowing up to moisten and nourish the delicate tissues of the eye. When that nourishment runs low, or when heat rises from the Liver, the eyes are the first place the imbalance shows. The good news is that this old logic leads straight to the kitchen. Let's look at how TCM connects the eyes and the Liver, which foods it has traditionally reached for, and how to brew a simple daily tea for tired eyes.

Why TCM Connects the Eyes to the Liver

To understand the food list that follows, it helps to know the two ideas doing the heavy lifting. First, the Liver stores Blood. In TCM, the Liver is the organ that stores and regulates the body's blood supply, releasing it where it is needed — and the eyes are among the thirstiest customers. When Liver Blood is abundant, the eyes are moist, bright, and steady; when it runs thin, eyes tend toward dryness, fatigue, and blurriness, often alongside pale nails, scanty periods, and restless sleep. This is why eye care in TCM is rarely just about the eyes — it is about the blood that feeds them.

Second, prolonged staring depletes that blood. An old TCM principle, jiu shi shang xue (久视伤血), holds that "prolonged gazing injures the blood." Long before smartphones, this referred to scholars reading by candlelight; today it describes eight hours of spreadsheets and an evening of scrolling. Meanwhile, stress, spicy food, alcohol, and late nights can stir up what TCM calls Liver Fire or Liver Heat — a rising, drying heat that shows up as red, sore, or gritty eyes. So the traditional food strategy has two jobs: nourish Liver Blood to support the eyes, and clear Liver Heat to keep them comfortable.

Goji Berries — The Classic Eye-Nourishing Fruit

If TCM had one mascot for eye health, it would be the goji berry (gou qi zi, 枸杞子). These small red-orange fruits were listed among the top-grade tonics in the ancient Shennong Bencao Jing (神农本草经), China's oldest surviving materia medica, and they have been a staple of scholar and student diets for centuries. In TCM theory, goji berries are sweet and neutral, nourishing both the Liver and the Kidneys, and they are traditionally said to "brighten the eyes" — a reputation earned by the belief that they enrich the Liver Blood that reaches the eyes.

Modern nutrition offers a friendly echo: goji berries are rich in zeaxanthin, lutein, and other carotenoids, the same pigments concentrated in the retina that some studies associate with eye health. The traditional way to use them is delightfully simple — a small handful, roughly one tablespoon (10–15 g), is the common daily amount, eaten as a snack, steeped in tea, or simmered into congee and soups. They are gentle enough for everyday eating for most people, though tradition suggests pausing them during an acute fever or heavy cold. For a full profile, see our goji berry deep dive.

More Foods That Nourish Liver Blood and Cool the Eyes

Goji berries rarely work alone. Traditional eye-supporting diets pair them with foods that clear heat on one side and build blood on the other. Here are the companions worth knowing.

Chrysanthemum — The Cooling Companion

Chrysanthemum flowers (ju hua, 菊花) are sweet and slightly bitter with a cooling nature, and they are the classic TCM answer to eyes that feel hot, red, or strained. Their traditional job is to clear Liver Heat and benefit the eyes (qing gan ming mu, 清肝明目). Yellow and white chrysanthemum are brewed daily across China, often alongside goji berries in the famous pairing known as goji-chrysanthemum tea. Because it is cooling, people with cold digestion or loose stools may prefer to sip it moderately. Learn how to brew it properly in our chrysanthemum tea guide, or try the classic goji-chrysanthemum tea.

Cassia Seeds — The "Bright Eyes" Seed

Cassia seeds (jue ming zi, 决明子) earn their name from their traditional reputation for brightening vision — jue ming literally means "to make the eyes clear." These small, glossy seeds are believed to clear Liver Heat and moisten the intestines. Roasted until fragrant and brewed as a tea, often blended with chrysanthemum, they are a common sight in Chinese offices. One honest caution: they have a mild laxative effect, so tradition advises small amounts — a teaspoon of roasted seeds per cup — and skipping them entirely if you are prone to loose stools.

Mulberry — The Dark Jewel of Liver Blood

Mulberries (sang shen, 桑椹) are the deep-purple fruits of the mulberry tree, and in TCM their dark juice is said to mirror the rich blood they are believed to nourish. Classified as sweet and cooling, mulberries are traditionally used to nourish Liver Blood and Kidney yin, making them a seasonal favorite for people whose eyes feel dry and tired alongside general signs of depletion. Fresh mulberries appear in late spring and early summer; dried ones are available year-round and are lovely steeped in tea, simmered into congee, or soaked in small handfuls like raisins. Our mulberry guide has more on choosing and using them.

Animal Liver — A Little, Occasionally

TCM food therapy also follows the logic of "like nourishes like": small amounts of pork or chicken liver (猪肝, zhu gan; 鸡肝, ji gan) are traditionally eaten to nourish the Liver and support the eyes. Liver is genuinely one of the most nutrient-dense foods in any tradition — rich in vitamin A, iron, and B vitamins — which is why a modest portion now and then can be a meaningful addition. The traditional approach, however, is occasional rather than daily: a small serving once a week or less, from a trusted source, cooked through gently. Because liver is so concentrated in vitamin A, pregnant women should check with their healthcare provider before eating it regularly. If you would like a gentle way in, our goji leaf and liver soup pairs liver with the leafy greens below.

Dark Leafy Greens — The Everyday Foundation

No eye-nourishing diet is complete without greens. Spinach (bo cai, 菠菜), goji leaves (gou qi ye, 枸杞叶), kale, and similar dark leaves are grouped in TCM with foods that nourish blood, support the Liver, and moisten dryness — and modern nutrition agrees they deliver iron, folate, lutein, and zeaxanthin. A helpful TCM nuance: greens are traditionally cooked lightly (stir-fried, blanched, or simmered into soup) rather than eaten raw in large salads, because gentle cooking makes their nourishment easier for the Spleen and Stomach to transform into blood. A handful of greens at dinner is the kind of quiet daily habit that adds up.

Foods to Limit When Your Eyes Feel Tired

TCM food therapy is as much about subtraction as addition. When eyes feel red, dry, or strained, these are traditionally believed to add fuel to the fire:

  • Excessively spicy food — chili-heavy meals are warming and drying in TCM, and may fan the Liver Heat that shows up in red, sore eyes.
  • Alcohol — traditionally regarded as heating and dehydrating, and well known for leaving eyes puffy and bloodshot the next morning.
  • Deep-fried and greasy foods — burden digestion and are believed to generate the damp-heat that clouds the face and discomforts the eyes.
  • Very strong coffee in excess — warming and drying in TCM terms; if your eyes already feel parched, it may be worth trading an afternoon cup for chrysanthemum tea.
  • Unbroken screen time and late nights — not a food, but the biggest drain of all: jiu shi shang xue reminds us that staring itself spends Liver Blood, and sleep is when that blood is restored.

A Simple Daily Tea for Tired Eyes

The most famous eye-supporting drink in Chinese food therapy is also one of the simplest: goji-chrysanthemum tea. It brings the two classic actions together — goji berries nourishing Liver Blood, chrysanthemum clearing Liver Heat — in a single fragrant cup. Here is the traditional way to make it:

Ingredients (1 cup): 1 tablespoon (about 10 g) dried goji berries; 6–8 dried chrysanthemum flowers (or 1 teaspoon loose chrysanthemum); 300–400 ml freshly boiled water; optional: a little rock sugar or honey, or 1–2 sliced red dates (jujube) for sweetness.

Steps:

  1. Rinse the goji berries and chrysanthemum briefly under cool water to remove dust.
  2. Place both in a mug or small teapot. If you like, add the optional sweetener and jujube now.
  3. Pour in the hot water — letting it rest for a minute after boiling makes a gentler, less bitter cup.
  4. Cover and steep for 5–10 minutes, until the water turns a soft golden yellow and the flowers have opened.
  5. Sip slowly. The brew can be topped up with hot water and re-steeped once or twice — and the goji berries themselves are edible, so chew them at the end rather than throwing them away.

A cup a day, mid-morning or early afternoon, is plenty for most people. Because chrysanthemum is cooling, those with a cold stomach or loose stools can use fewer flowers or add a slice of ginger. And while this tea is a lovely daily ritual, it is not a substitute for sleep, breaks from screens, or professional eye care.

Practical Tips for Screen-Fatigued Eyes

Foods work best when they travel with a few simple habits. TCM-friendly eye care pairs well with modern advice:

  • Follow the 20-20-20 rule — every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet away for 20 seconds. It rests the eye muscles and, in TCM terms, gives the Liver Blood a moment to settle.
  • Blink on purpose — screen staring cuts blinking dramatically, which dries the eyes; a few slow, full blinks help resurface them.
  • Soften your light — reduce glare and harsh overhead light, and keep screens slightly below eye level.
  • Keep goji within reach — a small dish of goji berries on your desk turns a mid-afternoon snack into a traditional eye tonic.
  • Warm the eyes at night — a warm (not hot) compress for a few minutes before bed is soothing, and pairs beautifully with an early night, since sleep is when Liver Blood is restored.
  • See a professional promptly — if you notice sudden vision changes, persistent pain, or anything that feels wrong, that is a moment for an eye care provider, not a tea.

Final Thoughts

For all its elegant theory, TCM eye care comes down to something refreshingly ordinary: eat foods that build the blood, calm the heat, and let the eyes rest. Goji berries at breakfast, greens at dinner, a chrysanthemum tea in the afternoon, and screens switched off at a reasonable hour — none of these are dramatic, and that is precisely the point. Traditional food therapy works the way good habits do: slowly, gently, and only with consistency. Give your Liver Blood a few weeks of steady nourishment and you may find your eyes feel calmer by evening.
