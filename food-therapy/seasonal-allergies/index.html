Foods for Seasonal Allergies — Defending the Wei Qi

🥗 Food Therapy · 7 min read

When the Seasons Turn Against You

For millions of people, the first warm weeks of spring and the dry weeks of late summer arrive with an unwelcome companion: sneezing, itchy eyes, a streaming nose, and a foggy head that never quite clears. Western medicine calls it allergic rhinitis. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) takes a different angle — it asks not only what is entering the body, but why the body is letting it in.

In TCM theory, the boundary between you and your environment is maintained by something called Wei Qi — literally "defensive qi." When that boundary is firm, pollen, dust, and shifting weather are said to pass by without much disturbance. When it is weak or out of balance, the same triggers are believed to slip past the surface and stir up symptoms.

This article looks at which foods and drinks the Chinese food therapy tradition has long used during allergy season, how they are prepared, and how to fold them into an ordinary day — using only the kitchen you already have. It is a companion piece to our guide on immune-building foods and Wei Qi.

The TCM View: Wei Qi, the Lungs, and Wind

Three ideas do most of the work in a TCM reading of seasonal allergies.

Wei Qi is the outer wall. Defensive qi circulates just below the skin and between the muscles. In classical theory, it "warms the muscles, fills the skin, and governs the opening and closing of the pores." A well-nourished Wei Qi means pores open and close cleanly with changes in temperature — an elegantly simple description of a body that adjusts smoothly to the outdoors.

The Lungs govern the skin and the nose. In TCM, the Lung system is said to "open into the nose" and to "spread qi to the skin and body hair." Stuffy noses, itchy throats, and reactive skin are therefore all read as signs of the Lung system responding to something at the surface. Supporting Lung qi is a central strategy in seasonal food therapy — see our deeper dive on the Five Elements for how Metal and the Lungs fit into the wider picture.

Wind is the classic carrier. In TCM, Wind is the pathogen most associated with symptoms that move and come and go: sneezing fits, itching that shifts location, tearing eyes, a headache that wanders. Seasonal allergy symptoms are a textbook Wind picture. This is exactly why so many traditional allergy teas include mildly dispersing aromatics such as perilla leaf and mint — they are believed to gently release the surface.

Put together: the traditional approach is not primarily "fight the pollen." It is strengthen the boundary and gently open the surface — nourish the root with tonics, clear the branch with light, aromatic, dispersing foods. This is the same root-and-branch logic described in our guide to food energetics.

What the Classics Suggest

Chinese food therapy has always treated the kitchen as the first pharmacy. The Shennong Bencao Jing (《神农本草经》), the oldest surviving Chinese materia medica, classifies herbs into three grades. Astragalus and licorice both appear among the "superior" class — substances described as suitable for long-term, food-like use. Perilla leaf, ginger, and chrysanthemum later became staples of the everyday "medicinal diet" (食疗, shí liáo) tradition, appearing in soups and teas rather than prescriptions.

The consistent message across these texts is that timing and gentleness matter more than intensity. A small bowl of the right congee, eaten consistently through a season, was considered more useful than an occasional dramatic remedy.

Core Foods Traditionally Used in Allergy Season

These foods share two qualities: they are gentle enough for daily use, and most of them are warming or mildly dispersing, which suits the Wind-and-Cold pattern that TCM associates with seasonal symptoms.

Astragalus (Huang Qi 黄芪)

Astragalus root is the best-known qi tonic in Chinese food therapy. Its name translates as "yellow leader," and it has a long traditional association with supporting defensive qi and Lung function — precisely the two systems TCM points to in seasonal allergies. It is mildly sweet and earthy, and it is almost always simmered rather than eaten.

How to use it: Add 3–5 slices of dried root to a pot of soup or broth and simmer for at least 45 minutes, then lift the slices out before serving — they stay woody. It pairs well with ginger and jujube dates. A gentle traditional routine is to simmer astragalus with a few jujubes in water for 40 minutes and sip the liquid in the morning.

Perilla Leaf (Zi Su Ye 紫苏叶)

Perilla leaf may be the single most allergy-relevant herb in the Chinese kitchen. It is classified as a surface-releasing, Wind-dispersing herb with a warming nature — traditionally used when the nose is stuffy and the body feels "stuck" at the surface. It is also a genuine culinary herb, used to wrap grilled food and to brighten soups.

How to use it: Perilla is volatile, so add it late. Stir 5–8 fresh leaves, or 1 teaspoon of dried leaf, into a soup or tea in the final 2–3 minutes and cover the pot. Long boiling is believed to drive off the very qualities you want.

Ginger (Sheng Jiang 生姜)

Fresh ginger is warming and pungent, traditionally used to warm the middle, expel Cold, and support the Lungs. During allergy season it serves two roles: it disperses at the surface and it keeps digestion strong, which matters because in TCM weak digestion eventually means weak Wei Qi.

How to use it: Simmer 3–4 thin slices in water for 10 minutes for a simple tea, or add them at the start of any soup. Our ginger tea guide covers steeping times and a few variations. If you tend to run hot — flushing, thirst, irritability — keep ginger modest.

Chrysanthemum Flower (Ju Hua 菊花)

Chrysanthemum is cooling, slightly sweet, and traditionally used for the head and eyes: red eyes, tearing, headache, and that hot, irritable feeling that often accompanies late-summer allergy flares. It is the balancing counterpart to the warming herbs above, which is why traditional blends often pair it with goji.

How to use it: Steep 5–8 dried flowers in hot — not boiling — water for 3–5 minutes. Very hot water is believed to make the infusion bitter. See our chrysanthemum tea guide and the goji-chrysanthemum blend.

Mint and Peppermint (Bo He 薄荷)

Mint is another light, aromatic, surface-releasing herb, cooling in nature and traditionally used for stuffy nose, sore throat, and itchy, irritated eyes. Because it is cooling, it works well in blends with the warming herbs for people whose symptoms include heat signs.

How to use it: Steep a small pinch of dried leaf for 3 minutes, or muddle a few fresh leaves into cooled tea. Like perilla, it should not be boiled. Our mint and licorice blend is a classic gentle combination.

Licorice Root (Gan Cao 甘草)

Licorice appears in a remarkable share of classical Chinese formulas, where it plays the role of the harmonizer — smoothing the effects of other herbs and supporting the Spleen and Lung systems. In food therapy it adds a pleasant sweetness to tea while, in TCM terms, helping the other ingredients work more comfortably together.

How to use it: Add one or two thin slices to a teapot and steep 5 minutes. Licorice is potent: keep to small amounts and short courses, and avoid it entirely if you have high blood pressure, kidney disease, or take diuretics or heart medication. Read our licorice deep dive before using it regularly.

Shiitake and Reishi Mushrooms

Both mushrooms are traditionally regarded as qi tonics, with shiitake prized in the kitchen and reishi used as a long-simmered decoction. Shiitake is a gentle, food-like tonic suitable for regular meals; reishi is more bitter and is best combined with sweeter herbs such as astragalus and jujube.

How to use them: Rehydrate 4–6 dried shiitake in warm water for 30 minutes and add both mushroom and soaking liquid to soups. For reishi, simmer 2–3 slices for 1–2 hours and use the liquid as a broth base.

Goji Berries (Gou Qi Zi 枸杞子)

Goji berries nourish the Liver and Kidneys and are traditionally said to benefit the eyes — a useful pairing for allergy season's itchy, tired eyes. They are sweet, mild, and easy to add to almost anything.

How to use it: Add a small handful to congee, soup, or tea during the final 5–10 minutes of cooking, or soak them overnight and eat them as is. Our goji berry guide covers selection and storage.

A Practical Day of Eating

Traditional food therapy is built on repetition, not intensity. A sample day during allergy season might look like this:

Morning: A bowl of warm congee — rice simmered long with a few slices of ginger and a handful of goji berries. Warm, cooked, easy on the Spleen, and completely ordinary.

Mid-morning: A cup of allergy-season tea (recipe below), sipped slowly rather than gulped.

Lunch: A clear soup or chicken soup with shiitake, ginger, and a handful of greens, plus cooked rice. TCM generally favors cooked vegetables over large raw salads during a flare-up.

Afternoon: A few dried jujube dates, or a small bowl of Chinese yam and goji soup if you can prepare it in advance.

Evening: A light bone broth or vegetable soup, kept early enough that it does not sit heavily overnight. Avoid iced drinks and heavy late meals.

Three Brews for Allergy Season

1. Astragalus & Jujube Defense Tea

Simmer 4 slices of dried astragalus root and 4 jujube dates (pitted, or slit with a knife) in 1 liter of water for 40 minutes on low. Strain and drink warm, about 1 cup per morning. Traditionally this is the "root" tea — nourishing rather than dispersing.

2. Perilla & Ginger Release Tea

Bring 2 cups of water to a boil with 3 slices of fresh ginger and simmer for 8 minutes. Turn off the heat, add 5–8 fresh perilla leaves (or 1 tsp dried), cover, and steep 3 minutes. This is the "branch" tea for days when the nose is stuffy and the head feels heavy.

3. Chrysanthemum & Mint Cooling Tea

Steep 5 dried chrysanthemum flowers and a small pinch of dried mint in hot, not boiling, water for 4 minutes. Add a few goji berries if you like. Best for symptoms with heat signs — red, itchy eyes and a hot, irritable feeling.

Foods Traditionally Eased Off During a Flare-Up

In TCM food therapy, what you pause matters as much as what you add. Traditionally, practitioners suggest temporarily easing off:

  • Iced and cold drinks — believed to weaken the Spleen and the surface.
  • Large amounts of raw salad and cold fruit, especially in a cold season, for the same reason.
  • Heavy dairy and very rich, greasy food — often described in TCM as producing Phlegm, which can make a stuffy nose feel worse.
  • Excess sugar and alcohol — traditionally understood to generate Dampness and add to congestion.
  • Very spicy food if you run hot — it may aggravate the heat component of symptoms.

Our guide to Dampness explains why Phlegm and congestion are treated as related ideas in TCM.

Timing, Dosage, and Consistency

A few practical conventions from the tradition:

  • Start early. Nourishing teas are traditionally begun two to four weeks before your usual season, rather than only once symptoms peak.
  • Warm and sipped. Tea is drunk warm, in small amounts, usually one or two cups a day.
  • Morning and early afternoon. Warming, dispersing herbs are typically taken earlier in the day so they do not disturb sleep.
  • Simple blends. Two or three ingredients per pot is the traditional norm — not a long list.
  • Consistency over intensity. A small daily habit through a whole season is the classic recommendation.

Gentle Habits That Work With the Food

Food therapy is one part of a pattern. TCM also emphasizes the everyday things that keep the surface strong:

  • Keep the neck and upper back covered in wind and cold — that region is traditionally considered the "gateway" of Wind.
  • Sleep consistently; in TCM, rest is when yin and blood are replenished.
  • Eat at regular times, with the largest meal in the middle of the day.
  • Move gently and regularly rather than in exhausting bursts.
  • Warm up after being outdoors in cool air, with a cup of warm tea rather than something iced.

Cautions

Food therapy is gentle, but it is not risk-free, and it is not a substitute for medical care.

  • Severe symptoms need a doctor. Wheezing, difficulty breathing, a tight chest, or swelling of the face, lips, or throat are medical emergencies. Call emergency services.
  • Herbs can interact with medication. Licorice in particular may affect blood pressure and potassium, and should be avoided by people on diuretics or heart medication. Astragalus and other tonics may interact with immunosuppressants.
  • Pregnancy and nursing. Avoid herbal teas beyond ordinary culinary amounts unless a qualified practitioner approves them.
  • Children and older adults. Use smaller amounts, and consult a practitioner first.
  • Stop if something disagrees. If a tea or food leaves you feeling worse, set it aside.
  • Keep up conventional care. Continue prescribed allergy medication, and do not stop or change it based on dietary changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which foods are traditionally used in TCM for seasonal allergies?

Astragalus (Huang Qi), perilla leaf (Zi Su Ye), fresh ginger, chrysanthemum flower, mint, licorice root, and shiitake mushrooms are the most common. They are usually taken as warm teas, broths, and congees rather than cold dishes, and they are often combined two or three at a time rather than all together.

Can I drink an allergy-support tea every day?

A mild blend of ginger, chrysanthemum, or perilla leaf is commonly sipped once or twice a day, usually in the morning and early afternoon. Keep licorice to small amounts for short periods, and check with a healthcare provider first if you take prescription medication or have high blood pressure.

How long before allergy season should I start?

Traditionally, nourishing teas such as astragalus and jujube are begun two to four weeks before your usual season arrives, so that the body's defensive qi is supported in advance. Dispersing teas with perilla, ginger, or mint are used as needed once symptoms start.

Is TCM food therapy a replacement for allergy medication?

No. TCM food therapy is a complementary, traditional approach that may support general wellness, and it is not a substitute for medical diagnosis, allergy testing, or prescribed treatment. Please continue any medication you have been given, and speak with a qualified healthcare provider about your symptoms.
