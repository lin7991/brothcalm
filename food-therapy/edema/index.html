Waking up with puffy eyes, tight rings on your fingers, or ankles that feel swollen by evening — these are gentle reminders that your body is holding onto more fluid than it needs. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), this experience is understood through the lens of water metabolism (水湿代谢): a body that is not smoothly transforming and moving fluids. Rather than reaching for a quick fix, TCM food therapy takes a slower, more nurturing path — choosing foods that are traditionally believed to support the spleen and kidneys, the two organs most responsible for fluid balance.

This guide walks through the TCM view of edema, the foods traditionally recommended for water retention, the foods to enjoy less often, and simple soups and teas you can make at home. As always in TCM, the goal is balance: enough moisture for the body's tissues, without excess fluid pooling where it shouldn't.

How TCM Understands Edema and Water Retention

In TCM theory, the body's fluids are regulated primarily by three organs: the spleen (脾) transforms and transports fluids, the lungs (肺) spread fluids downward and outward, and the kidneys (肾) provide the warming power that drives the whole system. When any of these fall out of harmony, fluids can accumulate and manifest as edema — puffy eyelids, swollen ankles, a heavy feeling in the limbs, or a sense of bloating that has nothing to do with the last meal.

Two patterns are especially common in daily life:

Spleen Qi Deficiency with Dampness

When the spleen is tired — from irregular meals, too much cold or raw food, or overthinking — its ability to transform fluids weakens. Fluids pool as "dampness" (湿), which TCM describes as heavy, sticky, and slow. People with this pattern may feel tired, carry a sense of heaviness in their limbs, and notice puffiness that is worse in the morning.

Kidney Yang Deficiency

The kidneys are considered the root of the body's warming fire. When kidney yang is insufficient, the body cannot "evaporate" fluids upward, and they sink downward — often showing as swelling in the feet and ankles that worsens toward the end of the day, sometimes accompanied by a cold feeling in the lower back and knees.

Because most cases involve the spleen, TCM food therapy for edema focuses on gentle, warming, drying foods that strengthen the spleen and help the body move fluids along.

Foods Traditionally Recommended for Edema

The foods below have been used for centuries in Chinese kitchens and apothecaries. They are not medicines, but everyday ingredients chosen for their traditional properties of supporting fluid metabolism.

Adzuki Beans (红豆 / 赤小豆)

Adzuki beans are among the most famous "dampness-drying" foods in TCM. They are traditionally considered sweet and neutral, and are believed to promote urination and help the body clear excess dampness. A classic preparation is adzuki bean soup, often simmered with a little red date for sweetness. In the clinic, adzuki beans are also paired with coix seed in the well-known "barley and adzuki bean porridge" used for damp conditions.

Coix Seed / Job's Tears (薏米)

Coix seed — also called pearl barley or Job's tears — is a staple of TCM dampness formulas. It is believed to strengthen the spleen, drain dampness, and support joint comfort. It has a mild, nutty flavor and cooks beautifully in porridge or soups. Some traditional sources suggest toasting it lightly before cooking to reduce its cooling nature.

Winter Melon (冬瓜)

Winter melon is a large, pale-green gourd with a mild, refreshing taste. TCM considers it cooling and believes it helps clear heat, promote urination, and reduce swelling. Winter melon soup with a little ginger and scallion is a classic summer dish in southern China, valued both for its flavor and its traditional role in supporting water balance.

Mung Beans (绿豆)

Mung beans are famous for clearing summer heat, but they also have a traditional role in supporting fluid metabolism. They are cooling, so they suit people with heat signs — such as dark urine or irritability — alongside fluid retention. Mung bean soup is a simple, everyday preparation.

Corn Silk (玉米须)

Corn silk — the fine, golden threads inside corn husks — is a gentle herb traditionally brewed as a tea to promote urination and support water balance. It is one of the mildest options, suitable for regular use. Steep a handful of fresh or dried corn silk in hot water for 10 minutes, and drink it warm.

Celery (芹菜)

Celery is considered cooling and is traditionally believed to help the body clear excess fluid and support healthy blood pressure. Its crisp texture and bright flavor make it easy to add to stir-fries, soups, and salads.

Poria (茯苓)

Poria, a mushroom that grows on pine roots, is one of the most commonly used herbs for dampness in Chinese medicine. It is traditionally believed to strengthen the spleen and promote urination without being harsh. Poria is often added to soups and congee — a few slices simmered with rice or chicken broth give the meal a subtle earthy note.

Watermelon (西瓜)

Watermelon is strongly cooling and diuretic in TCM tradition. It suits warm-weather swelling, but people with cold digestion should enjoy it in moderation. The white part of the rind, often discarded, is also traditionally used to make a gentle tea for water balance.

Foods to Limit with Water Retention

Equally important to what you add is what you set aside. TCM tradition, and modern experience, both point to a few categories that tend to make water retention worse:

  • Excess salt and salty foods — soy sauce, pickles, cured meats, and salty snacks encourage the body to hold onto water.
  • Cold and raw foods — iced drinks, raw salads, and cold fruit in large amounts are believed to weaken the spleen's ability to transform fluids.
  • Fried and greasy foods — heavy oils create dampness in the TCM sense, making fluid sluggish.
  • Excess sweets and refined carbohydrates — sugar is believed to generate dampness and burden the spleen.
  • Excessive dairy — for some people, dairy increases mucus and dampness; TCM often suggests reducing it during periods of water retention.
  • Alcohol — alcohol is damp and heating, and is traditionally avoided when fluids are not moving well.

Simple Recipes for Water Balance

These recipes are gentle, everyday, and require no special skills. Each one reflects a traditional TCM approach to supporting fluid metabolism.

Adzuki Bean and Coix Seed Porridge

  • ½ cup adzuki beans, rinsed
  • ½ cup coix seed, rinsed
  • 6 cups water
  • A small piece of tangerine peel (optional)

Soak the beans and coix seed overnight. Drain, add water and tangerine peel, and simmer gently for 60–90 minutes until soft and creamy. Enjoy warm in the morning. This classic porridge is traditionally used to support the spleen and help the body move dampness along.

Winter Melon Soup

  • 500 g winter melon, peeled and cubed
  • 2 slices fresh ginger
  • 1 scallion, chopped
  • 4 cups vegetable or chicken stock

Bring the stock to a boil with ginger. Add the winter melon and simmer for 20 minutes until translucent. Season lightly, garnish with scallion, and serve warm. In TCM tradition this soup is cooling and supports healthy urination.

Corn Silk Tea

  • 1 handful fresh corn silk (or 1 tablespoon dried)
  • 2 cups hot water

Place the corn silk in a teapot, pour hot water over it, and steep for 10 minutes. Strain and drink warm. This is one of the gentlest daily teas for water balance and can be enjoyed once or twice a day.

Lifestyle Habits That Support Fluid Balance

Food is only part of the picture. TCM emphasizes daily rhythms that keep the spleen and kidneys strong:

  • Move gently every day — walking, stretching, or qigong supports circulation and helps fluids move.
  • Elevate your legs — a few minutes with the legs raised helps gravity do some of the work for you.
  • Eat at regular times — the spleen is believed to thrive on routine; skipping meals weakens it.
  • Choose warm drinks — warm water, mild teas, and broths are easier for the body to process than cold drinks.
  • Rest well — the kidneys are traditionally associated with deep rest; consistent sleep supports the whole fluid system.
  • Manage stress — stress tightens the body and slows circulation; TCM links emotional tension with the liver, which also affects fluid movement.

When to See a Professional

Occasional puffiness from a salty meal or a long flight is common and usually harmless. But edema can also signal underlying conditions such as kidney, heart, or liver issues, or be a side effect of medication. Please see a healthcare provider if you experience any of the following:

  • Swelling that appears suddenly or spreads rapidly
  • Swelling in only one leg, especially with pain or redness
  • Shortness of breath or chest discomfort
  • Swelling accompanied by fever, or a reduction in urine output
  • Swelling that persists despite rest and dietary changes

TCM food therapy can be a beautiful companion to professional care, but it is not a substitute. Work with your healthcare team and, if you wish, a qualified TCM practitioner who can tailor recommendations to your specific pattern.
