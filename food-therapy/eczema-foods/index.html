Foods for Eczema — Cooling the Blood and Clearing Damp-Heat From Skin

🥗 Food Therapy · 8 min read

If you have woken up to another patch of itchy, red, weeping skin, you know how much eczema shapes a day — sleep, mood, what you wear, what you eat. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), stubborn flare-ups with heat and oozing are often read as damp-heat gathering in the blood: a combination of inflammation (heat) and fluid the body has not cleared efficiently (dampness). And in TCM theory, the kitchen is one of the first places to work. This guide covers the foods traditionally used to cool the blood and clear damp-heat, the foods usually set aside while the skin is active, and how to bring it all together in ordinary weeknight meals.

Why TCM Reads Eczema as Damp-Heat in the Blood

In TCM, the skin is understood as an outward reflection of what is happening inside. When the Liver, Spleen and Lungs are not moving fluids and qi smoothly, heat can accumulate and dampness can linger — and the two together tend to surface at the skin. That is why the traditional approach is rarely "put something on the rash" alone; it is about shifting the internal environment that is believed to keep the pattern alive.

What Damp-Heat Usually Looks Like

Damp-heat patterns are traditionally recognised by a fairly recognisable cluster of signs:

  • Red, warm, angry-looking patches that itch intensely
  • Oozing, weeping or crusting, especially in skin folds
  • Flare-ups in hot, humid weather, or after spicy food, alcohol or stress
  • A feeling of heaviness, sticky mouth, or a coated tongue
  • A tendency to feel better with cooling, drying, lightly cooked foods

By contrast, very dry, thin, flaky skin with fine scaling is more often read as a blood- and yin-deficiency pattern — a different picture that calls for nourishing, moistening foods instead. If that sounds closer to your skin, our guide to everyday skin health is the better starting point. For deeper background on the dampness half of the picture, see dampness explained.

Where the Framework Comes From

Classical Chinese materia medica catalogued cooling, dampness-draining substances long before modern dermatology existed. The Shennong Bencao Jing (Divine Farmer's Materia Medica, compiled around the 1st century) lists mung bean among substances described as clearing heat and resolving toxin. Li Shizhen's Bencao Gangmu (1596) later describes the same bean as cooling in nature and useful in hot, damp conditions. None of this is a claim about modern clinical outcomes — the language of heat, dampness and toxin describes patterns, not a diagnosis.

Cooling Foods Traditionally Used to Clear Damp-Heat

Mung Bean — The Classic Cooling Legume

Mung bean (lü dou) is the workhorse of cooling food therapy: sweet in flavour, cold in nature, and traditionally associated with the Heart and Stomach channels. It is used to clear heat and, in the classical phrasing, to "resolve toxin" — which is why it turns up so often in traditions for hot, inflamed, itchy skin.

How to prepare it: rinse 60 g of dried mung beans, add 6 cups of water, bring to a boil and simmer gently for 45–60 minutes until the beans have opened. Drink the broth warm and eat some of the beans. Do not add ice — cold drinks are traditionally thought to disrupt the Spleen and can make dampness worse. For a version that also supports digestion, simmer the beans with a little ginger, or follow our mung bean soup recipe.

How often: two to three times a week while the skin is hot and itchy. Reduce or pause if you feel chilled, unusually tired, or notice loose stools — in TCM that is a signal the cooling has gone far enough.

Honeysuckle and Chrysanthemum — Clearing Heat at the Surface

Honeysuckle (jin yin hua) is bitter, sweet and cold, traditionally linked to the Lung, Stomach and Large Intestine channels and used for "heat toxin" patterns where skin is red, hot and weeping. Chrysanthemum is gentler and traditionally used to cool the Liver and clear the eyes and head.

How to prepare them: steep 5–8 g of dried honeysuckle flowers, or 5–6 dried chrysanthemum blossoms, in 250 ml of just-boiled water for 5–8 minutes. Cover the cup so the aromatic compounds stay in. A gentle everyday option is chrysanthemum tea, with honeysuckle added during a flare.

How often: one to two cups a day, for a short stretch of about one to two weeks rather than indefinitely. Cool the cup to warm rather than drinking it iced.

Poria, Red Bean and Friends — Draining Dampness Gently

Where mung bean and honeysuckle handle the heat, a second group of foods is traditionally used to move the dampness out. Poria (fu ling) is bland, sweet and neutral, and is the quiet backbone of almost every dampness formula — it drains fluid gently without being harsh on digestion.

Pair it with red bean, Job's tears (coix seed), winter melon, celery, cucumber and corn silk in soups and congees. A simple weekly pot: 20 g poria, 40 g red bean, 40 g Job's tears and a strip of dried tangerine peel, simmered in 1.5 litres of water for an hour. Season lightly and eat the solids.

Moistening Foods So the Skin Is Not Left Dry

Constant cooling can leave skin tight and dry, so traditional plans usually alternate cooling dishes with foods that nourish blood and yin: pear, shiitake mushroom, black sesame, walnut, Chinese yam and lotus seed. A poached pear with a few goji berries, or a bowl of black sesame porridge, are classic ways to keep the skin supple while the heat is being cleared.

Foods Traditionally Set Aside During a Flare

If the goal is to stop adding fuel, several categories of food are traditionally reduced or paused while the skin is active:

  • Hot and spicy: chilli, hot sauce, Sichuan pepper, curry pastes, heavy garlic and ginger in large amounts
  • Alcohol in all forms — traditionally considered one of the most heating things you can drink
  • Fried, greasy and rich foods, including heavy cream sauces and pastries
  • Shellfish, shrimp and crab — traditionally grouped with "wind" or trigger foods
  • Very sweet foods and sugary drinks, which are believed to generate dampness
  • Heavy dairy — a common damp-forming food in TCM food therapy
  • Warming meats such as mutton, venison and large amounts of beef
  • Excess caffeine and energy drinks, which can stir heat and disturb sleep

Everyone is different, and TCM does not describe a single eczema diet. A simple two-week log — what you ate, how the skin looked next morning — often reveals your own pattern better than any generic list.

A Practical Week of Cooling, Damp-Clearing Meals

You do not need a special pantry. The pattern below uses ordinary ingredients and keeps the cooking simple, warm and light.

Morning

Mung bean or Job's tears congee with a little Chinese yam, or plain oat porridge topped with stewed pear. Keep it warm, not chilled.

Midday Tea

A cup of chrysanthemum tea, or honeysuckle and chrysanthemum together during an active flare. Sip it warm, and stop for the day after two cups.

Evening

Steamed or lightly stir-fried vegetables — winter melon, celery, cucumber, bok choy — with rice or millet and a small portion of fish, tofu or chicken. A bowl of red bean and poria soup makes a good lighter supper.

Alongside the Food

Food therapy is rarely only about the plate. Sleep before 11 pm where you can, keep showers lukewarm and short, wear soft cotton rather than wool, and watch your stress load — in TCM, Liver qi stagnation is believed to stir heat, which is one reason flare-ups so often arrive during difficult weeks.

Safety Notes — Read Before You Start

Please take these precautions seriously. Cooling food therapy is gentle, but it is not for everyone in every season.

  • Pregnancy and nursing: cooling herbs such as honeysuckle, chrysanthemum and mung bean are traditionally used in small culinary amounts, but should not be taken in concentrated or long-term doses during pregnancy or while nursing. Speak to your healthcare provider first.
  • Chronic conditions: if you have kidney, liver, thyroid, heart or autoimmune conditions, diabetes, or a bleeding disorder, check with your doctor before adding cooling herbs or making large dietary changes.
  • Medications: some herbs may interact with immunosuppressants, steroids, blood thinners, diuretics or antihistamines. Do not stop or change prescribed medication on your own.
  • Children and older adults: use smaller amounts and gentler foods, and ask a paediatrician or doctor before starting any herbal tea.
  • Listen to your body: if cooling foods leave you chilled, bloated or with loose stools, reduce them. A balanced approach is the traditional one.
  • See a doctor promptly if the skin worsens, spreads quickly, becomes infected, weeps pus, feels hot and painful, or is accompanied by fever. These require medical care, not dietary adjustments.

TCM food therapy is a complementary approach — it sits alongside dermatological care, not in place of it, and is best used gently and seasonally.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which foods are traditionally used for eczema in TCM?

In TCM theory, flare-ups with redness, warmth and weeping are read as damp-heat, so the foods traditionally used are cooling and damp-draining: mung bean, honeysuckle, chrysanthemum, winter melon, cucumber, celery, pear, red bean, Job's tears and poria. They are usually eaten as soups, congees and teas, alongside light, freshly cooked meals.

How often can I drink mung bean soup or honeysuckle tea during a flare?

Traditionally, mung bean soup is taken two to three times a week while the skin is hot and itchy, and honeysuckle or chrysanthemum tea one to two cups a day for a short stretch of about one to two weeks. Both are cooling, so they are usually reduced or paused if you start feeling chilled, tired, or notice loose stools.

What foods are usually avoided during an eczema flare?

TCM food therapy traditionally sets aside warming and damp-forming foods while the skin is active: chilli and other hot spices, alcohol, fried and greasy dishes, shellfish and shrimp, mutton and venison, very sweet desserts and sugary drinks, and heavy dairy. Many people also keep a simple two-week food and skin log, since individual triggers vary.

Is TCM food therapy safe for eczema if I am pregnant or on medication?

Cooling herbs such as honeysuckle and chrysanthemum are traditionally used in small culinary amounts, but should not be taken in concentrated or long-term doses during pregnancy or while nursing. If you have a chronic condition, take immunosuppressants, steroids or antihistamines, or are caring for a child or older adult, speak with a qualified healthcare provider before changing your diet, and see a doctor promptly if skin becomes infected or worsens.
