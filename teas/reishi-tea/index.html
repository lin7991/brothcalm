Reishi Tea (Ling Zhi Cha): Calming Shen with the Mushroom of Immortality

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 6 min read

Introduction: An Ancient Tonic for the Modern Mind

Few names in Chinese herbal tradition carry as much weight as Ling Zhi (灵芝) — reishi, the "mushroom of immortality." For more than two thousand years, this glossy, fan-shaped fungus has occupied a privileged place in Traditional Chinese Medicine, listed among the highest class of herbs reserved for nourishing life, calming the spirit, and supporting longevity. Its Latin name, Ganoderma lucidum, hints at the same reverence: ganos meaning brightness or sheen.

In our own age — one defined by relentless notifications, shallow breathing, and minds that refuse to switch off — reishi tea offers something quietly radical: a bitter, earthy cup that asks you to slow down. It is not a stimulant. It does not jolt you awake or sharpen your edge. Instead, it is traditionally used in TCM to calm the Shen (spirit), steady the Heart, and fortify the body's defensive Qi, making it a rare tea that serves both relaxation and resilience at once.

In this article, we explore how reishi is understood in TCM, how to brew a proper reishi tea at home, and what to keep in mind before making the mushroom of immortality part of your daily ritual.

Understanding Reishi in Traditional Chinese Medicine

TCM Properties and Meridians

In the framework of TCM, every food and herb carries a distinct energetic signature. Reishi is classified as neutral in nature — neither aggressively warming nor cooling — with a sweet taste that carries a subtle bitterness. This combination is significant: in Chinese herbal theory, the sweet flavour is believed to tonify and harmonise, gently nourishing Qi and blood, while the bitter flavour is thought to drain downward and calm excess, settling an agitated mind.

Reishi's primary affinities are the Heart, Lung, and Kidney meridians. The Heart houses the Shen and governs the blood; the Lung governs Qi and the protective layer known as Wei Qi (defensive Qi); the Kidney stores the deep essence (Jing) from which vitality is drawn. By gently harmonising all three, reishi is traditionally understood to address the root of many modern complaints: a restless mind, shallow energy, and a constitution that tires easily.

The Shen and the Mushroom of Immortality

Central to TCM's view of wellbeing is the concept of Shen (神) — the spirit, consciousness, or the mind's essential vitality. The Shen is said to reside in the Heart during the day and should return there at night for deep, restoring sleep. When Shen becomes agitated by stress, overwork, grief, or an imbalanced diet, it "wanders," showing up as insomnia, vivid dreaming, anxiety, palpitations, and forgetfulness.

Reishi is traditionally regarded as one of the most refined herbs for anchoring the Shen and nurturing the Heart. This is why it appears so often in formulas for insomnia and anxiety, and why a cup of reishi tea in the evening has become a gentle modern ritual for winding down. At the same time, because reishi tonifies the Lung and strengthens defensive Qi, it has a dual reputation: calming by night, fortifying by day.

Traditional Uses and the Classic Texts

A Superior Herb in the Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing

The Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing (Divine Farmer's Materia Medica), the foundational pharmacopoeia of Chinese medicine compiled around the Han Dynasty, organises herbs into three classes. The superior class — herbs considered gentle, non-toxic, and suitable for long-term use to nourish life — includes reishi, which the text famously describes as calming the spirit, benefiting the Heart Qi, sharpening the memory, and supporting longevity. The very term ling zhi carries the sense of "divine mushroom" or "spirit mushroom," reflecting the reverence ancient physicians held for it.

Later texts, including Ben Cao Gang Mu (Compendium of Materia Medica) compiled by Li Shizhen in the Ming Dynasty, continued this tradition, describing reishi as a tonic for weakness, a support for the Lung in cases of chronic cough and shortness of breath, and a calming agent for the Heart and Shen. In classical practice, reishi was often simmered into decoctions rather than steeped like a leaf tea — a method that survives today in the long-simmered reishi teas of Chinese households.

Traditional Uses Across the Lifespan

In TCM, reishi has been traditionally used for several overlapping purposes:

  • Calming the Shen — supporting restful sleep, easing anxiety and heart palpitations associated with stress.
  • Tonifying Heart Qi — gently strengthening the organ that houses the spirit, benefiting memory and mental clarity.
  • Nourishing the Lung — traditionally used for weak coughs, shortness of breath, and those who feel easily winded.
  • Strengthening defensive Qi — supporting the body's natural resilience, which is why reishi appears in many modern immune-support conversations.
  • Supporting longevity — as a superior-class herb, reishi was taken over long periods to sustain vitality and slow the effects of wear.

How to Brew Reishi Tea: A Practical Guide

Unlike delicate leaf teas, reishi is a dense, woody mushroom that releases its beneficial compounds slowly. A quick steep will produce little more than faintly tinted water — the traditional method is a patient simmer. Here is how to prepare a single serving.

Ingredients

  • 3–5 grams of dried, sliced reishi mushroom (Ganoderma lucidum) — look for thick, well-dried slices from a reputable TCM supplier
  • 500 ml (about 2 cups) of water
  • Optional: 3–4 jujube dates and a small handful of goji berries for sweetness and Heart support
  • Optional: 1 slice of dried licorice root or a small piece of rock sugar to soften the bitterness

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Rinse the reishi slices briefly under cool water to remove dust. If the slices are large, break them into smaller pieces to increase the surface area.
  2. Place the reishi in a small saucepan or ceramic pot. Add the water and, if using, the jujube dates and goji berries.
  3. Bring to a gentle boil, then reduce the heat to low and simmer uncovered for 20–40 minutes. The liquid will darken to a deep amber and take on a characteristic earthy, bitter aroma.
  4. Strain the tea into a cup, discarding the spent mushroom pieces. The slices can be simmered a second time with fresh water for a lighter second brew.
  5. Sweeten to taste with honey or rock sugar while the tea is still warm. Honey should be added after the tea cools slightly, as high heat may affect its character.
  6. Drink slowly, ideally in the evening, sitting quietly and letting the warmth settle your mind.

Flavour Variations

  • Reishi & Jujube Tea — add 3–4 jujube dates for natural sweetness and extra Heart-nourishing support. A classic combination in Chinese households.
  • Reishi & Goji Tea — add goji berries for a slightly sweeter brew that supports the Liver and Kidneys alongside the calming action.
  • Reishi & Licorice Tea — a small slice of licorice root dramatically softens the bitterness and harmonises the flavours, in line with traditional blending practice.
  • Double-Brewed Reishi — simmer the same slices twice, once for 40 minutes and again for 20 minutes, combining both batches for a stronger, more concentrated tonic.

Who May Benefit and When to Drink

Reishi tea is traditionally considered a gentle, long-term tonic rather than an acute remedy, and it may suit people who experience any of the following:

  • Difficulty winding down at night, racing thoughts, or sleep that feels shallow and unrefreshing
  • Feeling drained after a busy season, with low resilience to stress and minor illness
  • Frequent tiredness accompanied by palpitations or poor concentration
  • A tendency toward weak coughs or feeling easily winded, where Lung Qi support may be helpful

Because reishi contains no caffeine, it is well suited to the evening hours — many traditional drinkers take it as a nightly ritual one to two hours before bed. In TCM practice, consistency matters more than quantity: a regular, moderate cup is believed to be far more nourishing than an occasional strong dose.

Precautions and Who Should Be Cautious

Reishi is classified as a superior herb, but it is also a potent medicinal mushroom, and a few groups should approach it with care:

  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding — consult a qualified healthcare provider before drinking reishi tea regularly.
  • Blood-thinning medication — reishi may interact with anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs. Discuss it with your doctor first.
  • Blood-pressure medication — reishi may influence blood pressure; monitor and consult your practitioner if you take medication.
  • Autoimmune conditions — because reishi is traditionally used to support immune activity, those on immunosuppressants or with autoimmune disorders should seek professional advice.
  • Bleeding disorders or upcoming surgery — stop reishi use well in advance of any surgical procedure and inform your surgeon.

As with any herb, quality matters. Choose cultivated, food-grade reishi slices from a reputable supplier rather than wild-harvested mushrooms of uncertain origin. Start with one cup and notice how your body responds before increasing your intake.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drink reishi tea every day?

Reishi is traditionally regarded as a gentle, superior-class herb suited to long-term use, and many TCM practitioners consider a daily cup reasonable for most adults. Start with one cup, observe how you feel, and consult a practitioner if you take medication or have an underlying condition.

How long should I simmer reishi tea?

Dried reishi needs a long, gentle simmer to release its compounds — traditionally 20 to 40 minutes, and sometimes longer in a double-boil. If the tea tastes weak and nearly clear, it has not simmered long enough.

Can I add honey or other herbs to reishi tea?

Yes. Honey or rock sugar balances the bitterness, and jujube dates, goji berries, licorice root, and astragalus are all traditional companions that soften the flavour and complement reishi's calming and tonifying action.

Is reishi tea safe during pregnancy or while breastfeeding?

Because reishi is a potent medicinal mushroom, pregnant or nursing women should consult their healthcare provider before drinking it regularly. The same caution applies to anyone taking blood thinners or immunosuppressants.

Does reishi tea contain caffeine?

No. Reishi is a mushroom rather than a tea leaf, so it is naturally caffeine-free — one reason it is a popular evening drink for calming the Shen and preparing the body for rest.
