Lily Tea: A Calming Floral Brew for Dry Coughs & Restless Sleep

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 5 min read
Quick answer

Lily tea is a delicate, lightly sweet herbal brew made from dried lily bulbs (bǎi hé, 百合), a flower bulb that has been part of Chinese food therapy for more than two thousand years. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), lily is prized for two gentle jobs: moistening the lungs when they feel dry and tired, and settling a busy mind so sleep comes more easily. For anyone living with a nagging dry cough, restless nights, or the quiet burnout that often follows a long illness, a warm cup of lily tea is one of the simplest comforts in the Chinese pantry.

What Is Lily Tea?

Lily tea is not made from the fragrant white flowers you buy for a vase, but from the bulbs of certain lily species, most famously the Lanzhou lily (Lilium davidii var. unicolor) from northwest China. The peeled, ivory-white petals are dried until crisp and then simmered into a mild broth that tastes faintly sweet and starchy, with a soothing, almost silky texture. In TCM, dried lily bulb is classified as a food-grade herb, which means it can be used generously in daily cooking and brewing without the heavy-handedness of stronger medicinals.

TCM Properties: Cool, Sweet, and Calming

In the language of TCM food energetics, lily bulb has a sweet flavor, a slightly cold nature, and enters the Lung and Heart meridians. This combination explains its traditional uses:

  • Sweet and moistening — sweetness in TCM is nourishing and harmonizing; combined with a moistening quality, it is traditionally used to soften dryness in the lungs.
  • Slightly cool — a mild cooling energy is believed to help clear the low-grade heat that can build up in the body after illness or during chronic stress, which TCM calls deficiency heat (xū rè).
  • Heart-entering — because it reaches the Heart meridian, TCM tradition pairs lily with calming herbs to support a settled spirit and deeper sleep.

Traditional Uses Through the Centuries

Lily bulb has a long, documented history in Chinese medicine. The Shennong Bencao Jing (Divine Farmer’s Classic of Materia Medica), compiled around the first century, lists lily among its top-grade herbs, noting that it was traditionally used to soothe the chest and calm the spirit. Centuries later, the Han-dynasty physician Zhang Zhongjing included lily in his classic text Jin Gui Yao Lue (Essentials from the Golden Cabinet), where he described a pattern known as “lily disease” (bǎi hé bìng) — a state of restless fatigue, irritability, and disturbed sleep often seen after a prolonged illness. His famous formula Bai He Di Huang Tang paired lily bulb with rehmannia to nourish and settle. Today, food therapists still reach for lily in the same spirit: to support recovery, calm the mind, and ease a dry, irritable cough.

Potential Benefits of Lily Tea

In TCM tradition, and in the language of modern food therapy, drinking lily tea regularly is believed to offer several gentle supports. (As always, these are traditional uses, not medical claims.)

May Help Moisturize Dry Coughs

Lily bulb is one of the most commonly recommended foods in TCM for dry coughs — coughs that are hacking, ticklish, and produce little or no phlegm, especially in autumn or during winter heating season. Its moistening quality is traditionally paired with lung-nourishing foods such as snow fungus, pear, and honey. Lily tea is not intended to suppress a cough, but rather to support the lung’s own moisture so the throat feels less scratchy and irritated.

May Support Calm and Restful Sleep

Because lily enters the Heart meridian, TCM tradition uses it to calm a restless, overworked mind. A cup of warm lily tea in the evening is a common home remedy for people who lie awake with racing thoughts, wake frequently, or feel irritable and emotionally frayed. It is often combined with other calming botanicals such as jujube dates, longan, or suan zao ren (sour jujube seed) for a stronger sleep-supporting effect.

May Ease Restlessness After Illness

TCM describes a pattern of lingering dryness and irritability after a fever or long illness — the body feels depleted, sleep is shallow, and the nerves feel stretched. Lily bulb’s nourishing, cooling nature is traditionally used to gently rebuild and settle during this recovery window.

How to Make Classic Lily Tea

The basic brew is simple and forgiving. Use dried lily petals, not fresh flowers.

Ingredients

  • 10–15 g dried lily bulbs (about 2 tablespoons)
  • 500 ml water
  • Honey, to taste (optional, add after brewing)

Steps

  1. Rinse the dried lily petals quickly under cool water to remove dust.
  2. Place them in a small pot with the water and bring to a gentle boil.
  3. Reduce to a low simmer, cover, and cook for 20–30 minutes, until the petals are soft and the liquid is lightly milky.
  4. Strain into a cup. If using honey, stir it in only after the tea has cooled slightly — heat destroys honey’s enzymes.
  5. Drink warm, ideally in the evening or after a meal.

Tip: Lily bulb releases a delicate sweetness on its own, so most people do not need sweetener. If you prefer, a single red date (jujube) simmered with the lily adds natural sweetness and warmth.

Five Ways to Vary Your Cup

  • Lily + goji berries — adds a gentle liver-and-kidney nourishing note; a classic pairing for tired eyes and restless evenings.
  • Lily + jujube dates — warmer and sweeter; a good everyday tonic that balances lily’s cooling nature.
  • Lily + lotus seed — both calm the spirit; together they are traditionally used for scattered, anxious sleep.
  • Lily + snow fungus — a famous duo for dry skin and dry cough; stew them together for a thicker, soup-like brew.
  • Lily + honey — the simplest version; honey is itself traditionally used in TCM to moisten the lungs and soothe the throat.

Choosing and Storing Dried Lily Bulbs

Good dried lily petals are pale cream or light yellow, thin, and slightly curled, with a mild, clean scent. Avoid petals that are brown, moldy, or have a sour or musty smell, and be wary of very white petals — they may have been bleached. Store dried lily in an airtight container in a cool, dry, dark place; it keeps for about a year. If you can find fresh lily bulbs (in season from late summer to autumn), use about 30 g per 500 ml and simmer until completely tender.

Who Should Drink Lily Tea Cautiously

Because lily is slightly cold and moistening, TCM tradition suggests the following people use it in moderation:

  • Cold-damp or weak-spleen constitutions — people who often feel chilled, have loose stools, or a heavy, sluggish feeling may find large amounts of lily too cooling. Pair it with warm ingredients like jujube or ginger, or drink smaller amounts.
  • Wet coughs with lots of phlegm — lily is traditionally reserved for dry coughs; if your cough is rattling with mucus, other herbs such as tangerine peel may be a better fit.
  • Pregnancy — lily is generally considered food-safe, but because pregnancy changes how the body handles cooling foods, it is wise to enjoy it in small amounts and check with your healthcare provider.
  • Anyone managing a chronic condition — herbal teas can interact with medications and health conditions; talk to a qualified professional before making them a daily habit.

FAQ

Can I drink lily tea every day?

For most people, yes — 1 cup (about 500 ml) daily is a reasonable traditional amount. If you notice loose stools or a cold, sluggish feeling, reduce frequency or add a warming partner like jujube or ginger.

When is the best time to drink lily tea?

Evening is the classic time, because of the tea’s calming, sleep-supporting reputation. It also works well in the afternoon for a dry throat or a frazzled mood.

Is lily tea safe during pregnancy?

Lily is generally regarded as food-safe, and small amounts in cooking or tea are common. Because it is slightly cooling, pregnant women should enjoy it in moderation and consult their healthcare provider, especially in the first trimester.

Can I drink lily tea for a wet cough with phlegm?

Lily is traditionally reserved for dry, hacking coughs. For a wet cough with lots of phlegm, TCM practitioners more often reach for warming, drying ingredients like tangerine peel or ginger instead.
