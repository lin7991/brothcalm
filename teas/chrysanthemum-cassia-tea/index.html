Chrysanthemum & Cassia Seed Tea: Bright Eyes, Clear Vision

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 6 min read

When your eyes feel tired, dry, or strained after a long day of screens, Chinese tea culture has a classic answer: a golden cup of chrysanthemum and cassia seed tea. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), this humble pair has been brewed for centuries as a gentle way to support visual comfort and a clear, calm head. It is one of the most popular "eye tonics" in Chinese households — simple to make, pleasant to sip, and rooted in a tradition that treats the eyes as a mirror of inner balance.

This guide walks you through the TCM story of both ingredients, what makes them such a natural match, how to brew the tea properly, and who should drink it with extra care.

Why These Two Belong Together

In TCM, eye health is closely tied to the liver. The classic saying "the liver opens to the eyes" (肝开窍于目) reflects the belief that when liver blood and liver qi flow smoothly, the eyes are nourished and vision feels clear. When liver heat rises — from stress, late nights, or too much screen time — the eyes may feel dry, itchy, red, or tired.

Both chrysanthemum and cassia seed are traditionally classified as cooling herbs that enter the liver channel, which is why they are so frequently paired. Chrysanthemum gently clears and cools from above, while cassia seed works from below to guide heat downward. Together they are believed to support the whole pathway of "clear eyes" in a way neither achieves alone.

Chrysanthemum (菊花, jú huā): The Cooling Flower

Chrysanthemum flowers are one of the most recognizable ingredients in Chinese tea culture. In TCM, they are described as sweet and bitter in taste, slightly cold in nature, and associated with the lung and liver channels. The Shennong Ben Cao Jing (《神农本草经》), one of the oldest Chinese pharmacopoeias, lists chrysanthemum among its top-grade herbs and praises it for supporting longevity and clear vision.

Traditionally, chrysanthemum has been used to:

  • Scatter wind-heat — a TCM pattern often linked to red, watery eyes and headaches
  • Support visual clarity, especially when the eyes feel tired or strained
  • Calm the liver and soothe irritability associated with rising heat

White chrysanthemum is often preferred for eye comfort, while yellow chrysanthemum is considered stronger for clearing heat. For a daily eye tonic, either works beautifully. You can read more about its broader uses in our chrysanthemum ingredient guide and the classic single-flower chrysanthemum tea.

Cassia Seed (决明子, jué míng zǐ): The Brightener

Cassia seed — also called jue ming zi, literally "the seed that brightens" — comes from the plant Senna obtusifolia (formerly Cassia obtusifolia). The Chinese name itself tells the story: in TCM tradition, this small brown seed is believed to support clear, bright eyes.

In TCM, cassia seed is described as sweet, bitter, and slightly cold, entering the liver and large intestine channels. It is traditionally used for two main purposes:

  • Clearing liver heat to support eye comfort and vision
  • Moistening the intestines, which gives it a gentle, mild laxative quality

Because of that second property, cassia seed is often roasted before brewing — toasting softens its cold nature and its impact on digestion while deepening a pleasant, nutty flavor. In many Chinese households, the tea is made with pre-roasted cassia seed for exactly this reason.

How to Brew Chrysanthemum & Cassia Seed Tea

This is a forgiving tea — exact amounts are a matter of taste. Here is the classic proportions used in Chinese homes.

Ingredients

  • 5–8 dried chrysanthemum flowers (about 1 teaspoon)
  • 1–2 teaspoons roasted cassia seed (决明子)
  • 1–2 cups hot water (about 85–95°C / 185–205°F, not boiling hard)
  • Optional: 2–3 goji berries, a small piece of rock sugar, or honey to taste

Steps

  1. Rinse briefly. Give the chrysanthemum flowers and cassia seed a quick rinse in cold water if they look dusty (a fine strainer helps).
  2. Combine in a cup or teapot. Add both ingredients. If using a teapot, a larger pot with 4–6 cups of water and doubled ingredients works well for sharing.
  3. Add hot water. Pour water at around 90°C over the herbs. Very hot, rolling-boil water can make chrysanthemum taste slightly bitter.
  4. Steep 5–8 minutes. Chrysanthemum opens quickly, but cassia seed needs a few extra minutes to release its flavor. Cover the cup to keep the heat in.
  5. Strain and sip. Drink warm. You can top up the same leaves once or twice more with fresh hot water.

Three Easy Variations

  • Goji + chrysanthemum + cassia: Add 5–6 goji berries to the cup. Goji is considered sweet and nourishing in TCM, balancing the cooling herbs and adding a light fruity sweetness — a very popular screen-worker's blend.
  • With a slice of licorice or a date: If you find the tea too cooling, a small slice of dried licorice root or one pitted red date softens the brew and supports the stomach in TCM terms.
  • Iced version: Brew it strong, let it cool, and serve over ice with a little honey for a warm-weather refresher. Some TCM practitioners would suggest keeping iced versions occasional, as cold drinks are thought to slow digestion.

When to Reach for This Tea

People in China traditionally reach for chrysanthemum and cassia seed tea in a handful of situations:

  • After long screen sessions — as a calming ritual when eyes feel strained, dry, or heavy
  • During hot, dry weather — when the body feels overheated and the eyes feel gritty
  • When stress and frustration build — TCM links irritability and red eyes to liver heat, and this tea is the classic "cooling-off" drink
  • As part of a short seasonal reset — many people drink it for a week or two at a time rather than year-round

It pairs naturally with a diet that supports eye comfort — think leafy greens, orange vegetables, and foods traditionally used in TCM for vision, which we cover in our food therapy guides.

Who Should Drink It With Care

Because both ingredients are cooling and cassia seed has a gentle laxative tendency, this tea is not for everyone. In TCM terms, it is best avoided or reduced by:

  • People with weak digestion or loose stools — cassia seed may increase bowel movements
  • People with a "cold" constitution — those who often feel chilly, have cold hands and feet, or get bloating after cold foods may find this tea too cooling
  • Pregnant and nursing women — cooling herbs and laxative-type seeds are traditionally approached with caution; check with your healthcare provider first
  • Anyone on blood-thinning or blood-pressure medication — chrysanthemum has traditional associations with blood pressure and circulation, so a professional check is wise

A few practical cautions: don't brew it so strong that it tastes bitter, avoid drinking large amounts every day for months on end, and remember that if eye pain, vision changes, or persistent discomfort occur, this tea is not a substitute for seeing an eye care professional.

FAQ

Can I drink chrysanthemum and cassia seed tea every day?

For most people a cup a day for a short period is fine, but cassia seed is cooling and mildly laxative in TCM, so it is best treated as a course of a few weeks rather than an everyday habit. If you notice loose stools, reduce the amount of cassia seed or pause the tea.

Who should avoid chrysanthemum and cassia seed tea?

People with weak digestion, loose stools, or a cold spleen and stomach in TCM terms should be cautious because both ingredients are cooling. Pregnant women, anyone with low blood pressure, and people taking blood-thinning medication should check with a healthcare provider first.

Does this tea actually help tired eyes?

In TCM theory, chrysanthemum and cassia seed both enter the liver channel, and the liver is said to open to the eyes, which is why this pair has traditionally been used to support visual comfort. Modern research on the individual herbs is still early, so treat it as a soothing ritual rather than a treatment.

Can I sweeten chrysanthemum and cassia seed tea?

Yes. Rock sugar is the classic choice in Chinese households, or a little honey once the tea has cooled to warm rather than hot. A few goji berries added to the cup add a mild sweetness too.
