Cassia Seed Tea (Jue Ming Zi Cha) — The Eye-Clearing Nightcap

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 6 min read
Quick answer

The seeds crackle as they toast, release a toasty, coffee-like aroma, and by bedtime the drink is ready — warm, earthy, and surprisingly calming to tired eyes.

The seeds crackle as they toast, release a toasty, coffee-like aroma, and by bedtime the drink is ready — warm, earthy, and surprisingly calming to tired eyes.

Cassia seed — the dried seed of Senna tora (formerly Cassia tora), also called jue ming zi — has been part of Chinese food therapy for centuries. Its very name carries its promise: jue ming means "to brighten the eyes." In this guide, we will look at what makes cassia seed tea a beloved TCM bedtime brew, how to make it properly, which traditional blends work best, and who may wish to skip it.

What Is Cassia Seed Tea?

Cassia seed tea is a caffeine-free herbal infusion made from the small, glossy, brown seeds of Senna tora, a plant in the legume family that grows throughout Asia. The seeds are harvested, dried, and often lightly roasted before brewing. Unlike green or black tea, cassia seed tea contains no tea leaves at all — it belongs to the Chinese category of dai cha (代茶饮), "replacement teas" made from herbs and grains that are steeped or simmered like tea for everyday wellness.

Because the seeds have a naturally nutty, roasted flavor with a faint bitterness, the tea is often compared to a very light coffee or roasted barley tea. It is commonly served in the evening — hence its nickname as an "eye-clearing nightcap" — though it can be enjoyed at any time of day.

TCM Properties of Cassia Seed

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, every food and herb is understood through its nature (性), flavor (味), and meridian affinity (归经). Cassia seed is described as:

  • Nature: slightly cold (微寒)
  • Flavors: sweet (甘), bitter (苦), and slightly salty (咸)
  • Meridians: Liver (肝) and Large Intestine (大肠)

In TCM theory, the Liver meridian "opens to the eyes" — meaning liver health and eye health are closely linked. When liver heat builds up, it is believed to show up as red, dry, itchy, or tired eyes, irritability, and even headaches. Cassia seed's cooling, bitter nature is traditionally used to clear that liver heat, which is why it has been paired with eye care in Chinese medicine for over a thousand years.

Traditional Uses Through the Centuries

Cassia seed appears in some of the oldest Chinese pharmacopoeias. The Shennong Bencao Jing (《神农本草经》), a foundational herbal classic compiled around the Han dynasty, lists cassia seed among the "superior" herbs and records that it was traditionally used for the eyes — including clouded vision and eye pain with tearing. Later texts, including the Bencao Gangmu (《本草纲目》) by Li Shizhen in the 16th century, continue this theme, describing cassia seed as a remedy traditionally used to "benefit the essence of the eyes."

Beyond the eyes, cassia seed was historically grouped with herbs that "moisten the intestines" (润肠) and "guide out" stagnant heat. In plain terms, it was used for two classic TCM concerns:

  • Liver heat rising — traditionally linked with red or tired eyes, dry eyes, irritability, and a bitter taste in the mouth.
  • Dry-type bowel sluggishness — the seeds' moistening, slightly oily nature was believed to support smooth bowel movements when stools were dry and difficult to pass.

What Modern Observations Suggest

While we keep our language careful here at BrothCalm, it is worth noting that researchers have shown interest in cassia seed. Laboratory studies have identified compounds in the seeds — including anthraquinone glycosides such as emodin and chrysophanol — that may explain its traditional reputation as a gentle laxative. Some preliminary studies suggest cassia seed may also support healthy cholesterol levels already within the normal range, though these findings are early and more research is needed.

These observations do not "prove" the ancient claims, but they do suggest that the TCM tradition — which treated cassia seed as a cooling seed that moves things along — was pointing at real physiological effects. As always, use this information as education, not self-prescription.

How to Make Cassia Seed Tea

The classic preparation has two steps: a quick dry-roast, then a gentle simmer. Roasting is traditional for a reason — it deepens the nutty aroma, reduces the raw, slightly grassy taste, and is said to soften the seed's cooling nature on the stomach.

Ingredients

  • 1–2 teaspoons dried cassia seeds (about 5–10 g)
  • 2 cups (500 ml) water
  • Optional: 4–6 dried chrysanthemum flowers, a small handful of goji berries, or a slice of licorice root for sweetness

Steps

  1. Dry-roast: Place the cassia seeds in a dry pan over low-to-medium heat. Shake the pan frequently for 2–3 minutes until the seeds darken slightly and smell fragrant and nutty. Do not let them smoke or burn.
  2. Simmer: Transfer the roasted seeds to a small pot with 2 cups of water. Bring to a gentle boil, then lower the heat and simmer, covered, for 10–15 minutes.
  3. Steep extras (optional): If using chrysanthemum or goji berries, add them in the last 3–5 minutes so their delicate flavors are not overcooked.
  4. Strain and serve: Pour the tea through a fine strainer into a cup. Drink warm. The seeds can be simmered once more with fresh water for a weaker second brew.

Three Traditional Variations

  • Bright Eyes Blend: Combine cassia seed with dried chrysanthemum flowers and goji berries — a famous trio for eye comfort in TCM. You can also try our goji chrysanthemum tea as a caffeine-free daytime companion.
  • Digestive Support Blend: Add a few slices of dried hawthorn berry to cassia seed for a slightly tart tea traditionally enjoyed after rich, heavy meals.
  • Soothing Bedtime Blend: Pair cassia seed with a small piece of dried tangerine peel for a warm, citrusy note that many find comforting before sleep.

When to Drink and How Much

Traditionally, cassia seed tea is enjoyed 1–2 cups per day, often in the late afternoon or evening. Because it is caffeine-free and cooling, it makes a natural wind-down drink — the "eye-clearing nightcap" of the title. A common traditional pattern is to drink it in short courses of a few weeks, then take a break, rather than sipping it daily for months.

One practical tip from Chinese households: because of its gentle moistening effect on the bowels, many people prefer to drink it earlier in the evening rather than immediately before bed, so there is no middle-of-the-night trip to the bathroom.

Who Should Be Cautious

Cassia seed tea is gentle, but it is not for everyone. TCM food therapy is about balance, and a cooling, moving tea needs to match the person drinking it:

  • Cold spleen and stomach patterns: If you frequently have loose stools, diarrhea, a cold feeling in the abdomen, or poor appetite with chills, cassia seed's cooling nature may not suit you.
  • Pregnancy and nursing: Because cassia seed has a traditional reputation for moving the bowels and has been studied for its anthraquinone content, pregnant and nursing women are traditionally advised to avoid it unless a qualified practitioner says otherwise.
  • Medication interactions: Anyone taking blood-thinning medication, laxatives, or medication for blood pressure or blood sugar should consult a healthcare provider before adding cassia seed tea to a daily routine.
  • Long-term daily use: Drinking very large amounts every day for long periods is not traditional. If you notice loose stools, reduce the amount or stop.

Buying and Storing Cassia Seeds

Quality cassia seeds are small, oval, flattened, and glossy brown with a slight greenish tint. They should smell faintly grassy when raw and turn nutty when roasted. Store them in an airtight jar away from light and moisture; like most seeds, they keep best in a cool, dark cupboard and can last up to a year when properly sealed.

Cassia seed tea is also sold pre-bagged in many Asian grocery stores and online — often labeled as "cassia seed tea," "jue ming zi tea," or in ready-made sachets with chrysanthemum. If buying whole seeds, choose ones without dust or broken pieces.

Final Thoughts

Cassia seed tea is a quiet, comforting example of Chinese food therapy at work: a humble seed, dry-roasted and simmered, turned into an evening ritual for tired eyes and a settled digestion. It is not a miracle drink — nothing in TCM food therapy is — but for people who spend hours in front of screens and enjoy warm, caffeine-free herbal brews, it is a lovely tradition worth trying.

Brew it gently, listen to your body, and as with any herb, check with a healthcare professional if you have health conditions or take medication.
