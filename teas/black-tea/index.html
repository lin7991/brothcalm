Black Tea (Hong Cha) — The Warming Fermented Brew

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 6 min read

What Is Black Tea (Hong Cha)?

In Chinese, black tea is 红茶 (hóng chá) — literally "red tea," named for the amber-red color of the brewed liquor rather than the color of the leaf. English speakers call it black tea because the finished leaves are dark and glossy. Confusingly, what Chinese calls 黑茶 (hēi chá, "black tea") — pu-erh and other post-fermented teas — is an entirely different family of tea. When you order "black tea" in the West, the Chinese equivalent is 红茶, not 黑茶.

Black tea is made from the same plant as green tea (Camellia sinensis), but the leaves are allowed to fully oxidize — a process tea makers still traditionally call "fermentation" — before being dried. Rolling the leaves breaks down cell walls and lets enzymes transform the leaf's catechins into theaflavins and thearubigins. That chemistry gives black tea its copper-black color, its malt-and-honey sweetness, and, in TCM terms, its warming nature.

Well-known Chinese black teas include:

  • Lapsang Souchong (正山小种) — from Fujian's Wuyi mountains, the oldest known black tea, often finished over pine smoke.
  • Keemun (祁门红茶) — from Anhui; floral, wine-like, and one of the classic bases for English breakfast blends.
  • Dianhong (滇红) — from Yunnan; golden tips, sweet and malty.
  • Jin Jun Mei (金骏眉) — a delicate, honeyed Fujian tea made from young buds.
  • Yingde Hong (英德红茶) — from Guangdong; bold, bright, and popular in milk tea.

In TCM food therapy, black tea is the warm one. Where green tea is described as cooling and brisk, black tea is considered warm and settling — the cup that belongs to winter mornings, to cold weather, and to the end of a heavy meal.

Black Tea's TCM Properties: Nature, Flavor, and Meridians

According to classical Chinese materia medica, every food and drink is described by its nature (xing), flavor (wei), and the organ networks, or meridians (gui jing), it is believed to influence:

  • Nature (性): Warm (温) — sometimes described as "warm, leaning toward neutral," which fits its mellow, rounded character.
  • Flavor (味): Sweet (甘) first, with a mild bitter note (苦) and a lingering sweet aftertaste (回甘).
  • Meridians (归经): Traditionally associated with the Heart (心), Spleen (脾), and Stomach (胃) networks.

Why should processing change a plant's nature at all? TCM explains it as a shift in the food's energetic direction. Fresh tea leaf is bitter, cold, and clearing — traditionally used to "descend fire." Full oxidation softens the bitterness, develops sweetness, and turns the tea's energy inward and downward: warm, nourishing, and settling. The sweet flavor is understood to nourish and harmonize, the mild bitterness to move and drain, and the warmth to disperse cold.

What the Classical Texts Say

Tea's place in Chinese medicine was recorded long before black tea existed. Lu Yu's Classic of Tea (《茶经》, Tang dynasty, 8th century) already describes tea as bitter and cooling, and as something best taken in moderation rather than in large quantities.

Li Shizhen's Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》, 1578) was written just as black tea was emerging in the Wuyi mountains of Fujian. It characterizes tea as "bitter and cold, the yin within yin," notes that it is at its best when clearing excess fire, and makes the important observation that roasting and processing change the leaf's character — the earliest hint that tea made warm is not the same medicine as tea made cold.

A century later, Zhao Xuemin's Supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目拾遗》) records that Wuyi tea — an early black tea — is warm in nature and was traditionally used to help disperse rich, greasy food and settle the stomach. Later texts and household practice repeat the phrase that made black tea famous in daily life: 红茶暖胃 — "black tea warms the stomach."

These are traditional observations recorded over centuries and repeated in practice. They describe a pattern of use, not a medical claim, and they are best read as a guide to sensible, seasonal drinking rather than a prescription.

How Black Tea Is Used in TCM Food Therapy

Warming the Middle Burner (温中)

The "middle burner" (中焦) is the digestive center — the Spleen, the Stomach, and the space between them. When it runs cold, the classic signs are cold hands and feet, a preference for warm drinks, loose stools, and a chill that settles in the belly after raw or cold food. TCM food therapy reaches for warm, sweet, easy-to-digest foods in this pattern, and black tea — warm in nature, sweet in flavor, gentle on the stomach — is traditionally one of them.

Aiding Digestion and Cutting Greasiness (消食解腻)

The most familiar role of strong black tea in Chinese households is at the end of a rich meal. Its mild bitterness is believed to help the Stomach move food downward, while its warmth keeps that movement comfortable — the opposite of the heavy, sluggish feeling that follows too much oil and meat. Pu-erh is the tea most famous for "cutting grease," but in many regions a strong pot of black tea does exactly the same job.

Supporting Cold and Damp Patterns (寒湿)

Warm tea is traditionally offered for cold-type discomfort: a chill that settles in the stomach, occasional loose stools after cold food or cold drinks, and the heavy, achy feeling that damp weather brings. For this purpose, black tea is often brewed with a slice or two of fresh ginger, a piece of chenpi (dried tangerine peel), or a small piece of brown sugar — simple additions that reinforce the tea's warming direction.

Lifting the Spirits in the Morning

Tea's aroma is described in TCM as aromatic and penetrating: it moves qi, clears the head, and lifts the mood. A cup of black tea in the morning is a traditional way to wake the Spleen, open the senses, and start the day's qi moving. Because black tea also contains caffeine, it is best kept to the morning and early afternoon.

Black Tea vs Green Tea vs Pu-erh

If you remember only one thing about tea in TCM, remember that processing decides the nature:

  • Green tea (绿茶) — unoxidized, cooling and bitter-sweet; clears heat and refreshes. Traditionally favored in summer and by people who tend to feel hot.
  • White tea (白茶) — lightly oxidized, cool to neutral; the gentlest of the family.
  • Oolong (乌龙) — partly oxidized, neutral to slightly warm; a good all-rounder.
  • Black tea (红茶) — fully oxidized, warm; the cold-weather cup and the after-dinner companion.
  • Pu-erh and other dark teas (黑茶) — post-fermented by microbes, warm and deep; traditionally the tea for digestion and rich food.

A simple rule of thumb: if you often feel cold, prefer black tea; if you often feel hot and thirsty, prefer green; if you fall somewhere in between, choose oolong or a lighter black tea.

How to Brew Black Tea

Black tea is forgiving, but a few habits make a real difference: use enough leaf, keep the water just below a rolling boil, and don't over-steep.

What You Need

  • Black tea leaves: about 3–5 grams (1–2 teaspoons) per 200–250 ml of water
  • Water at roughly 90–95 °C (194–203 °F) — just off the boil
  • A teapot, gaiwan, or a mug with a strainer
  • Optional: fresh ginger, chenpi, one or two jujubes, or a little honey

Simple Everyday Brew

  1. Warm the vessel. Rinse your pot or mug with hot water and pour it out. This keeps the first steep hot.
  2. Add the leaves. 1 teaspoon of leaf per cup, or 1 rounded teaspoon per 250 ml if you like it stronger.
  3. Pour the water. Let boiled water rest for a minute or two, then pour it over the leaves.
  4. Steep 2–3 minutes. Taste at two minutes. Beyond four minutes, tannins dominate and the cup turns bitter and drying.
  5. Decant. Pour the tea off the leaves (or lift the strainer) so it stops steeping. Re-steep the same leaves once or twice.

Gongfu-Style Brew

  1. Use a small teapot or gaiwan and about 5 grams of leaf.
  2. Rinse the leaves with hot water and immediately discard that first pour — it wakes the leaf and warms the vessel.
  3. Steep with near-boiling water for 10–15 seconds, pour off, and repeat. Each steep can grow by a few seconds; good whole-leaf black tea gives six to eight enjoyable steeps.

Classic Pairings

  • Ginger black tea (姜红茶). Simmer two or three slices of fresh ginger for 5 minutes, then pour over the leaves. The classic cold-day and cold-stomach combination.
  • Chenpi black tea (陈皮红茶). Add a few strips of dried tangerine peel. Traditionally used to support the Spleen and help transform phlegm.
  • Jujube black tea (红枣红茶). Steep with two or three dried red dates, split open. A sweeter, blood-supporting brew for cold weather.
  • Rose black tea (玫瑰红茶). Add a few dried rose buds to help move liver qi and lift the mood.
  • Milk tea (奶茶). Pleasant and popular, but note that in TCM milk is considered damp-forming and slightly cooling — it softens the warming effect. Drink it as a comfort, and keep plain or ginger tea for the traditional purposes.

Buying and Storing

Buy whole-leaf black tea when you can: it brews sweeter, steeps longer, and gives a cleaner cup than the dust in most paper tea bags. Smell the leaf before you buy — it should be malty, honeyed, or lightly smoky, never flat or dusty. Store black tea in an airtight tin away from light, heat, moisture, and strong spices; unlike pu-erh, black tea does not improve with long aging and is best within one to two years.

When to Drink Black Tea

  • Morning and mid-morning — the traditional time, to lift the spirits and start digestion quietly.
  • After meals — especially after rich, oily, or heavy food.
  • In cold weather — autumn and winter, or any cold, damp day, ideally with ginger.
  • Not on an empty stomach — strong tea without food can leave the stomach sour or unsettled, and in TCM terms it can disturb the middle burner. Eat something small first, or brew it weaker.
  • Not late in the evening — black tea carries caffeine; if you are sensitive, switch to a caffeine-free infusion after lunch.
  • Keep a gap from medication — leave one to two hours between tea and prescription medicines or iron supplements, since tea tannins can reduce absorption.

Precautions and Who Should Be Careful

Black tea is one of the safest everyday drinks in the Chinese tradition, but it does not suit everyone equally, and moderation is part of the practice:

  • Pregnancy and nursing: black tea contains caffeine and tannins. Many authorities advise keeping total caffeine modest; ask your healthcare provider what is right for you.
  • Iron-deficiency anemia: the tannins in tea can reduce iron absorption. Avoid drinking it with or immediately after iron-rich meals, and leave a gap before or after iron supplements.
  • Insomnia and anxiety: caffeine can aggravate both. Keep black tea to the morning, or choose a caffeine-free alternative such as roasted barley or chamomile.
  • Acid reflux, gastritis, or ulcers: strong tea may irritate an inflamed stomach lining. Drink it weaker, after food, and not in large amounts.
  • Palpitations or arrhythmia: the caffeine in tea can be stimulating; limit intake and discuss it with your doctor.
  • Kidney stone history: tea contains oxalates. Very strong, very frequent tea drinking is best avoided in this case; hydration matters more than the tea itself.
  • Children: offer weak tea in small amounts, if at all, and avoid it close to bedtime.
  • Tea drunkenness (茶醉): drinking very strong tea on an empty stomach can cause dizziness, nausea, a racing heart, or cold sweats. Eat something sweet or a small snack, and next time brew it weaker and after food.

As with all food therapy, black tea is a daily ritual that complements — never replaces — professional medical care. If you have a digestive condition, a heart condition, or are managing a chronic illness, talk to a qualified practitioner before making it a daily habit.

FAQ

In TCM, is black tea warming or cooling?

Black tea is classified as warm in nature and sweet in flavor in TCM food therapy, traditionally associated with the Heart, Spleen, and Stomach meridians. It is the warm member of the tea family: green tea is cooling, oolong is neutral to warm, and fully oxidized black tea is warm — which is why it is the classic cold-weather cup and the companion to heavy meals.

Can I drink black tea every day?

Yes. In TCM practice black tea is enjoyed daily, most often in the morning or after meals, and two to three cups a day is a reasonable habit for most healthy adults. Because it contains caffeine, keep it to the earlier part of the day if you are sensitive, avoid brewing it very strong on an empty stomach, and leave one to two hours between tea and any medication or iron supplement.

Why is black tea called red tea in Chinese?

Black tea is 红茶 (hóng chá) — "red tea" — in Chinese, named for the amber-red color of the brewed liquor rather than the leaf. English speakers call it black tea because of the dark oxidized leaf. What Chinese calls 黑茶 (dark tea), such as pu-erh, is a different, post-fermented family of tea, so the two names do not describe the same drink.

Is black tea with milk still warming in TCM?

Adding milk softens the tea's warming character in TCM thinking, because milk is considered nourishing but damp-forming and slightly cooling. Milk tea is a pleasant everyday comfort drink, but if you want the traditional warming, digestion-supporting quality, drink the tea plain or with ginger, chenpi, or a small piece of jujube instead of milk.

Who should limit or avoid black tea?

Pregnant and nursing women are usually advised to limit caffeine, including black tea. People with iron-deficiency anemia, insomnia, anxiety, acid reflux, or a history of stomach ulcers may also want to limit it or drink it weaker and after food. Anyone taking prescription medication should leave a gap of one to two hours and ask a qualified healthcare provider if unsure.
