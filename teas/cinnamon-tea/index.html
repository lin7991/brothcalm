Cinnamon Tea — A Warming Brew for Cold Hands & Digestion

When the weather turns cold and your hands feel like they belong to someone else, a warm cup of cinnamon tea is one of the oldest comfort remedies in the Chinese kitchen. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), cinnamon is not just a spice for baking — it is a warming, aromatic herb that has been used for centuries to chase off cold, settle the stomach, and support the body's inner fire.

This guide covers the TCM story of cinnamon, how it is traditionally used, a simple step-by-step recipe for cinnamon tea, and who should think twice before drinking it.

What Is Cinnamon in TCM?

In TCM, cinnamon is known as rou gui (肉桂) when it comes from the thick bark of the Cinnamomum cassia tree, and gui zhi (桂枝) when it comes from the twigs. For tea, the bark form is most common.

TCM Properties

  • Nature: Hot and drying (大热)
  • Flavor: Pungent (辛) and sweet (甘)
  • Channels: Kidneys, spleen, heart, and liver

In TCM theory, cinnamon is one of the classic "warming the interior" herbs. Its pungent, hot nature is believed to warm the middle burner (the digestive center of the body), strengthen the spleen, and help kidney yang — the body's metabolic "fire" — to reach the hands and feet.

Traditional Uses

Cinnamon has been used in Chinese medicine for over two thousand years. The Shennong Bencao Jing (Divine Farmer's Materia Medica), one of the oldest pharmacopoeias, lists cinnamon among its "upper class" herbs. Traditionally it is used to:

  • Warm the middle and dispel cold — for cold stomach, poor appetite, and sluggish digestion
  • Warm the channels and promote circulation — for cold hands and feet, and a general feeling of chilliness
  • Support kidney yang — in classical formulas for fatigue, low energy, and frequent urination linked to yang deficiency
  • Settle the stomach — for mild nausea or a heavy feeling after eating

It is important to say that these uses are based on TCM tradition, not clinical proof. In the language of modern wellness, cinnamon is also known to be rich in antioxidants and is sometimes studied for its effect on blood sugar, but that research is separate from the TCM food-therapy story told here.

Why Cinnamon Tea for Cold Hands and Digestion?

Cold hands and feet are, in TCM thinking, a common sign of yang deficiency or qi and blood not reaching the extremities. The body's warmth is generated by yang energy; when it is weak, the periphery gets cold first. Cinnamon is believed to warm the channels and help push warmth outward, which is why a warm, spicy cup of cinnamon tea is a traditional answer to chilly hands.

For digestion, the warming, pungent nature of cinnamon is thought to "wake up" a sluggish spleen and stomach. Many people notice that a small cup of cinnamon tea after a heavy or cold meal leaves them feeling more comfortable — an experience that matches the traditional description of cinnamon as a herb that warms the middle and harmonizes the stomach.

How to Brew Cinnamon Tea (Simple Recipe)

The simplest version needs only two ingredients: cinnamon and hot water. You can scale it up for a whole pot or make it in a single mug.

Ingredients

  • 1 cinnamon stick (about 5 cm / 2 inches), or 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon powder
  • 400 ml (about 1 2/3 cups) hot water
  • Optional: 2-3 slices of fresh ginger, 2 dried jujube dates, or a teaspoon of honey

Steps

  1. If using a cinnamon stick, gently crush or break it in half to release the aromatic oils.
  2. Place the cinnamon (and any optional ginger or jujube) in a teapot or heatproof mug.
  3. Pour over just-boiled water, cover, and steep for 10 minutes. The longer it steeps, the stronger and spicier the tea becomes.
  4. Strain and enjoy warm. Add honey only after the tea has cooled slightly — honey loses its character in boiling water.

Yield: 1-2 cups. Traditionally, cinnamon tea is sipped warm, not gulped down cold.

Variations

  • Cinnamon-Ginger Tea: Add 3-4 slices of fresh ginger for extra warmth — a classic pair for cold weather.
  • Cinnamon-Jujube Tea: Add 2 dried red dates to balance the spice with sweetness and support the spleen.
  • Cinnamon Apple Tea: Simmer a cinnamon stick with sliced apple for 10 minutes for a naturally sweet, warming drink.
  • Mulled-style spice blend: Combine cinnamon with a clove and a piece of dried orange peel for a cozy evening brew.

Who Should Be Careful with Cinnamon Tea?

Cinnamon is strongly warming, so it is not for everyone, and moderation matters even for those who enjoy it.

  • People with heat signs: If you tend toward dry mouth, sore throat, mouth ulcers, acne, or feeling hot easily, TCM would say your body already has too much heat — cinnamon may add to it.
  • Pregnant women: Because of its strong, warming, acrid nature, medicinal amounts of cinnamon are traditionally avoided during pregnancy. A sprinkle in cooking is a different matter; a daily tea habit deserves a conversation with your healthcare provider.
  • People taking blood-thinning medication: Cinnamon, especially in concentrated amounts, may interact with anticoagulants. Check with your doctor or pharmacist.
  • Children and sensitive stomachs: Start with a very small amount — half a stick — and see how it sits.

A good general rule: one cup a day during cold weather is plenty for most people, and it is best enjoyed in the morning or early afternoon rather than late at night.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drink cinnamon tea every day?

For most people, one cup a day is a reasonable amount, especially during cooler months. Keep the dose moderate — about one 10-minute steep of a stick or up to 1/2 teaspoon of powder — and stop if you notice heat signs like dry mouth or a sore throat.

Does cinnamon tea actually help with cold hands and feet?

In TCM theory, cinnamon is believed to warm the channels and promote the smooth flow of qi and blood to the extremities. It may support circulation and help you feel warmer, but it works best as part of a broader routine of warm food, movement, and good sleep.

Is cinnamon bark the same as cinnamon powder?

They come from the same plant, but sticks release flavor slowly and make a clearer tea, while powder infuses quickly and is easy to overdo. For tea, a stick is often the more traditional choice.

Can pregnant women drink cinnamon tea?

TCM tradition generally advises pregnant women to be cautious with strongly warming herbs. A small culinary amount is usually considered fine, but a daily medicinal tea should be discussed with your healthcare provider first.

When should I drink cinnamon tea for the best effect?

Morning and early afternoon are the traditional windows, when the body's yang energy is rising. Late-night cups may disturb sleep for some people.
