Luo Han Guo Tea (Monk Fruit): Sweet Lung Relief Without Sugar

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 4 min read

Luo han guo — called monk fruit in the West — is a small, round fruit that grows in the misty mountains of Guangxi in southern China. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), it has been valued for centuries as a cooling, moistening food that supports the lungs and throat.

Brewed into tea, the dried fruit produces a deep amber drink with a rich, raisin-like sweetness — yet it contains no added sugar. For anyone who craves something sweet while trying to cut back on sugar, monk fruit tea is one of the most pleasant bridges between ancient food wisdom and modern wellness.

What Is Luo Han Guo?

Luo han guo (Siraitia grosvenorii) is a perennial vine in the gourd family, related to melons and cucumbers. The fruit has been cultivated for centuries in Guangxi province, particularly around the towns of Yongfu and Lingui, where the cool mountain climate is said to give it its characteristic sweetness.

Locals nicknamed it “the immortal fruit” (神仙果, shén xiān guǒ), partly because the dried fruit keeps for a very long time and partly because of its reputation as a gentle throat and lung soother. It appears in the Qing dynasty supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目拾遗》), where it is described as sweet and cooling, useful for coughs and dry throat.

Most of the fruit’s sweetness comes from compounds called mogrosides, which are intensely sweet yet contain negligible calories. In TCM practice, the whole dried fruit — shell, flesh, and seeds together — is brewed, not just an isolated extract.

Luo Han Guo in TCM: Properties and Actions

In the TCM framework, every food carries a thermal nature, a taste, and a set of organ pathways (meridians). Luo han guo is described as:

  • Nature: cool (凉)
  • Taste: sweet (甘)
  • Meridians: Lung and Large Intestine

Because it is cool and sweet, luo han guo is traditionally used to:

  • Clear lung heat — helpful when the body feels “heated” after rich, spicy, or fried food
  • Moisten the lungs and ease dry cough — traditionally used for dry, tickling coughs with little phlegm
  • Promote fluids and relieve thirst — its sweetness is believed to support the body’s production of moisture
  • Moisten the intestines — in TCM the sweet, moistening quality is thought to support easy digestion and regular bowel movements

In TCM theory, dryness and heat often disturb the Lung, which governs the skin and respiration and is considered especially sensitive to dry environments. A cool, moistening food like monk fruit is believed to help balance that pattern.

Sweet Without Sugar: The Modern Appeal

Monk fruit has found a second life in the modern wellness world as a natural zero-calorie sweetener. Mogrosides are reported to be roughly 150–300 times sweeter than table sugar, and some studies suggest they do not raise blood sugar the way sucrose does.

For everyday tea drinking, this means you can brew a genuinely sweet, satisfying drink without reaching for honey or sugar. People who follow low-sugar diets, parents who want to limit their children’s sugar intake, and anyone who simply prefers natural sweetness all have a reason to keep a few dried fruits in the pantry.

How to Make Luo Han Guo Tea

The classic preparation is almost embarrassingly simple — the fruit does all the sweetening work itself.

Classic Monk Fruit Tea

Ingredients:

  • 1 dried luo han guo (or half for a lighter, less sweet pot)
  • About 1.2 liters (5 cups) of water
  • Optional: 3–4 red dates and a small handful of goji berries for extra warmth

Steps:

  1. Rinse the dried fruit under running water. Crack the shell lightly with the back of a spoon — it is brittle and opens easily — so the flesh is exposed.
  2. Drop the pieces into a pot and add the water.
  3. Bring to a boil, then lower the heat and simmer for 15–20 minutes, until the water turns a deep amber-gold and smells richly sweet.
  4. Strain into cups and drink warm. The tea keeps in the fridge for 2–3 days and can be reheated or served cold.

Tip: one dried fruit can be brewed twice. The second brew is lighter but still pleasantly sweet — an economical habit that matches the fruit’s “immortal” reputation.

Three Easy Variations

  • Monk fruit & chrysanthemum tea: add a small handful of dried chrysanthemum flowers for the last 5 minutes of simmering. The cooling combination is traditionally enjoyed when both the throat and the eyes feel strained — a common complaint after long days of screen work. (Similar in spirit to our goji chrysanthemum tea.)
  • Monk fruit & snow pear: simmer the fruit with one sliced Asian pear for a richer, more deeply moisturizing drink that is traditionally turned to during dry autumn weather.
  • Cold-brew monk fruit tea: crack half a fruit into a large jar of cold water and leave it in the refrigerator overnight. The result is a clean, lightly sweet iced tea with no bitterness.

When to Drink It

Monk fruit tea is a flexible everyday drink, but it shines at specific moments:

  • When your throat feels dry, scratchy, or “rough” from talking, singing, or dry indoor heat
  • After spicy, fried, or heavy meals when you feel internally heated
  • During autumn, when TCM tradition says dryness can easily affect the lungs
  • In hot weather, served cool, as a naturally sweet way to stay hydrated

Who Should Be Careful

Because luo han guo is cool in nature, TCM tradition advises people with a cold or deficient spleen-stomach pattern — think loose stools, weak appetite, a pale tongue, or constantly cold hands and feet — to drink it less often or to balance it with warming companions such as ginger or red dates.

  • Pregnancy and nursing: monk fruit tea is generally regarded as a mild food drink, but because research is limited, it is wise to check with your healthcare provider first.
  • Children: a small, diluted cup occasionally is usually fine — treat it as a treat, not a tonic.
  • Digestion: the fruit is very sweet and its cooling nature may loosen stools if consumed in large amounts. Start with half a fruit and see how your body responds.

Finally, monk fruit tea is a food, not a medicine. If a cough, sore throat, or any symptom is severe, persistent, or accompanied by fever, please seek professional medical care.
