Lemon Balm Tea — Gentle Stress Relief from the Garden

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 5 min read

Some evenings ask for something gentler than coffee and warmer than plain water — a cup of lemon balm tea with its soft citrus scent and quietly soothing character. Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) has been grown in kitchen gardens for centuries, valued for its calming aroma and its place in traditional wellness routines. In the language of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), it is often described as a herb that gently moves the qi, settles the shen (spirit), and helps the body find its way back to ease.

This guide covers how lemon balm is understood in TCM food therapy, its traditional properties and uses, a complete brewing recipe with variations, and the practical details of when and how to drink it — plus who may want to be more careful with it.

What Is Lemon Balm?

Lemon balm is a leafy perennial in the mint family, native to southern Europe and the Mediterranean. Its leaves carry a bright lemony fragrance with a hint of mint, which is why it has long been a favorite in herbal teas, summer drinks, and even salads. The name Melissa comes from the Greek word for honeybee — bees are famously drawn to its flowers — and the plant has been associated with calm and refreshment since ancient times.

In modern herbal practice, lemon balm is frequently used for two things: helping people feel less tense, and supporting a good night's sleep. In TCM-inspired food therapy, it fits naturally into the category of herbs that soothe the Liver and quiet the Shen — the same group of gentle remedies traditionally chosen when stress, worry, or a racing mind make it hard to unwind.

Lemon Balm in TCM Theory: Nature, Flavor, and Meridians

In TCM, every food and herb is described by its nature (temperature), flavor, and the organ systems (meridians) it is believed to influence. While lemon balm is not an ancient Chinese materia medica classic, modern TCM-inspired food therapy reads it through the same lens:

  • Nature (性): Cool and slightly drying. Its coolness makes it a natural choice for warm, restless evenings or for people who tend to feel hot, irritable, and on edge.
  • Flavor (味): Pungent, bitter, and slightly sweet. Pungent herbs are traditionally said to move and disperse; bitter herbs are said to clear heat and dry dampness; sweet herbs are said to harmonize and nourish.
  • Meridians (归经): Most commonly associated with the Liver and Heart. The Liver governs the smooth flow of qi in TCM, while the Heart is said to house the shen. Together, these two channels are the classic targets of calming herbal teas.

When stress builds up in TCM terms, it is often described as Liver qi stagnation — a state where qi gets "stuck" and shows up as irritability, a tight chest, sighing, tension headaches, or difficulty falling asleep. Calming teas like lemon balm are traditionally used to help that qi flow more freely again, which is why they are paired so often with gentle breathing and a quiet evening routine.

Traditional Uses of Lemon Balm Tea

Lemon balm has a long history in European herbalism as a "gladdening" herb — one that lifts the mood without overstimulating. In TCM-inspired food therapy, it is traditionally used for:

  • Calming a busy mind: A cup in the evening is believed to help settle scattered thoughts and support a peaceful transition into sleep.
  • Soothing tension in the body: Its cool, pungent nature is traditionally said to ease the tightness of stress — tense shoulders, a tight chest, or a knotted stomach.
  • Supporting digestion after a heavy meal: Like other mint-family herbs, lemon balm is traditionally used to help the stomach settle and to relieve mild bloating or indigestion.
  • Cooling a hot, restless mood: For people who feel irritable, flushed, or overheated, the cooling quality of lemon balm is believed to bring a sense of refreshment.

In the same way that TCM pairs ingredients by nature and flavor, lemon balm is rarely drunk alone in traditional practice — it is usually blended with a sweet, nourishing partner (like jujube dates or a little honey) to balance its coolness and make the tea more harmonizing.

How to Brew Lemon Balm Tea: Complete Recipe

Ingredients

  • 1 to 2 tablespoons fresh lemon balm leaves (or 1 to 2 teaspoons dried)
  • 1 cup (about 240 ml) freshly boiled water
  • Optional: 2 to 3 jujube dates, sliced, or 1 teaspoon honey
  • Optional: a few mint leaves or a thin slice of fresh ginger

Steps

  1. Prepare the leaves. If using fresh lemon balm, rinse the leaves and gently tear them to release their oils. If using dried lemon balm, measure it into your cup or teapot.
  2. Add the extras (optional). Drop in sliced jujube dates, mint, or ginger for a more layered, nourishing brew.
  3. Pour the hot water. Water that has just come off the boil — about 90°C (195°F) — is ideal. Boiling water straight from the kettle can make the tea taste slightly bitter.
  4. Steep for 5 to 10 minutes. Cover the cup while steeping to keep the aromatic oils from escaping. Five minutes gives a light, fragrant cup; ten minutes gives a deeper, more calming brew.
  5. Strain and sweeten. Strain out the leaves, stir in honey if you like, and drink it warm — ideally sitting down, with no screens, for a few quiet minutes.

Variations to Try

  • Lemon Balm & Chamomile: Equal parts of both herbs for a classic wind-down blend before bed.
  • Lemon Balm & Jujube Date: Add two sliced jujube dates to the pot. The dates add gentle sweetness and are traditionally believed to nourish the blood and calm the shen.
  • Lemon Balm & Ginger: Add two thin slices of fresh ginger for a warming twist — a nice balance if the cool nature of lemon balm feels too cold for your digestion.
  • Iced Lemon Balm Refresher: Brew a double-strength pot, let it cool, and pour over ice with a squeeze of lemon for a daytime cooler.

When to Drink Lemon Balm Tea

Timing matters in TCM food therapy. Lemon balm is best suited to the late afternoon and evening, when the body is winding down. Some people also reach for it mid-morning when stress tends to build. A few simple guidelines:

  • For sleep: Drink a cup about 60 to 90 minutes before bed, as part of a calm routine — dim lights, slow breathing, no phone.
  • For stress at work: A smaller cup (half the recipe) in the early afternoon can be a gentle reset without making you drowsy.
  • For digestion: Drink it after a heavy or rich meal rather than before, to help the stomach settle.

Consistency beats quantity. One well-timed cup every evening is traditionally considered more helpful than several cups at random moments.

Who Should Be Careful With Lemon Balm

Lemon balm is generally regarded as a mild, food-grade herb, but a few groups should exercise caution:

  • People taking sedatives or thyroid medication: Lemon balm may interact with some medications. Talk to your doctor before adding it to your routine.
  • Pregnant or nursing women: Herbal teas are best discussed with a midwife or healthcare provider first.
  • People with very cold digestion: Because lemon balm is cool in nature, someone who feels constantly cold, has cold hands and feet, or gets loose stools from cold drinks may prefer a warming partner like ginger or jujube dates alongside it.
  • Anyone about to drive or operate machinery: If you are sensitive to calming herbs, check how the tea affects you before getting behind the wheel.

As with any herbal remedy, moderation is the traditional principle: enjoy lemon balm tea as a pleasant daily ritual, not as a treatment for a medical condition.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drink lemon balm tea every day?

For most people, one to two cups a day is traditionally considered gentle and well-tolerated. In TCM practice, calming herbs are often used in focused courses of a few weeks, then paused. If you take any medication, check with a healthcare provider first.

Does lemon balm tea help with sleep?

It is traditionally used to settle the mind and quiet the shen, which in TCM theory supports restful sleep. Many people find a warm cup about an hour before bed helps them wind down — best thought of as a calming ritual rather than a strong sleep aid.

Is lemon balm tea safe during pregnancy?

Always discuss herbal teas with your midwife or doctor during pregnancy. Lemon balm has a long food history and is generally regarded as mild, but professional guidance is the safest approach.

Can I combine lemon balm with other herbs?

Yes. It pairs beautifully with chamomile for relaxation, with mint for digestion, or with goji berries and jujube dates for a sweet, nourishing evening brew.
