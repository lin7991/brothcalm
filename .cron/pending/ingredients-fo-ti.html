Fo-Ti (He Shou Wu) — The Longevity Herb for Hair and Vitality

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 7 min read

There is an old story told in Traditional Chinese Medicine about a man named He, who was frail, gray-haired, and middle-aged beyond his years. After taking a root that grew wild near his village, his hair is said to have turned black again, his strength returned, and he lived long enough to see his descendants many generations deep. The root became known as He Shou Wu — "Mr. He's black hair" — and Western herbalists came to call it Fo-Ti.

Whether or not the legend is literally true, Fo-Ti (He Shou Wu, 何首乌) has been one of the most celebrated longevity herbs in Chinese food therapy for over a thousand years. It is prized not as a dramatic medicine but as a gentle, nourishing tonic — the kind of ingredient that works slowly, quietly, and deeply. In this guide, we explore what TCM tradition says about Fo-Ti, how it is prepared, and how to use it safely in everyday cooking.

What Is Fo-Ti (He Shou Wu)?

Fo-Ti is the root of Polygonum multiflorum (also known as Fallopia multiflora), a climbing vine native to China. The name "Fo-Ti" comes from the phonetic translation of the Mandarin he shou wu, and the herb has been documented in Chinese materia medica since the Tang dynasty. It appears in the classic text Kai Bao Ben Cao (《开宝本草》), compiled in the 10th century, where it is described as a tonic for the blood and a support for long life.

In Chinese pharmacies you will usually find two forms:

  • Prepared Fo-Ti (Zhi He Shou Wu, 制何首乌) — the root is traditionally processed by steaming with black bean juice until soft and dark. This is the form used for nourishing and tonifying, and it is the version most commonly used in food therapy.
  • Raw Fo-Ti (Sheng He Shou Wu, 生何首乌) — the unprocessed root, which is considered stronger in action and is traditionally used for clearing heat and moving stagnation. In TCM it is not recommended for long-term daily use.

Most traditional tonic formulas call for the prepared form, and the processing method matters: the black bean steaming is said to "guide" the herb toward the kidney and liver channels and to soften its potency.

TCM Energetics: What Makes Fo-Ti Special

Temperature: Slightly Warm

Fo-Ti is classified as slightly warm, making it a comfortable tonic for most constitutions. Unlike aggressively hot herbs, it is considered gentle enough to be used over longer periods in soups and congee.

Flavor: Bitter, Sweet, and Astringent

Its flavor profile is bittersweet with a slightly astringent finish. In TCM, sweet flavors tonify, bitter flavors drain and dry, and astringent qualities help hold and preserve — which is why Fo-Ti is traditionally associated with "holding" the body's essence rather than dispersing it.

Meridian Tropism: Liver and Kidney

Fo-Ti is traditionally said to enter the Liver and Kidney meridians. In TCM theory, the kidneys store jing (essence), which is linked to aging, bones, and reproductive vitality, while the liver stores the blood that nourishes the hair and nails. This dual tropism is the foundation of Fo-Ti's reputation as a longevity herb.

Traditional Uses: Hair, Jing, and the Art of Aging Well

The most famous traditional use of Fo-Ti is for hair health. In TCM, premature graying or thinning hair is often interpreted as a sign of blood deficiency or weakened kidney essence. Because Fo-Ti is traditionally considered a blood and kidney tonic, it is a recurring ingredient in formulas for gray hair, hair loss, and weak nails.

Beyond the hair, Fo-Ti is traditionally used to support:

  • Kidney essence — lower back weakness, sore knees, and fatigue are traditionally linked to kidney deficiency, and Fo-Ti is one of the herbs classically paired with Eucommia (Du Zhong) for this purpose.
  • Blood nourishment — in TCM theory, Fo-Ti is believed to help build and replenish the blood, supporting a calm mind, restful sleep, and healthy complexion.
  • Graceful aging — texts describe it as a "longevity" herb that supports vitality in later years, often combined with goji berries, jujube dates, and Chinese yam.
  • Warm, nourishing recovery — in traditional postpartum and recovery congee, small amounts of prepared Fo-Ti are sometimes added to support the body's rebuilding process.

Fo-Ti is rarely used alone. In classic formulas, it is almost always paired with other tonics — goji berries, suan zao ren, black sesame, or longan — to round out its action and make it suitable for longer-term use.

Modern Scientific Research

Modern research on Fo-Ti is ongoing, and findings should be read with care. Some laboratory and animal studies have explored its traditional uses:

Compounds of Interest

Fo-Ti contains a range of bioactive compounds, including stilbenes (such as resveratrol-like molecules), anthraquinones (such as emodin), phospholipids, and lecithin. Some studies have suggested the stilbene glycosides may have antioxidant properties, which aligns with TCM's description of Fo-Ti as an anti-aging tonic.

Hair and Melanin Research

A few animal studies have investigated Fo-Ti extracts and melanin production, with some suggesting possible effects on hair pigmentation. These are early-stage findings and do not constitute evidence that Fo-Ti can prevent or reverse graying in humans.

Liver Consideration

One of the most important modern findings is a caution: some cases of liver injury have been reported in association with Fo-Ti, particularly with raw Fo-Ti or high-dose, long-term use. This is why TCM tradition uses the prepared form and moderate doses, and why anyone with liver conditions should avoid it entirely. This is a case where modern research and traditional practice agree — respect the herb.

How to Cook with Fo-Ti

Prepared Fo-Ti has a mild, slightly earthy-sweet taste that disappears into soups and congee. It is not a star flavor — it is a background tonic. Here are the most common ways to use it:

1. Fo-Ti Black Bean Congee (首乌黑豆粥)

This simple congee echoes the traditional processing method and is one of the gentlest introductions to Fo-Ti:

  • 10 g prepared Fo-Ti slices
  • 1/2 cup black beans (soaked overnight)
  • 1/2 cup rice
  • 4 cups water
  • 3-4 jujube dates, pitted

Simmer everything over low heat for 60-90 minutes until the beans are soft and the congee is creamy. Remove the Fo-Ti slices before serving. Sweeten lightly if desired.

2. Fo-Ti Longevity Soup (首乌养生汤)

  • 10 g prepared Fo-Ti
  • 1 small piece of pork or chicken bone (optional)
  • 20 g goji berries
  • 2 slices fresh ginger
  • 5-6 jujube dates

Add all ingredients to a pot with 6-8 cups of water. Bring to a boil, then simmer for 1.5 hours. Strain and drink the broth warm. This soup is traditionally enjoyed in cooler months for its warming, nourishing feel.

3. Fo-Ti Tea (首乌茶)

For a lighter daily ritual, simmer 3-5 g of prepared Fo-Ti with 2 cups of water for 20 minutes. Add a few goji berries and a slice of jujube for sweetness. Drink warm, ideally in the morning or early afternoon.

Dosage and Preparation Tips

  • Typical dose: In traditional food therapy, 3-10 g of prepared Fo-Ti per serving is common. It is meant to be used in cycles, not as an everyday supplement forever.
  • Always use the prepared form for tonic purposes. Raw Fo-Ti is traditionally used only in specific short-term situations and is not suitable for home cooking.
  • Simmer, don't boil hard. Gentle, long simmering is the traditional way to extract a tonic's goodness.
  • Cycle your use. Many traditional practitioners recommend using Fo-Ti for a few weeks, then taking a break, rather than using it continuously for months.
  • Pair it with food. Fo-Ti is at its best alongside blood- and kidney-nourishing foods like black beans, black sesame, goji berries, and jujube dates.

Who Should Use Fo-Ti?

In TCM thinking, Fo-Ti is traditionally considered for people who:

  • Notice premature graying or thinning hair and are otherwise healthy
  • Feel fatigued, with lower back and knee weakness
  • Experience dizziness or poor sleep linked to blood deficiency patterns
  • Want a gentle, warming tonic during cooler seasons

Who Should Avoid Fo-Ti

  • People with liver conditions — Fo-Ti should be completely avoided due to reported liver safety concerns.
  • People with loose stools or weak digestion — Fo-Ti has an astringent, slightly warm nature that traditionally doesn't suit damp or weak-spleen patterns.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women — as with any tonic herb, avoid unless a qualified practitioner advises otherwise.
  • Anyone on medication — always check with a healthcare provider, since herbal tonics can interact with prescription drugs.

Selecting and Storing Fo-Ti

When buying Fo-Ti, look for prepared (processed) slices that are dark brown to black, dense, and slightly glossy. Quality prepared Fo-Ti should smell mild and earthy — not sharp or sour. Store it in a cool, dry, airtight container away from sunlight, where it will keep for up to a year. If the slices develop a stale or musty smell, discard them.

Fo-Ti Through the Seasons

Fo-Ti fits naturally into seasonal eating patterns. Its slightly warm, nourishing nature makes it most comfortable in autumn and winter, when the body traditionally benefits from gentle tonics. In warmer months, TCM would typically favor lighter, cooler foods and save Fo-Ti for cooler weather or for short restorative cycles.

The Bottom Line

Fo-Ti (He Shou Wu) is one of the most beloved longevity herbs in Chinese food therapy — a quiet, deep tonic traditionally used to support hair health, kidney essence, and graceful aging. The key to using it well is respect: choose the prepared form, keep doses moderate, cycle your use, and skip it entirely if you have liver concerns or a weak digestive system.

Used the traditional way — simmered slowly into black bean congee, hearty soups, or a simple warming tea — Fo-Ti offers a gentle way to bring the wisdom of ancient Chinese food therapy into a modern kitchen.
