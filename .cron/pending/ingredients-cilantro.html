Cilantro (Yan Sui): The Detox Green in Chinese Food Therapy

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 5 min read

Cilantro — known as yan sui (芫荽) in Mandarin and called coriander in many parts of the world — is the most underappreciated green in the Chinese kitchen. A single chopped handful can lift a bowl of noodles, brighten a slow-braised dish, or turn a simple broth into something memorable. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), however, cilantro has always been regarded as far more than a garnish. It is classified as a pungent, warming herb that is traditionally used to support digestion, encourage healthy perspiration, and help the body release what it no longer needs — a role that has earned it a reputation as a natural detox green.

This guide explores cilantro through the lens of Chinese food therapy: its TCM properties, its traditional uses, the modern "detox" conversation around it, and practical ways to bring more of it into your daily meals.

Cilantro's TCM Properties: Warm, Pungent, and Moving

In TCM theory, every food carries a nature (hot, warm, neutral, cool, or cold) and a flavor (sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, or salty), and together these determine how the food acts on the body. Cilantro is classified as:

  • Nature: warm (温)
  • Flavor: pungent (辛)
  • Meridians: lung and spleen, with some classical texts also including the stomach

The pungent flavor is understood in TCM to be "moving" — it is believed to help qi and blood circulate rather than stagnate. Because cilantro enters the lung meridian, it is traditionally associated with the body's surface and its ability to "open" through perspiration. Because it also reaches the spleen and stomach, it is used to support the digestive transformation of food, especially after rich or heavy meals. This combination — warm enough to be activating, pungent enough to be aromatic, and gentle enough to be eaten by the handful — makes cilantro one of the most versatile herbs in the Chinese pantry.

Traditional Uses in Chinese Medicine

Cilantro has a long written history in Chinese medicine. The Ben Cao Gang Mu (《本草纲目》, Compendium of Materia Medica), compiled by the great Ming dynasty physician Li Shizhen, records cilantro's use for digestive complaints, cold-type discomforts, and skin eruptions. Traditional applications include:

Supporting Digestion

In TCM food therapy, cilantro is often recommended for what is called "food stagnation" — the heavy, bloated, slightly nauseated feeling that can follow an indulgent meal. Its aromatic pungency is believed to help the spleen and stomach "move" the food along, stimulate appetite, and settle the stomach. This is why you will so often see cilantro (and its relative, coriander seed) alongside rich, fatty, or slow-cooked dishes in Chinese cooking.

Encouraging Healthy Sweating

Because cilantro is warm and enters the lung meridian, it is traditionally used in formulas intended to "release the exterior" — that is, to encourage gentle perspiration that helps the body respond to early-stage wind-cold discomfort, such as the first chill of a cold. In classical practice, a warm soup or broth seasoned generously with cilantro was a common home-style response to feeling "under the weather."

Helping Skin Eruptions Surface

Historically, TCM practitioners used cilantro to help rashes such as measles "erupt" properly — the idea being that its moving, surface-reaching nature helps the body push things outward. This is an old, specific use that is rarely relevant to modern home cooking, but it illustrates why cilantro is understood as a herb that works on the surface of the body as well as the interior.

The "Detox" Connection: Tradition Meets Modern Interest

In modern wellness circles, cilantro is famous for a different reason: its reputation as a food that may help the body eliminate heavy metals. This idea comes mostly from contemporary research interest and folk use rather than classical TCM texts. Some studies suggest cilantro may support the body's natural elimination of certain metals, and many detox protocols include it as a gentle daily green. The TCM framework approaches the same intuition differently: cilantro's pungent, aromatic quality is believed to help the body release what it does not need — through sweat, through improved digestion, and through supporting the liver's clearing functions.

Whichever lens you prefer, the practical conclusion is the same. Cilantro is a safe, inexpensive, flavorful food that many traditions regard as gently cleansing. It is not a medical treatment, and it should not replace professional care for any serious condition — but as part of a balanced diet, it is a delicious way to support the body's own cleanup systems.

How to Choose and Store Cilantro

Because cilantro's value lives in its fragrance, freshness matters enormously. When buying:

  • Look for bright green leaves with no yellowing or wilting
  • Check the stems — they should be crisp, not limp or slimy
  • Give it a sniff — good cilantro smells distinctly aromatic and slightly citrusy
  • Avoid bunches with tiny flowers or seed heads, which signal older, bitter leaves

To keep it fresh for up to a week: trim the stems, stand the bunch in a glass with an inch of water, and loosely cover the leaves with a plastic bag in the refrigerator. Or wrap the roots in a damp paper towel and store in the crisper drawer. Change the water every day or two and it will stay perky far longer than a sad plastic bag in the fridge drawer.

How to Cook with Cilantro

The golden rule of cilantro in the Chinese kitchen: add it at the very end. Cilantro's aromatic compounds are delicate and evaporate quickly with heat. In TCM cooking, the herb is usually chopped and scattered over a finished dish, stirred into a just-cooked soup, or folded into a sauce moments before serving — preserving the fragrance that carries its warming, moving qualities.

Easy ways to use it daily:

  • Soup finishing: scatter a handful over noodle soups, congee, or bone broth just before serving
  • Stir-fry garnish: toss chopped cilantro in after the wok is off the heat
  • Cold dishes: mix with smashed cucumber, garlic, chili oil, and rice vinegar for a classic appetizer
  • Egg dishes: fold finely chopped cilantro into beaten eggs before pan-frying
  • Sauces and dips: blend into a fresh green dipping sauce with ginger and scallion

Quick Recipe: Cilantro and Ginger Tofu Soup

This five-minute soup is a lovely, warming way to use cilantro the way TCM home cooks always have — as a finishing herb for a light, comforting broth.

Ingredients:

  • 4 cups vegetable or chicken broth
  • 3 slices fresh ginger
  • 1 block soft tofu, cubed
  • 1 cup cilantro, roughly chopped
  • Soy sauce and sesame oil to taste

Steps:

  1. Bring the broth to a simmer with the ginger slices for 5 minutes.
  2. Add the tofu and simmer gently for another 3 minutes.
  3. Season with soy sauce. Turn off the heat, stir in the cilantro, and finish with a few drops of sesame oil.
  4. Serve hot — the cilantro keeps its fragrance because it never boils.

Who Should Eat Cilantro in Moderation

Cilantro is a gentle food, but TCM tradition still notes a few situations where moderation is wise:

  • Qi deficiency with easy sweating: because cilantro encourages perspiration, those who already sweat easily or feel constantly fatigued are traditionally advised not to overdo it
  • Certain skin conditions: people with hot, irritated, or weeping skin conditions are sometimes advised to limit pungent, "surface-moving" foods like cilantro
  • Allergies: cilantro belongs to the carrot/celery family — anyone with a known allergy to coriander or related plants should avoid it
  • Pregnancy: normal cooking amounts are fine in most traditions, but very large amounts are not recommended; when in doubt, ask your healthcare provider

As with any food, the best guide is your own body. Enjoy cilantro in ordinary meal-sized portions, and notice how you feel.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is cilantro the same as coriander?

Yes and no. In North America, "cilantro" refers to the fresh leaves and stems, while "coriander" usually means the dried seeds. In many other countries, the fresh plant is called coriander, and cilantro is the Chinese-influenced name. In TCM texts, both the fresh herb (yan sui) and the seed (hu sui zi) are used, though their properties differ slightly — the seeds are warmer and more drying.

Can I eat cilantro every day?

For most people, yes — a small daily handful in soups, salads, or stir-fries is a reasonable and traditional way to use it. If you notice excessive sweating, digestive upset, or skin irritation, simply reduce the amount. People with allergies or specific health conditions should follow their healthcare provider's advice.

Does cooking destroy cilantro's benefits?

Long cooking does. Cilantro's aromatic, pungent qualities — the very things TCM values — are fragile. To keep them, add cilantro at the end of cooking or use it raw. This is why Chinese recipes almost always treat cilantro as a finishing herb rather than a braising green.

Cilantro is proof that the humblest ingredients often carry the oldest wisdom. A green that many people scrape off their tacos and push to the edge of the plate has, for centuries, been one of Chinese food therapy's most dependable little helpers — warming, aromatic, digestive, and gently cleansing. Next time you chop a bunch for your noodles, you will know exactly why your grandmother's tradition kept it close.
