Chicken and Chinese Yam Soup — Spleen and Lung Nourishing Bowl

🥘 Recipe · 8 min read

There are soups that simply feed you, and then there are soups that seem to hold a whole tradition of healing in every warm spoonful. Chicken and Chinese yam soup (山药鸡汤, shān yào jī tāng) belongs firmly to the second category. Across centuries of Chinese home cooking, this gentle amber broth has been the bowl families turn to when someone needs strength — after a long illness, in the changeable seasons, or simply when the body feels tired and the digestion sluggish.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), this pairing is regarded as one of the most balanced combinations in the food therapy repertoire. Chicken is a classic Qi and Blood tonic, while Chinese yam — one of the most praised ingredients in the Chinese pharmacopoeia — is believed to strengthen the Spleen and Lung. Together they create a soup that is nourishing without being heavy, warming without being hot. This guide will walk you through the TCM wisdom behind the dish, the exact recipe, and the variations that make it your own.

The TCM Logic Behind the Pairing

TCM food therapy works on a simple principle: every food carries an energetic nature — its temperature, its flavor, and the organs it is said to support. When ingredients are combined deliberately, their energies work together like instruments in an orchestra. Chicken and Chinese yam are a well-rehearsed duet.

Chinese Yam: The Gentle Tonifier

Chinese yam (山药, shān yào), known to botanists as Dioscorea opposita, is classified in TCM as neutral in nature and sweet in flavor, and is said to enter the Spleen, Lung, and Kidney meridians. The Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing (神农本草经), one of the oldest Chinese herbal classics, lists yam as a top-grade substance — the highest classification, reserved for foods believed to nourish without harm when used over a long time.

In practical TCM terms, yam is prized for three traditional actions:

  • Spleen support — it is traditionally used to strengthen digestion, ease fatigue, and firm loose stools.
  • Lung nourishment — it is believed to moisten the Lung and support easy breathing, especially in dry weather.
  • Kidney grounding — it is said to support the Kidney's role in stamina and healthy aging.

What makes yam exceptional among tonics is its mildness. It is often the first "reinforcing" food given to children, the elderly, and people recovering from illness because it does not overheat or congest the body the way stronger tonics can.

Chicken: The Warm Builder

Chicken is classified in TCM as warm in nature and sweet in flavor, entering the Spleen and Stomach meridians. It is traditionally regarded as a Qi and Blood tonic — a food that builds vitality, warms the middle burner (the digestive core of the body), and helps the body recover its strength.

The classic Chinese way to prepare chicken for medicinal purposes is as a long-simmered soup. Gentle, prolonged heat slowly draws minerals and gelatin from the bones into the broth, producing a nourishing liquid that TCM describes as "easy to transform into Qi and Blood" — in other words, a food that is deeply restorative yet gentle on the digestive system.

Why This Soup Is Traditionally Recommended

In TCM theory, the Spleen is responsible for transforming food into Qi (vital energy) and Blood. When the Spleen is strong, digestion is smooth, energy is steady, and the muscles feel supported. The Lung governs breath and, together with the Spleen, is said to protect the body's "defensive Qi" — the protective layer that helps ward off seasonal imbalances.

This is why chicken and Chinese yam soup is classically offered in situations like:

  • After illness or surgery — to help rebuild strength gently.
  • During seasonal changes — especially moving from autumn into winter, when the Lung needs extra moisture and the body welcomes warmth.
  • For tired digestion — when food feels heavy and energy dips after meals.
  • For dry coughs and scratchy throats — the yam's moistening nature may support the Lung during dry spells.

As with all TCM food therapy, these are traditional beliefs and gentle supports, not medical claims. The soup is best enjoyed as part of an overall balanced, seasonal diet.

The Recipe: Chicken and Chinese Yam Soup

This recipe serves 4-6 people and takes about 2 hours from stovetop to bowl. It is intentionally simple — the ingredients are few, and each one earns its place.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken (about 1.5 kg / 3.3 lb), or 4-6 bone-in chicken thighs
  • 400 g / 14 oz fresh Chinese yam (山药), peeled and cut into thick slices
  • 3 slices fresh ginger (about 5 cm / 2 inches total)
  • 4-5 red dates (jujube), pitted
  • 1 small handful goji berries (about 15 g / 0.5 oz)
  • 1.5 L / 6 cups water
  • Salt to taste

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Prepare the chicken. Rinse the chicken or thighs under cold water. If using a whole chicken, remove any excess fat and the tail. Blanching is optional in TCM home cooking — if you prefer a cleaner broth, put the chicken in cold water, bring to a boil for 3 minutes, then drain and rinse. Otherwise, start directly with fresh water.
  2. Start the broth. Place the chicken in a large pot with the ginger slices and red dates. Add the water and bring to a boil over medium-high heat. Skim off any foam that rises to the surface — this keeps the broth clear and mild.
  3. Simmer low and slow. Reduce the heat to low, cover, and simmer gently for about 1 hour and 15 minutes. The surface should barely tremble; a rolling boil would make the broth cloudy and the chicken tough.
  4. Add the yam. Add the sliced Chinese yam and continue simmering for another 25-30 minutes, until the yam is tender but still holds its shape.
  5. Finish with goji. Add the goji berries in the final 5 minutes — TCM tradition adds goji late so its brightening energy and sweet flavor stay fresh.
  6. Season and serve. Season with salt just before serving. Ladle the soup into bowls, including some chicken and yam in each. Enjoy warm.

Three Variations

  • Qi-building version: Add 10 g astragalus (黄芪) and 2 slices of dried codonopsis (党参) with the chicken. Strain them out before serving. This version is traditionally chosen when energy feels especially depleted.
  • Lung-moistening version: Add half a sliced fresh pear or 15 g of dried snow fungus (银耳, soaked) along with the yam. This is a favorite during dry autumn days.
  • Kidney-supporting version: Add 30 g of soaked lotus seeds and a handful of walnuts in the last 30 minutes. This variation is traditionally enjoyed for its grounding, strengthening character.

Who Should Be Cautious

Because chicken and Chinese yam soup is gentle and balanced, most people tolerate it well. Still, TCM tradition and common sense suggest a few cautions:

  • During acute illness: If you have a fever, sore throat, or thick yellow phlegm, many TCM practitioners recommend postponing rich tonifying soups until the acute stage passes.
  • Digestive congestion: If you feel bloated, have a heavy sensation in the stomach, or are prone to greasy tongue coating, start with a smaller bowl and see how your body responds.
  • Skin reactions: A small number of people experience itching when handling raw Chinese yam sap. Wear gloves while peeling, or rinse the peeled pieces well before cooking.
  • Medication interactions: If you are on blood-thinning medication, speak with your healthcare provider before regularly consuming goji berries.

How to Buy and Store the Ingredients

Choose Chinese yam that feels heavy and firm, with smooth, unbroken skin. Store it unpeeled in a cool, dry place or the refrigerator for up to two weeks. Once peeled, it turns brown quickly — keep it in cold water with a little vinegar if you are not cooking immediately. For the chicken, look for free-range or pasture-raised birds if available; many cooks feel they produce a richer broth.

Leftover soup keeps for up to 3 days in the refrigerator and freezes well for up to 2 months. Reheat gently on the stove rather than boiling hard, to preserve the delicate texture of the yam.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use dried Chinese yam instead of fresh?

Yes. Dried yam slices (干山药) are a common pantry staple in TCM cooking. Use about 100 g (3.5 oz) and add them at the same time as the chicken — they need the full simmering time to rehydrate and release their nourishing character.

Should I peel the Chinese yam?

Yes, the skin is tough and not pleasant to eat. Use a vegetable peeler or paring knife, and consider wearing gloves — the raw sap can irritate skin in sensitive people.

Is this soup suitable for children?

Yes. Chinese yam is traditionally regarded as one of the most child-friendly tonics in TCM, and the mild broth suits most children over one year old. Reduce the salt and let the natural sweetness of the dates and yam carry the flavor.

What is the best time of day to drink it?

TCM tradition suggests enjoying warm, strengthening soups at lunch or dinner when the digestive fire is at its daytime peak. A small bowl in the morning is also a gentle way to start the day in cooler seasons.
