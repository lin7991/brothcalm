Perilla Leaf Tea (紫苏叶茶): Warming Relief for Cold and Stagnation

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 5 min read

When the first chill of autumn arrives, or after a long day when your stomach feels tight and your shoulders refuse to relax, a cup of perilla leaf tea can be remarkably comforting. Perilla leaf — known as 紫苏叶 (zǐ sū yè) in Chinese — is one of the gentlest warming herbs in the Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) pantry. It is not a dramatic remedy. It is a quiet one: a cup that helps the body let go of cold and tension, and that has been brewed in Chinese kitchens for centuries.

In this guide, we explore what makes perilla leaf special in TCM, how to brew it properly, and when it is traditionally believed to help most.

What Is Perilla Leaf?

Perilla (Perilla frutescens) is a member of the mint family, and you may already know it by other names: shiso in Japanese cooking, where its green and purple leaves garnish sushi and salads, or deulkkae in Korean cuisine. In China, the purple-leafed variety has been cultivated for both food and medicine for over a thousand years.

In TCM, the plant is used in three distinct parts, each with its own traditional role:

  • Perilla leaf (紫苏叶, zǐ sū yè) — releases the exterior, moves qi, and harmonizes the stomach. This is the part we brew in tea.
  • Perilla stem (紫苏梗, zǐ sū gěng) — moves qi and is traditionally used in some formulas for chest tightness and pregnancy-related discomfort.
  • Perilla seed (紫苏子, zǐ sū zǐ) — moistens the lungs and is traditionally used for coughs with phlegm.

Classic texts, including the Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》), describe the leaf as able to "release the muscles and disperse wind-cold, move qi and widen the middle." That dense classical phrase is worth unpacking, because it explains almost everything perilla tea is used for today.

TCM Properties of Perilla Leaf

In the language of food energetics, perilla leaf is described like this:

  • Nature: Warm (温)
  • Flavor: Pungent (辛)
  • Meridians: Lung and Spleen

A warm, pungent herb that enters the Lung and Spleen channels has a clear traditional profile. Pungent flavors "move" — they encourage circulation and dispersal. Warmth counteracts cold. Together, these qualities make perilla leaf a classic remedy for wind-cold (风寒), the TCM pattern behind the early stages of a cold when you feel chilly, a little achy, and your nose is stuffy but your throat is not yet sore.

Traditional Uses in TCM

Releasing Wind-Cold

At the very beginning of a cold — when you feel a draft on your neck and a shiver that will not settle — perilla leaf is traditionally one of the first herbs reached for. It is considered milder than stronger diaphoretics such as ephedra, which makes it suitable for people who prefer a gentler approach, including children and the elderly. In folk practice, perilla leaves are often simmered with ginger and scallion whites for a warming cup that encourages a light sweat, which TCM views as the body's way of "releasing the exterior."

Harmonizing the Stomach and Calming Nausea

Perilla leaf's second famous role is in the stomach. Because it enters the Spleen channel and moves qi, it is traditionally used when digestion feels stuck: queasiness, a feeling of food sitting heavily, mild bloating, or the specific nausea that some people feel when they are cold. It is frequently paired with tangerine peel (陈皮, chén pí) in formulas designed to dry dampness and get qi moving again — a combination that appears in the classic formula Xiang Su San (香苏散) family.

Resolving Fish and Crab "Poison"

One of the most beloved kitchen traditions involving perilla is its use alongside seafood. The Compendium of Materia Medica records that perilla leaf "resolves the toxicity of fish and crab" (解鱼蟹毒). In practice, this means cooks traditionally add a handful of fresh perilla leaves to crab, shellfish, or rich fish dishes. The belief is that perilla helps the stomach handle heavy, cold-natured seafood, reducing the bloating or queasy feeling some people get afterward. It is also why perilla leaf tea makes a sensible after-dinner cup when a seafood feast is on the menu.

How to Make Perilla Leaf Tea

Perilla leaf tea is simple, and the dried leaf is widely available in Chinese herb shops, Asian grocery stores, or online. Here is the basic recipe:

Basic Perilla Leaf Tea

  • Dried perilla leaf: 3–6 g (about 1–2 teaspoons)
  • Hot water: 300 ml (about 1¼ cups)
  • Steep: 5–10 minutes with a lid on

Pour the hot water over the leaves, cover the cup to trap the aromatic oils, and let it steep. The tea has a pleasant, herbaceous, slightly minty aroma with a gentle warmth. Strain and drink while still warm. You can brew the leaves twice; the second steep will be lighter.

Warming Perilla-Ginger Tea

When you feel cold and a little queasy, combine perilla with ginger:

  • 3 g dried perilla leaf
  • 3 thin slices fresh ginger
  • 1–2 red dates (jujube), optional, for sweetness
  • 300 ml hot water, steep 10 minutes

Ginger adds its own warming, stomach-settling qualities, making this the go-to cup for chilly, nauseous moments. Sip it slowly.

Perilla and Tangerine Peel Digestive Tea

For bloating and a heavy, stuck feeling after a meal:

  • 3 g dried perilla leaf
  • 1 small piece dried tangerine peel (about 1 g)
  • 300 ml hot water, steep 10 minutes

This pairing is straight out of the TCM tradition of moving qi and harmonizing the middle, and it is one of the most pleasant ways to end a heavier meal.

Honey-Perilla Soothing Tea

If your throat feels scratchy, let the tea cool to drinking temperature and stir in a teaspoon of honey. Do not add honey to boiling water — gentle warmth preserves both flavor and the traditional belief in its soothing qualities.

When to Drink Perilla Leaf Tea

Perilla leaf tea is traditionally considered most helpful in these moments:

  • Early wind-cold: the first shivers, stuffy nose, and achiness of a cold, before it settles into the chest.
  • Cold stomach: queasiness, mild bloating, or a "cold knot" feeling in the belly, especially on chilly days.
  • After seafood: a digestive-friendly follow-up to crab, shrimp, or rich fish meals.
  • Stagnant qi: that tight, sighing feeling in the chest when stress makes digestion sluggish.

Because it is warming and moving, perilla tea suits people who tend to feel cold, who have sluggish digestion, or who catch colds easily. People who run hot — always warm, prone to sore throats and heat rashes — will usually find cooler herbs more comfortable.

Precautions and Who Should Be Careful

Perilla leaf is generally gentle, but it is still a herb with a direction of action, and a few groups should be cautious:

  • Heat signs: If you have a high fever, sore throat, yellow phlegm, or strong thirst, warming herbs are traditionally avoided. Perilla is not for wind-heat colds.
  • Excessive sweating: People who sweat very easily or have yang deficiency patterns should use warming diaphoretic herbs sparingly.
  • Yin deficiency with heat: Those with dry mouth, night sweats, and a tendency to feel hot inside should avoid long-term use.
  • Pregnancy and nursing: Perilla appears in some traditional pregnancy formulas, but herbs affect everyone differently. Consult a qualified TCM practitioner or your doctor first.
  • Long-term daily use: Traditional usage favors short courses of a few days, not months of daily drinking. If you notice dryness, restlessness, or discomfort, stop and reconsider.

As with all herbal teas, perilla leaf is a complementary food-therapy practice. It is not a treatment for any disease, and it should never replace professional medical care. If symptoms are severe, persistent, or worsening, see a healthcare provider.

Next time a chill creeps in or your stomach feels tight, consider reaching for perilla leaf. One warm, aromatic cup — a small habit with a thousand years of tradition behind it.
