Silkie Chicken Soup (Wu Ji Tang) — The Ultimate Blood and Qi Tonic

🥘 Recipe · 8 min read

What Is Silkie Chicken Soup?

Silkie chicken soup — known in Chinese as wu ji tang (乌鸡汤) — is one of the most treasured restorative dishes in Traditional Chinese Medicine. For centuries, the black-boned silkie chicken has been simmered into a rich, golden broth with herbs like astragalus, dang gui, red dates, and goji berries, creating what many call the ultimate blood and qi tonic.

Unlike the white chickens common in Western kitchens, the silkie has black skin, dark bones, and snow-white feathers. In TCM, this unusual appearance is not a curiosity — it is a sign of its special nature. The dark color is traditionally associated with the kidney system and with deep, blood-level nourishment.

If you are recovering from illness, adjusting after childbirth, or simply feeling drained and depleted, wu ji tang is the classic bowl to turn to. In this guide, we will look at its TCM properties, its traditional uses, and a complete step-by-step recipe you can make at home.

TCM Properties of Silkie Chicken

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, every food has a nature, a flavor, and a set of channels it travels to. Silkie chicken is no exception.

Nature, Flavor, and Meridians

Silkie chicken is considered neutral to mildly warm in nature, with a sweet flavor. It is traditionally said to enter the Liver and Kidney channels, with many texts also noting its action on the Spleen and Stomach.

  • Neutral-warm nature — gentle enough for regular use, yet warming enough to support yang and circulation
  • Sweet flavor — in TCM, sweet foods tonify, harmonize, and nourish
  • Liver and Kidney channels — the two organ systems most closely tied to blood storage, essence (jing), and deep vitality

Compared to regular chicken, silkie chicken is traditionally viewed as a stronger tonic — deeper acting on blood and essence, while remaining relatively easy to digest.

Traditional Uses in Chinese Medicine

The silkie chicken has a long written history. In the Compendium of Materia Medica (Bencao Gangmu), Li Shizhen recorded that black-boned chicken was traditionally used to support those weakened by chronic illness, to nourish deficiency, to aid women's health, and to support recovery after childbirth.

In TCM theory, the soup is most often recommended for two classic patterns:

Blood Deficiency (Xue Xu)

Blood deficiency may show up as a pale complexion, dry hair, brittle nails, dizziness, poor memory, scanty menstruation, or a feeling of being constantly tired. Because silkie chicken is believed to nourish the blood and support the Liver (which stores blood), it has long been a go-to food for this pattern.

Qi Deficiency (Qi Xu)

Qi deficiency often appears as low energy, a weak voice, shortness of breath with light activity, loose appetite, and a tendency to catch colds easily. The sweet, tonifying nature of the chicken — especially when paired with astragalus — is traditionally used to strengthen qi and fortify the Spleen.

Postpartum Recovery

After childbirth, a woman's blood and qi are traditionally considered to be depleted. Across China, silkie chicken soup with dang gui and red dates is one of the most famous confinement dishes, served warm during the first month after delivery to support recovery, milk production, and overall strength.

What to Pair with Silkie Chicken

The magic of wu ji tang lies in the supporting herbs. Each ingredient brings a specific traditional action:

  • Dang gui (Chinese angelica) — the classic blood-nourishing herb, traditionally used to support women's health
  • Astragalus (huang qi) — a renowned qi tonic that is believed to support immunity and energy
  • Red dates (jujube) — sweet and nourishing, traditionally used to support the blood and calm the mind
  • Goji berries — believed to nourish the Liver and Kidneys and support the eyes
  • Chinese yam (shan yao) — a gentle Spleen and Stomach tonic that supports digestion
  • Ginger — warms the middle, helps digestion, and balances the herbs

Silkie Chicken Soup Recipe (Wu Ji Tang)

This classic version balances blood and qi support with a clean, savory broth. It takes about 3 hours from start to finish, most of it hands-off simmering.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole silkie chicken (about 1.2–1.5 kg), cleaned
  • 10 g dried astragalus root (huang qi)
  • 10 g dried Chinese angelica root (dang gui)
  • 8 red dates (jujube)
  • 15 g dried goji berries
  • 3–4 slices fresh ginger
  • 3–4 slices dried Chinese yam
  • 1.5 L water
  • Salt to taste

Instructions

  1. Blanch the chicken. Bring a pot of water to a boil, add the whole silkie chicken, and blanch for 3 minutes. This removes impurities and gives a clearer broth. Rinse and drain.
  2. Combine everything. Place the chicken, astragalus, dang gui, red dates, ginger, and yam in a large soup pot. Dang gui is often tied in a small muslin bag so the soup stays clear.
  3. Add water and bring to a boil. Pour in 1.5 L of water and bring to a boil over high heat. Skim off any foam that rises to the surface.
  4. Simmer low and slow. Reduce the heat to low, cover, and simmer gently for 2 to 2.5 hours. The longer, gentle simmer is what draws the nourishment out of the bones and herbs.
  5. Add goji berries last. Add them in the final 15 minutes so they stay plump and sweet rather than dissolving into the broth.
  6. Season and serve. Season with salt to taste. Serve the soup warm, with the chicken meat either in the bowl or alongside with a light dipping sauce.

Cooking Tips

  • Use a clay pot (sand pot) if you have one — it distributes heat gently and is the traditional choice for tonic soups.
  • Do not rush the simmer. A slow, low heat extracts more from the herbs and bones.
  • Leftovers keep for 2–3 days in the fridge. Reheat gently; the soup may gel slightly when cold, which is normal and a sign of a collagen-rich broth.

Variations

  • Extra blood support: add a handful of longan (dragon eye) for warmth and blood nourishment.
  • Extra kidney support: add a few slices of dried eucommia bark or a small handful of black sesame.
  • Lighter version: reduce the herbs to just red dates, goji, and ginger for a milder everyday soup.
  • With mushrooms: add shiitake or wood ear mushrooms for an umami-rich, immunity-supporting twist.

Who Should Drink It and When

Silkie chicken soup is traditionally considered a restorative food, best enjoyed:

  • During postpartum recovery, especially in the first month after childbirth
  • After illness or surgery, when the body is rebuilding
  • During periods of deep fatigue or burnout
  • In colder months, as a warming, grounding meal
  • For anyone with signs of blood or qi deficiency (see above)

A common traditional rhythm is once or twice a week for general nourishment, or more frequently under a practitioner's guidance during recovery.

Precautions and Contraindications

While wu ji tang is a gentle tonic for most people, it is not for everyone:

  • Excess heat signs: if you have a fever, sore throat, red tongue, or strong thirst, a rich tonifying soup may be too warming. Wait until the heat clears.
  • Active infections: during a cold or flu with heat symptoms, it is traditionally better to keep meals light and avoid strong tonics.
  • Poor digestion: if you feel bloated or heavy after rich soups, start with a smaller bowl and fewer herbs.
  • Pregnancy: herbs such as dang gui are traditionally used with caution during pregnancy. Always consult your healthcare provider first.
  • Chronic conditions: if you manage a chronic illness, talk to your doctor or a qualified TCM practitioner before making this soup a regular habit.
