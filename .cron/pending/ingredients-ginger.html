Ginger (姜) — The Warming Healer

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 8 min read

Few ingredients are as beloved in the Chinese kitchen as ginger. Known in Chinese as sheng jiang (生姜), this knobby root sits on nearly every stove — sliced into soups, steeped into teas, and minced into stir-fries. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), ginger is more than a flavoring: it is one of the most widely used warming herbs, traditionally employed to chase away cold, settle an upset stomach, and support healthy digestion.

For anyone exploring food therapy, ginger is an ideal place to start. It is affordable, easy to find, and remarkably versatile. Whether you feel a chill creeping in on a rainy day or your stomach is unsettled after a heavy meal, a slice of fresh ginger is believed to offer gentle, comforting support. This guide explores how ginger is understood in TCM, how to choose and store it, and simple ways to bring more of its warmth into your daily meals.

What Is Ginger in TCM?

In TCM theory, every food carries an energetic signature — a temperature, a flavor, and a set of organ channels it acts upon. Ginger is one of the most clearly understood examples of a warming food.

Nature, flavor, and channels

TCM texts describe fresh ginger as warm in nature and pungent in flavor. Its warmth is traditionally said to enter three channels:

  • Lung (肺) — ginger is believed to support the lungs and help disperse cold that may settle in the chest, which is why ginger tea is a classic comfort during early chills and seasonal sniffles.
  • Spleen (脾) — the spleen is the center of digestion in TCM. Ginger is traditionally used to warm the stomach and spleen, helping the body transform food into nourishment.
  • Stomach (胃) — ginger is often described as harmonizing the stomach and settling nausea, a use that matches its reputation across many cultures as a travel-sickness remedy.

Because it is warming and dispersing, fresh ginger is traditionally reserved for patterns of cold — think chills, cold hands and feet, watery stools, or a pale tongue — rather than patterns of heat.

Traditional Uses of Ginger

Ginger has been part of Chinese medicine for more than two thousand years. The Shennong Bencao Jing (《神农本草经》), one of the oldest Chinese materia medica texts, lists ginger among its medicinal substances, and later classics such as the Bencao Gangmu (《本草纲目》) describe it as warming the middle and dispersing cold.

In classical formulas, ginger often appears as a guiding or harmonizing herb. One famous example is Gui Zhi Tang (桂枝汤, Cinnamon Twig Decoction), where fresh ginger works alongside cinnamon twig and jujube dates. In that formula, ginger is traditionally understood to support the protective energy of the body and help the other herbs work together without irritating the stomach.

What ginger is traditionally used for

  • Warming the stomach after cold foods or weather — a slice of ginger in tea is believed to help the body return to comfortable warmth.
  • Supporting digestion — ginger may support the Spleen and Stomach in transforming rich or heavy meals, which is why many Chinese soups begin with a few slices of ginger.
  • Settling nausea — ginger is traditionally used for morning queasiness, motion sickness, and upset stomachs. Many cultures have used ginger this way for centuries.
  • Dispersing exterior cold — in early-stage chills, a hot bowl of ginger tea with scallions is a classic home remedy believed to encourage gentle sweating and comfort.

How to Choose and Store Ginger

Good ginger makes a real difference, both in flavor and in the strength of its warming qualities.

Choosing fresh ginger

  • Look for smooth, taut skin — wrinkled or soft ginger is older and less aromatic.
  • Check for a spicy aroma — fresh ginger should smell pungent and clean when you break a piece.
  • Prefer firm, heavy roots — heavy for its size means juicy flesh, which matters for tea and soups.
  • Avoid mold or damp patches — these can signal age or poor storage.

Storing ginger

Whole fresh ginger keeps best in a cool, dry, dark place — a paper bag in the pantry works well for about a week. For longer storage, keep it in the refrigerator's crisper drawer, where it can last several weeks. Peeled or sliced ginger can be frozen in an airtight container and used directly in soups; the texture softens slightly, but the flavor and warmth remain.

How to Cook with Ginger

There are three common forms of ginger in the Chinese kitchen, and each suits different dishes:

  • Fresh slices (sheng jiang) — the most common form for tea and soup. Slice thinly and simmer; the flavor is bright and pungent.
  • Minced or grated — best for stir-fries, marinades, and dipping sauces, where the ginger releases its aroma quickly in hot oil.
  • Dried ginger (gan jiang) — hotter and more concentrated in TCM terms, traditionally used in slow-cooked tonics and warming broths.

Three simple ways to use ginger daily

1. Classic ginger tea. Add 4–5 thin slices of fresh ginger (with a piece of jujube date if you like) to two cups of water. Simmer for 10 minutes, strain, and sip warm. A spoonful of honey can be added once the tea has cooled slightly.

2. Ginger in congee. Add a few slices of ginger to a pot of rice congee along with scallion whites. This is a traditional breakfast for cool mornings and is believed to be gentle on the stomach.

3. Ginger in soup. Almost every Chinese meat soup — from chicken soup to bone broth — starts by sautéing a few ginger slices in a dry pan or adding them to the simmering pot. Ginger is believed to warm the broth and help balance rich, heavy ingredients.

Precautions and Who Should Be Cautious

Ginger is a food, and for most people it is safe in normal culinary amounts. However, because it is warming and slightly stimulating, TCM tradition and modern guidance both suggest caution in a few situations:

  • Heat patterns — people with signs of excess heat (a red tongue, sore throat, yellow phlegm, strong thirst, or skin flare-ups) may find large amounts of ginger too drying or stimulating.
  • Pregnancy — small amounts in food are generally fine, and ginger is traditionally used for morning nausea, but large supplemental doses should be discussed with a healthcare provider.
  • Blood-thinning medication — ginger may interact with anticoagulants. If you take these medicines, talk to your doctor before eating large amounts.
  • Surgery — because of its mild blood-thinning effect, some healthcare providers recommend pausing concentrated ginger supplements before procedures.

As always in food therapy, the guiding principle is balance: a few slices of ginger in your daily cooking is a gentle practice, while large concentrated amounts deserve more thought.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is fresh ginger different from dried ginger in TCM?

Yes. TCM distinguishes between sheng jiang (fresh ginger), which is warm and dispersing, and gan jiang (dried ginger), which is hotter and more deeply warming. Dried ginger is traditionally used in slow tonics for internal cold, while fresh ginger suits daily cooking and mild stomach support.

Can ginger help with motion sickness?

Ginger is one of the most studied traditional remedies for nausea, including motion sickness. In TCM, it is traditionally used to settle an unsettled stomach. Sipping ginger tea before or during travel may offer gentle support, though everyone responds differently.

Does cooking destroy ginger's benefits?

Cooking softens ginger's pungency but does not remove its warming character — in fact, TCM recipes almost always use cooked ginger. For the most aromatic tea, simmer gently for 10 minutes rather than boiling hard.

What is the best time of day to drink ginger tea?

Traditionally, ginger tea suits mornings and cool evenings, or right after a meal to support digestion. Many TCM practitioners suggest avoiding very large amounts late at night, since its warming, stimulating nature may keep some people alert.
