Boxthorn Leaf and Pork Liver Soup — Eyesight Nourishing Broth

🥘 Recipe · 8 min read

In the Cantonese kitchen, few bowls say "take care of yourself" like a pot of boxthorn leaf and pork liver soup (枸杞叶猪肝汤, gou qi ye zhu gan tang). It is a classic home-style soup — quick to make, deeply savory, and traditionally linked to two things that modern life taxes constantly: the eyes and the blood.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the health of the eyes is closely tied to the liver. The liver is said to store blood, and the eyes are described as the "window" of the liver — when liver blood is well nourished, vision is traditionally believed to stay clear and comfortable. This soup pairs the leafy greens of the goji plant with pork liver, bringing together two ingredients that have been used in Chinese food therapy for this purpose for centuries.

Why This Soup Matters in TCM

The logic behind this soup is simple and elegant: it combines a cooling leaf with a warming organ meat, so the two balance each other. Boxthorn leaves are traditionally considered slightly cool in nature — helpful for clearing what TCM calls "liver heat," which can show up as red, dry, or tired eyes and irritability. Pork liver is warming and deeply nourishing, classically associated with replenishing liver blood.

People traditionally reach for this soup when they notice:

  • Tired, dry, or strained eyes after long days of reading or screen work
  • A pale complexion, dizziness, or easy fatigue — signs TCM associates with blood deficiency
  • Feeling rundown after illness, exams, or heavy work
  • Mild irritability or a "hot" feeling in the eyes

As always in TCM food therapy, this is a tradition of nourishment, not a treatment. It is believed to support the body's balance — it does not replace eye exams, glasses, or medical care.

TCM Properties of Boxthorn Leaves (Gou Qi Ye)

Boxthorn leaves are the tender leaves of the goji plant — the same plant that gives us goji berries. In TCM texts they are sometimes called tian jing cao (天精草), a name that hints at their reputation as a "heavenly essence" herb.

Traditional characteristics

  • Nature: slightly cool
  • Flavor: sweet and bitter
  • Channels: Liver and Kidney
  • Traditional actions: clears liver heat, brightens the eyes, nourishes liver blood, supports yin

Because the leaves are mild and cool, they are traditionally seen as gentle enough for regular meals — especially when balanced with ginger and the warming nature of liver. If you enjoy goji berries, the leaves are their quieter, greener cousin: less sweet, more vegetable-like, and cooked rather than dried.

TCM Properties of Pork Liver

Organ meats hold an honored place in Chinese food therapy, and pork liver is one of the most classic examples.

Traditional characteristics

  • Nature: warm
  • Flavor: sweet and bitter
  • Channel: Liver
  • Traditional actions: nourishes liver blood, benefits the eyes, supports strength and recovery

Modern nutrition offers a complementary view: pork liver is one of the richest natural sources of vitamin A (retinol), along with heme iron, vitamin B12, and riboflavin. Vitamin A is essential for healthy vision, and iron supports the blood — which is a nice parallel to the TCM idea of nourishing liver blood. Some studies suggest that including moderate amounts of organ meat in a varied diet can help prevent iron deficiency, though the research on any single food should always be read in the context of your overall diet.

How to Make Boxthorn Leaf and Pork Liver Soup

This is a fast "rolled" soup (gun tang) — it comes together in about 20 minutes of cooking, unlike the long-simmered broths Cantonese cooking is famous for. The secret is timing: the liver should just turn opaque, and the leaves should still be bright green.

Ingredients

  • 150 g fresh boxthorn leaves (gou qi ye), thick stems removed and rinsed
  • 200 g pork liver, sliced thin
  • 2–3 slices fresh ginger
  • 1 tablespoon Shaoxing wine (optional)
  • 1 handful goji berries (optional)
  • 1.2 L water or light chicken stock
  • Sea salt and white pepper to taste
  • A few drops of sesame oil to finish

Steps

  1. Prep the liver: Slice the pork liver into thin pieces, rinse in cold water, then soak in lightly salted water with a splash of wine for 10–15 minutes. Drain well — this step softens the flavor and removes impurities.
  2. Boil the base: Bring the water or stock to a rolling boil with the ginger slices.
  3. Cook the liver: Add the liver and cook for 1–2 minutes, just until it turns opaque. Overcooked liver turns rubbery and bitter.
  4. Add the leaves: Stir in the boxthorn leaves (and goji berries if using) and cook 1–2 minutes more, until wilted and vivid green.
  5. Season and serve: Add sea salt and a pinch of white pepper, drizzle with sesame oil, and serve hot with rice.

Variations

  • With egg: Crack a whole egg into the soup in the last minute and let it set — a very common Cantonese addition that makes the soup more filling.
  • Lighter version: Swap the pork liver for lean pork slices or firm tofu; the soup is still delicious, though the traditional liver-blood association is specific to the liver.
  • Richer broth: Use chicken stock and add a few red dates (jujube) for extra sweetness.
  • Can't find boxthorn leaves? Fresh spinach is the closest common substitute in texture and color, though the TCM properties differ — seek out boxthorn leaves at Chinese or Asian grocers when you can.

Who This Soup May Suit Best

In TCM food therapy, this soup is traditionally considered most helpful for people with signs of liver blood deficiency or liver heat:

  • Desk workers and students who spend hours in front of screens and notice tired or dry eyes
  • People with a pale complexion, mild dizziness, or fatigue that TCM associates with blood deficiency
  • Anyone recovering from illness or a demanding period and wanting a gentle, nourishing meal
  • Those who feel irritable or "hot-headed" and want a cooling, calming bowl of food

Precautions and Who Should Be Careful

Pork liver is concentrated nutrition, and that means a little goes a long way:

  • Frequency: Enjoy this soup once or twice a week at most. Liver is very high in vitamin A, and regularly eating large amounts can exceed recommended intakes.
  • Pregnancy: Because of its high retinol content, many guidelines suggest limiting liver in early pregnancy. Check with your healthcare provider.
  • Gout and high uric acid: Organ meats are high in purines, so people managing gout may want to choose a lighter version.
  • Iron overload conditions: If you have hemochromatosis or similar conditions, avoid liver entirely.
  • Food safety: Always cook liver thoroughly — it should be opaque all the way through, with no pink center.

Boxthorn leaves are mild, but because they are cool in nature, people with a cold spleen and stomach — loose stools, cold hands, low appetite — should keep the ginger generous and enjoy the soup in moderation.
