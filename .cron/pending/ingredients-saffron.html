Saffron (Xi Hong Hua) — The Precious Blood-Moving Spice

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 6 min read

What Is Saffron?

Saffron — known as xi hong hua (西红花) or fan hong hua in Chinese — is the dried stigma of the Crocus sativus flower. Each flower produces only three tiny red threads, and it can take more than 70,000 flowers to yield a single pound of saffron. That is why it has earned nicknames like "red gold" and why it is the most expensive spice in the world by weight.

Originally from the Mediterranean and Persia, saffron travelled to China along the ancient Silk Road. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), it was welcomed as a precious blood-moving ingredient — one used sparingly, in small pinches, to support circulation, mood, and women's health. Later classical works such as the Bencao Gangmu Shiyi (本草纲目拾遗, 1765) recorded it as an exotic, high-value medicinal material.

TCM has a tradition of "small doses of precious things": ingredients like saffron, deer antler, and bear gallbladder are valued not for quantity but for precision. A few threads of saffron are traditionally considered enough to nudge the body back toward balance.

Saffron in Traditional Chinese Medicine

In TCM theory, every food and herb has a temperature, a flavor, and a set of channels (meridians) it enters. Saffron is traditionally classified as:

  • Nature: slightly cold (微寒)
  • Flavor: sweet and pungent (甘、辛)
  • Channels: Heart and Liver

The sweet flavor is traditionally said to nourish, while the pungent flavor is associated with movement — helping things circulate rather than stagnate. The Heart is known in TCM as the seat of the shen (spirit), and the Liver is the organ most closely linked to the smooth flow of qi and blood. Because saffron enters both, it is traditionally used for two main purposes: moving blood and calming the spirit.

Traditionally Used to Move Blood

In TCM, many discomforts — painful periods, chest tightness, and a sense of heaviness — are understood as blood or qi stagnation. Saffron's pungent, moving nature is traditionally used to quicken blood circulation and resolve stasis. Classical materia medica describe it as working at the blood level, and it has been included in formulas for menstrual pain, irregular menstruation, and postpartum support.

Traditionally Used to Calm the Shen

Saffron is also classified among ingredients that settle and calm. It is believed to help relieve mental constraint and soothe the spirit, which is why it appears in traditional blends for mood support and restlessness. Modern research has begun to look at saffron for mood and anxiety, and some studies suggest it may support a calm outlook — though larger, well-designed trials are still needed before firm conclusions can be drawn.

Traditional Uses of Saffron

  • Women's health: Traditionally used to support regular menstruation and ease menstrual cramping. In TCM practice it is often reserved for patterns of blood stagnation.
  • Mood support: Believed to help lift a low spirit and calm the mind during times of stress or grief.
  • Digestive comfort: In culinary traditions, saffron is added to rice and broths to make meals more fragrant and easier to digest — a gentle way to warm the center without heaviness.
  • Skin: Because it is traditionally seen as cooling, it has been used in small amounts to address heat-related skin irritations.

How to Use Saffron

The golden rule of saffron is that a little goes a long way. Culinary amounts — a pinch of threads — are safe for most people, while medicinal doses should only be used under professional guidance.

Saffron Tea

One of the simplest ways to enjoy saffron is as a tea.

  • Place 3–5 saffron threads in a cup.
  • Pour in 200 ml (about 1 cup) of hot, not boiling, water.
  • Steep for 5–10 minutes until the water turns golden.
  • Drink warm, once per day, ideally in the morning.

For flavor, you can add a little honey or a slice of lemon. Some people like to pair saffron with goji berries or jujube dates for a more nourishing brew — the sweet, nourishing notes of goji balance saffron's moving, slightly cool character.

Saffron in Cooking

Saffron pairs beautifully with rice, congee, and gentle soups. A pinch of threads soaked in warm water can be added to:

  • Congee for a golden, aromatic breakfast
  • Chicken or vegetable broth
  • Milk-based drinks such as warm golden milk
  • Steamed rice for a festive, golden color

Pairing Saffron with Other Foods

In TCM food therapy, ingredients are rarely used alone — they are combined to balance one another. Saffron's moving, slightly cool nature pairs well with sweet, nourishing partners such as goji berries, jujube dates, and honey, which are traditionally used to support the blood and calm the spirit. A classic combination is saffron with goji berries steeped as a tea: the bright, moving quality of saffron is complemented by the gentle, nourishing sweetness of goji. For a more comforting evening drink, add warm milk — its sweet, moistening character rounds out saffron's aroma and makes the cup feel complete.

How Much Saffron Is Safe?

For everyday culinary use, 3–5 threads (about 10–30 mg) per serving is plenty. In traditional herbal practice, larger amounts — up to 1–3 grams per day — have been used in formulas, but only under supervision. Very large doses (more than 5 g) are considered toxic, so treat saffron with respect: it is a spice, not a snack.

How to Choose and Store Saffron

Because saffron is expensive, adulteration is common. Real saffron threads are deep red with a slightly orange tip, have a strong honey-like aroma, and stain water slowly and evenly. Powder can be more easily mixed with fillers such as turmeric, so whole threads are usually a safer choice.

Store saffron in an airtight container, away from light and heat. Kept well, it can stay aromatic for up to two years — a small investment that lasts a long time.

Precautions and Who Should Avoid It

  • Pregnancy: In TCM, saffron has traditionally been used to promote menstruation, so it is generally avoided during pregnancy.
  • Heavy menstrual flow: Because it moves blood, people with very heavy periods should use it with caution.
  • Bleeding disorders: Anyone with clotting issues, or who is about to have surgery, should avoid concentrated amounts.
  • Blood-thinning medication: If you take anticoagulants, talk to your doctor before using saffron regularly.

As with all foods, start with small amounts and observe how your body responds. If you are managing a chronic condition, consult a qualified practitioner first.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does saffron taste like?

Saffron has a subtle, floral, honey-like flavor with a faint hint of bitterness. It is not spicy like most "spices" — its main gift is a beautiful golden color and a delicate aroma.

What is the difference between saffron and safflower (hong hua)?

Safflower (hong hua, 红花) is a completely different plant — an inexpensive orange flower often used in TCM as a blood-moving herb. Saffron (xi hong hua, 西红花) comes from the crocus and is far more precious. In Chinese, the word "hong hua" literally means "red flower," which is why the two are often confused.

Can I take saffron every day?

In small culinary amounts (a few threads), most people can enjoy saffron regularly. Larger, more medicinal amounts should be intermittent and used under guidance.

Why is saffron so expensive?

Each crocus flower gives only three stigmas, and they must be hand-picked at dawn. About 150 flowers are needed for a single gram of dried saffron.
