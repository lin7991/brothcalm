Longan and Jujube Tea — Sweet Blood-Building Comfort

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 5 min read

The Sweetest Pairing in Chinese Food Therapy

Some tea recipes are about caffeine and a morning jolt. Longan and jujube tea is the opposite — it is a warm, honeyed cup that is traditionally enjoyed at the end of a long day, when you want to slow down rather than speed up. In Chinese food therapy (食疗), this pairing is one of the most beloved home brews: two pantry staples, a pot of water, and twenty minutes of gentle simmering.

In TCM theory, the tea is believed to do two quiet jobs at once. Dried longan is traditionally associated with nourishing the heart and calming the shen (spirit), while jujube dates are classically used to support the spleen and stomach and to help the body build blood. Together, they are often recommended for people who feel drained, sleep restlessly, or simply need a little sweetness that actually does something.

Longan: The Fruit That Calms the Shen

Dried longan — the golden, chewy flesh of the longan fruit — is known in Chinese as long yan rou (龙眼肉). In TCM, its nature is considered sweet and warm, and it is traditionally said to enter the heart and spleen channels. The classic Materia Medica compiled by Li Shizhen, the Ben Cao Gang Mu, describes longan as a fruit that is believed to nourish the heart and soothe the spirit.

Because the heart is said to house the shen, or the spirit of the mind, TCM practitioners have long associated heart blood with emotional calm. When heart blood is considered sufficient, a person is traditionally thought to sleep soundly, remember clearly, and feel steady. When it is felt to be depleted — after long hours of work, worry, or recovery from illness — the same tradition describes symptoms like scattered sleep, a racing mind, forgetfulness, and a pale complexion. This is where longan is believed to lend its support.

Modern wellness writers sometimes call longan "nature's tranquilizer." That is an overstatement — in TCM it is simply a warming, blood-nourishing fruit. But its reputation as a calming food is very old, and it remains a favorite in teas and porridges for winding down.

Jujube: The Peacemaker of the Pantry

Jujube dates, or hong zao (红枣), are the dried red dates that appear in almost every TCM tonic. Their nature is also sweet and warm, and they are traditionally said to nourish the spleen and stomach, support qi, and help generate blood. In classical formulas, jujube is so often added to harmonize other ingredients that herbalists call it the "peacemaker" of the materia medica.

The spleen in TCM is the organ most closely tied to digestion and to turning food into qi and blood. When the spleen is considered strong, energy is steady and the body is believed to produce good blood. Jujube is traditionally used to gently support this process, which is one reason it shows up in everything from congee to goji jujube tea to bone broths.

Between the two, longan leans toward the heart and the mind, while jujube leans toward digestion and vitality. That is why the pairing feels so complete: one cup touches both the body's energy and the mind's rest.

How to Brew Longan and Jujube Tea

The classic version is simple enough to make on a weeknight, and every ingredient is available at a Chinese grocery store or online.

Ingredients

  • 8–10 dried longan (about 1 small handful, or 15–20 g)
  • 4–6 dried jujube dates (about 25–30 g), pitted and sliced
  • 3 cups (700 ml) water
  • Optional: 1 teaspoon goji berries, 1–2 thin slices of fresh ginger, a small piece of rock sugar or brown sugar to taste

Steps

  1. Rinse the longan and jujube under cool water. Slice the jujube open and remove the pits — scoring the flesh helps the sweetness release.
  2. Add everything except the sugar to a small pot with the water.
  3. Bring to a boil, then lower the heat and simmer uncovered for 20–25 minutes, until the liquid turns a deep amber and the dates are soft.
  4. If you like it sweeter, stir in a little rock sugar or brown sugar in the last 5 minutes and let it dissolve.
  5. Strain into a mug and drink warm. The cooked fruit is traditionally eaten too, not thrown away.

Three Easy Variations

  • With goji berries: add 1 teaspoon of goji in the last 5 minutes. The classic goji longan tea is believed to support the liver and eyes as well as the blood.
  • With ginger: two slices of fresh ginger add warmth and are traditionally used when you feel chilled or your digestion feels sluggish.
  • With rose petals: a few dried rose buds make the tea fragrant and are traditionally associated with supporting the liver qi and a calm mood.

For a heartier version, the same two ingredients star in longan date soup, which is often simmered with a soft-boiled egg for extra nourishment.

When to Drink It (and Who It Traditionally Suits)

Because the tea is sweet and warming, it is traditionally enjoyed in the afternoon or evening, rather than first thing in the morning or late at night. A cup in the late afternoon can be a gentle bridge between work and rest; a cup an hour before bed is often paired with the intention of a settled night's sleep.

In TCM tradition, longan and jujube tea is frequently suggested for:

  • People who feel tired but wired — drained in the body, busy in the mind
  • Those with restless or light sleep, especially after stress or long work hours
  • Women around their monthly cycle or during postpartum recovery, when blood is traditionally considered to need rebuilding
  • Students and desk workers who spend the day concentrating
  • Anyone recovering from illness, heavy physical work, or a period of poor eating

If your pattern leans toward blood deficiency — pale lips and nails, fatigue, dizziness, poor memory — the blood deficiency food guide and the anemia food guide cover the wider picture, and this tea is a pleasant daily complement.

Who Should Drink It Less Often

Sweet and warming is wonderful for some people and less suited to others. In TCM, people who tend toward heat — frequent sore throats, mouth ulcers, acne, constipation, a feeling of heat in the body — are traditionally advised to keep this tea occasional rather than daily. The same goes for people with signs of dampness: a heavy feeling, bloating, loose stools, or a greasy tongue coating.

A few practical cautions:

  • Blood sugar: both fruits are naturally high in sugar. If you have diabetes or watch your sugar intake, keep portions small and skip added sweetener.
  • Pregnancy: many cultures use these fruits in small amounts during pregnancy, but always check with your healthcare provider before making herbal teas a daily habit.
  • Quantity: one to two cups a day is the traditional range. More is not better — too much sweet and warming food is believed to create heat over time.
  • Sleep: because the tea is gently stimulating in its sweetness, some people prefer it a few hours before bed rather than right at bedtime.

FAQ

Can I drink longan and jujube tea every day?

For most people, one cup a day is traditionally considered a gentle, pleasant habit. Because both fruits are sweet and warming, people who tend to feel hot, or who notice sore throats, mouth ulcers, or constipation, are traditionally advised to drink it less often or in smaller amounts.

Is longan and jujube tea safe during pregnancy?

In TCM tradition, small amounts of longan and jujube are commonly used in nourishing soups and teas for new mothers, but pregnancy is a special situation. Always check with your healthcare provider before adding herbal teas to your routine while pregnant, and keep portions modest.

Can I use fresh jujube instead of dried red dates?

Yes. Dried red dates have a more concentrated sweetness and are the classic choice in TCM recipes, but fresh jujube can be used in a pinch. Use roughly one and a half times the weight, simmer a little longer, and expect a lighter, fruitier flavor.

Does longan and jujube tea really help with sleep?

In TCM theory, longan is traditionally said to nourish heart blood and calm the shen, which is why it appears in classic formulas for restless sleep and a busy mind. A warm, sweet cup before bed may also simply be a relaxing ritual. It is not a sleep medication, but it is believed to support a settled evening.
