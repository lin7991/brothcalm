Cordyceps (Dong Chong Xia Cao) — The Lung and Kidney Tonic

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 8 min read

In the high-altitude meadows of the Tibetan plateau, a strange and precious organism emerges each spring: a fungus that grows out of a caterpillar it has consumed over winter. The Chinese named it dong chong xia cao (冬虫夏草) — literally "winter worm, summer grass." For centuries, cordyceps has been one of the most treasured tonics in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), traditionally reserved for those who needed deep nourishment of the Lung and Kidney systems.

Today, cordyceps appears in wellness teas, bone broths, and supplement shelves around the world. But before you reach for the nearest capsule, it helps to understand how this remarkable fungus is used in Chinese food therapy — how it is classified, how it is traditionally prepared, and who is best suited to enjoy it.

What Is Cordyceps?

Cordyceps is a genus of parasitic fungi. The most famous species, Cordyceps sinensis (now classified as Ophiocordyceps sinensis), infects ghost moth caterpillars that burrow underground at high altitudes. In spring, a fruiting body sprouts from the caterpillar's head, looking like a small brown blade of grass rising from the soil. Harvesters dig them out by hand — one reason genuine wild cordyceps is among the most expensive medicinal ingredients in the world.

Because wild cordyceps is rare and costly, most people today use Cordyceps militaris, a cultivated cousin that can be grown on grain or rice substrates. In food therapy, cultivated cordyceps militaris is widely accepted as a practical, everyday alternative with a similar traditional profile.

TCM Properties: Nature, Flavor, and Meridians

In TCM theory, cordyceps is classified as:

  • Nature: Warm (甘温)
  • Flavor: Sweet
  • Meridians entered: Lung (肺) and Kidney (肾)

The sweet flavor in TCM is associated with harmonizing and tonifying actions, while a warm nature is traditionally linked to supporting yang energy. Because cordyceps enters both the Lung and Kidney meridians, it occupies a special place in the TCM pharmacopoeia: it is believed to nourish the Lungs — the organ system traditionally associated with breath, the protective qi, and the skin — while also supporting the Kidneys, which in TCM are considered the root of vitality, aging, and constitutional strength.

Classical texts such as the Ben Cao Cong Xin (New Compilation of Materia Medica, 1757) describe cordyceps as beneficial for the Lungs and Kidneys, noting it may support those who are easily fatigued, short of breath, or recovering from prolonged illness. These are traditional descriptions passed down through generations of practice, not modern medical claims.

Traditional Uses of Cordyceps

In Chinese food therapy, cordyceps is used in a few distinct ways:

1. Lung Support and Respiratory Comfort

Because cordyceps is traditionally linked to the Lung meridian, it has long been used in soups for people who feel their breathing is shallow, who tire easily, or who want gentle support during the dry autumn months — a time when TCM holds that the Lungs are especially vulnerable. It is often combined with other moistening ingredients such as snow fungus, pear, or lily bulb.

2. Kidney Nourishment and Vitality

The Kidney system in TCM is associated with stamina, lower back strength, and the body's "deep reserves" of energy. Cordyceps is traditionally considered a tonic for those who feel depleted after long working hours, during recovery, or as part of aging. It is frequently paired with goji berries, which also enter the Kidney meridian, in restorative broths.

3. A Tonic for Gentle Daily Use

Unlike some stronger herbs, cordyceps is regarded as a gentle, balancing tonic. In traditional practice it is not used for acute illness or dramatic change; instead, it is simmered slowly into everyday food — soups, congee, and broths — so its nourishment is absorbed gradually over weeks of steady use.

How to Select and Store Cordyceps

Choosing Quality

  • Whole pieces: Look for intact specimens with a caterpillar body and a complete fruiting body. Color should be a natural tan-to-brown, not artificially bright.
  • Cultivated militaris: For everyday use, cultivated cordyceps militaris is affordable and consistent. It is sold dried, whole, sliced, or powdered.
  • Powder vs. whole: Whole pieces are better for slow soups; powder is convenient for teas and congee but can be harder to source in genuine form.
  • Source: Buy from a reputable herbal supplier with clear origin information. Wild sinensis is heavily counterfeited due to its price.

Storing

Store dried cordyceps in an airtight glass jar in a cool, dry, dark place. Properly stored, it can keep for a year or more. Avoid humidity — mold is the main risk. If you notice any musty smell or discoloration, discard it.

How to Cook with Cordyceps: Soups, Teas, and Congee

Cordyceps Chicken Soup (经典冬虫夏草鸡汤)

This is the classic way cordyceps appears on Chinese family tables — a slow, restorative soup.

Ingredients:

  • 1 whole free-range chicken (or 4–6 chicken thighs), blanched
  • 5–9 g dried cordyceps (about 10–15 whole pieces)
  • 6–8 red dates (jujube), pitted
  • 5 slices fresh ginger
  • 1.5 L water
  • A pinch of sea salt, to finish

Method:

  1. Blanch the chicken in boiling water for 3 minutes, then rinse and drain.
  2. Rinse the cordyceps gently; do not scrub.
  3. Place chicken, cordyceps, red dates, and ginger in a large pot. Add water.
  4. Bring to a boil, then reduce to a gentle simmer. Skim any foam.
  5. Simmer covered for 1.5–2 hours, until the chicken is very tender.
  6. Season lightly with salt and serve warm.

Traditionally this soup is enjoyed 1–2 times per week during the cooler months or during recovery. The broth becomes rich and deeply savory, with a subtle earthy, almost nutty note from the cordyceps.

Cordyceps and Goji Tea

For a simpler everyday preparation, try a warming tea.

  • 3 g dried cordyceps (militaris slices work well)
  • 1 tbsp goji berries
  • 3 red dates, sliced
  • 600 ml water

Simmer all ingredients in a small pot for 15–20 minutes. Strain and sip warm. This makes a gently sweet, earthy tea that is traditionally enjoyed in the morning or early afternoon. You can re-simmer the same herbs once more with fresh water to extract further goodness.

Cordyceps Congee

For those who prefer a soft, easily digested breakfast:

  • 1/2 cup rice (or glutinous rice)
  • 3 g dried cordyceps
  • 5 cups water or light broth
  • 2 slices ginger

Rinse the rice, combine everything in a pot, bring to a boil, then simmer on low for 45–60 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the congee is thick and silky. Season with a little salt and a few chopped scallions. This gentle porridge is a traditional choice for days when you want something nourishing and easy to digest.

Who Should Be Cautious With Cordyceps?

Because cordyceps is traditionally considered warm and tonifying, TCM practitioners generally advise caution in certain situations:

  • Acute illness and fever: During colds, flu, or any active infection with fever, tonics are traditionally set aside until the body recovers.
  • Excess-heat patterns: People who tend toward strong heat signs — such as frequent sore throats, red face, or constipation — may find a warming tonic too stimulating.
  • Children: Cordyceps is traditionally regarded as an adult tonic; it is not typically given to young children.
  • Pregnancy and nursing: Always consult a qualified healthcare provider before using cordyceps during pregnancy or while breastfeeding.
  • Medication interactions: Anyone taking blood-thinning medication, immunosuppressants, or diabetes medication should discuss cordyceps with their doctor before use.

As with any tonic, moderation matters. In food therapy, cordyceps is used in small amounts over time — it is a slow, steady nourisher, not a one-time boost.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I take cordyceps every day?

In traditional practice, small daily amounts (about 3–5 g) in soups or tea are common during a seasonal tonic period, often for a few weeks at a time. Many practitioners suggest a pause after several weeks of daily use. If you are managing a health condition, check with a healthcare provider first.

Is cultivated cordyceps as good as wild?

Wild Cordyceps sinensis is traditionally prized and very expensive, but cultivated Cordyceps militaris is the practical choice for everyday food therapy. Many herbal practitioners use militaris routinely and regard it as sharing a similar traditional profile at a fraction of the cost.

Does cordyceps taste strong?

No — cordyceps has a mild, earthy, slightly nutty flavor. It does not overpower soups or teas, which is one reason it has been so readily incorporated into everyday cooking. Powdered cordyceps is stronger in aroma than whole pieces.

Can cordyceps be combined with other TCM ingredients?

Yes. It is commonly paired with goji berries and red dates for kidney nourishment, with snow fungus or lily bulb for the Lungs, and with astragalus in recovery broths. These combinations follow traditional TCM pairing logic, so it is best to follow an established recipe or consult a practitioner.
