Pumpkin (Nan Gua) — The Sweet Spleen Warmer in TCM

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 6 min read

When the weather turns cool and the market stalls fill with round, golden gourds, TCM kitchens reach for pumpkin — known in Chinese as nán guā (南瓜), literally the "southern gourd." In Traditional Chinese Medicine, pumpkin is regarded as a gentle, sweet, warming food that nourishes the spleen and stomach (脾胃). It appears in congee, soups, stews, and steamed dishes across China, valued as much for its comforting flavor as for its traditionally believed benefits to digestion and energy.

This guide explores pumpkin through the lens of TCM food therapy: its nature and flavor, traditional uses, how to choose and store it, and simple ways to bring more of it into your kitchen. Whether you are new to TCM or a longtime practitioner of food therapy, pumpkin is an easy place to start — mild in character, versatile in the kitchen, and gentle on the stomach.

Pumpkin's TCM Properties: Sweet, Warming, and Grounding

In TCM theory, every food carries a nature (性), a flavor (味), and a set of meridians (归经) it acts upon. Pumpkin is described as:

  • Nature: Warm (温)
  • Flavor: Sweet (甘)
  • Meridians: Spleen and stomach (脾、胃经)

The sweet flavor in TCM is associated with the spleen and stomach — the organs responsible for transforming food into qi (vital energy) and blood. Sweet foods are traditionally said to harmonize the middle (和中), support digestion, and ease tension in the digestive system. Pumpkin's warm nature makes it a natural choice for cooler months, when digestive warmth runs low and many people notice sluggish digestion.

Why Pumpkin Is Called a "Spleen Warmer"

In TCM, the spleen (脾) is not just a physical organ — it represents the whole digestive-transport system. When spleen qi is strong, appetite is good, energy is steady, and digestion is smooth. When it is weak, people may feel tired after meals, experience bloating, or notice loose stools. Pumpkin is traditionally recommended as a mild, nourishing spleen tonic — sweet enough to be palatable, warm enough to support digestion, yet gentle enough for daily use.

Traditional Uses of Pumpkin in Chinese Medicine

Pumpkin has a long history in Chinese food therapy. The Ming dynasty text Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》) by Li Shizhen describes pumpkin as sweet in flavor and warm in nature, traditionally used to support the spleen and stomach. Later texts, including the Qing dynasty Bencao Congxin (《本草从新》), echo these themes, noting pumpkin's traditional use in supporting digestion and supplementing qi.

Traditionally, pumpkin has been used in food therapy to:

  • Strengthen the spleen and stomach — supporting healthy appetite and smooth digestion
  • Supplement qi — providing steady, gentle energy, especially during fatigue
  • Transform phlegm and soothe coughs — a folk use in which pumpkin is simmered into soups for the respiratory system
  • Support blood health — in some traditions, pumpkin is combined with jujube dates to nourish blood

These uses come from a long folk tradition; in modern TCM practice, pumpkin is most often recommended as a daily nourishing food rather than a strong medicinal herb.

Modern Nutrition Notes: What's Inside a Pumpkin

While TCM looks at pumpkin through its energetics, modern nutrition adds another layer. Pumpkin is rich in:

  • Beta-carotene — a precursor to vitamin A, which supports eye health and skin
  • Fiber — supporting healthy digestion and a feeling of fullness
  • Potassium — helping the body maintain fluid balance
  • Vitamins C and E — antioxidants believed to support the immune system

Some studies suggest pumpkin may help support blood sugar balance thanks to its fiber and certain polysaccharides, though it is still a carbohydrate-dense food and should be eaten in sensible portions. As always, these modern notes complement, rather than replace, TCM's traditional understanding.

Pumpkin as a Seasonal Autumn Food

In TCM's seasonal eating framework, each season calls for foods that support the corresponding organ. Autumn is associated with the metal element, the lungs, and dryness. Warm, sweet foods like pumpkin are traditionally favored in early autumn because they nurture the spleen and stomach, helping the body build qi for the colder months ahead — a folk concept often summed up as "supplementing in autumn." Pumpkin's soft, moist texture also suits a season when the body is said to need gentle nourishment.

How to Choose and Store Pumpkin

Choosing the Best Pumpkin

  • Look for weight: a heavy pumpkin feels dense and ripe
  • Check the skin: hard, dull skin (not shiny) indicates ripeness
  • Tap it: a hollow sound suggests mature, sweet flesh
  • Prefer whole: whole pumpkins keep much longer than cut pieces

Storing Pumpkin

  • Whole: store in a cool, dry, dark place for several weeks
  • Cut: wrap tightly and refrigerate, using within 5–7 days
  • Cooked: refrigerate for up to 4 days, or freeze for longer storage

Simple Ways to Cook with Pumpkin

Pumpkin is one of the easiest ingredients to love — it needs little more than heat to become sweet and silky.

Pumpkin Congee (南瓜粥)

The classic TCM preparation. Simmer diced pumpkin with rice and water (about 1:6 rice-to-water) for 30–40 minutes until soft, then mash lightly. Traditionally eaten warm as breakfast, it is believed to be especially gentle on the spleen and stomach.

Steamed Pumpkin (蒸南瓜)

Cut pumpkin into wedges, remove the seeds, and steam for 15–20 minutes until tender. A drizzle of honey or a sprinkle of cinnamon enhances the natural sweetness. This is a popular, low-effort way to enjoy pumpkin's warming nature.

Pumpkin and Jujube Soup (南瓜红枣汤)

Combine diced pumpkin with 6–8 red jujube dates in water, simmer for 20 minutes, and finish with a small amount of rock sugar. This sweet soup pairs two classic blood- and spleen-nourishing ingredients and is traditionally enjoyed in cooler weather.

Roasted Pumpkin Seeds

Don't throw away the seeds. Rinse them, dry them, toss with a pinch of salt, and roast at 160°C (320°F) for 15–20 minutes until golden. Pumpkin seeds are traditionally appreciated for their nutrients and make a satisfying, warming snack for cooler evenings.

Who Should Be Careful with Pumpkin?

Pumpkin is a mild food, but TCM food therapy always considers the individual:

  • People with damp-heat conditions — those with heaviness, bloating, or a greasy tongue coating may find sweet, warming foods too rich and should moderate their intake
  • People with excess heat or fever — warming foods may not be ideal during acute heat conditions
  • People watching blood sugar — pumpkin is carbohydrate-dense; portion control is sensible, and anyone managing diabetes should speak with a healthcare provider
  • Over-consumption note — eating very large amounts of pumpkin can turn the skin slightly yellow (carotenemia); this is harmless and fades when intake is reduced

If you are unsure how your body responds, start with a small bowl and notice how you feel. As with all foods, pumpkin is best enjoyed as part of a varied, balanced diet rather than in large quantities.

Conclusion

Pumpkin — the sweet "southern gourd" — earns its place in the TCM pantry as a gentle, warming food for the spleen and stomach. Whether you stir it into morning congee, steam it for a simple side, or simmer it into a jujube soup, it brings comfort and nourishment in the traditional style of Chinese food therapy. Eat it warm, eat it often, and let your middle be the judge.
