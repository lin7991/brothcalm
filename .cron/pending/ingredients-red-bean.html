Adzuki Beans (Hong Dou) — The Dampness-Draining Red Bean

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 7 min read

Small, glossy, and the color of polished mahogany, adzuki beans have been part of East Asian kitchens and medicine cabinets for thousands of years. In Chinese food therapy, these humble red beans — called hong dou (红豆) or chi xiao dou (赤小豆) — are traditionally seen as one of the most practical foods for gently helping the body let go of what it does not need. When the seasons turn damp, or when your digestion feels heavy and sluggish, a pot of adzuki bean soup is often the first thing a traditional Chinese cook reaches for.

This guide covers the TCM energetics of adzuki beans, what they are traditionally used for, how to choose and store them, and three easy ways to bring them into your kitchen.

TCM Energetics of Adzuki Beans

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, every food carries a temperature, a flavor, and a set of organ affinities. Adzuki beans are classified as:

  • Nature: Neutral to slightly cool
  • Flavor: Sweet and slightly sour
  • Affinities (meridians): Heart and small intestine
  • Primary action: Traditionally believed to drain dampness, promote urination, and support the spleen's job of transforming fluids

The sweet flavor in TCM is associated with nourishing and harmonizing the spleen and stomach, while the slightly sour note gives adzuki beans an astringent quality that is traditionally thought to help hold things in place even as fluids are gently moved. It is this combination — moving dampness without harshness — that makes them a favorite for gentle, everyday food therapy.

Traditional Uses in Chinese Food Therapy

Adzuki beans appear in Chinese medical classics as a food that "drains dampness and benefits the spleen." In the language of TCM, dampness (湿) describes a pattern of sluggishness, heaviness, and water retention that can show up as:

  • A feeling of heaviness in the limbs, especially in humid weather
  • Soft or loose stools and a bloated feeling after meals
  • Mild puffiness, especially around the eyes or ankles
  • A coating on the tongue that looks thick or greasy

When a person shows these patterns, traditional cooks have historically reached for adzuki beans paired with other draining or spleen-supporting foods. One classic pairing is adzuki beans with aged tangerine peel (chen pi), which is traditionally used to move stagnant qi and dry dampness. Another is adzuki beans with Chinese yam, which is believed to strengthen the spleen so it can process fluids more effectively.

Historically, adzuki bean congee was also a common recovery food. The Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》), compiled by Li Shizhen in the 16th century, describes the small red bean as beneficial for promoting urination and reducing swelling — a traditional observation echoed across centuries of folk practice.

How to Choose and Store Adzuki Beans

Choosing good adzuki beans is simple once you know what to look for:

  • Appearance: Look for beans that are uniformly deep red, glossy, and free of cracks, holes, or discoloration. A few speckles are normal for some varieties.
  • Freshness: Dried beans should feel hard and sound crisp when they rattle in the bag. If they look wrinkled or dusty, they may be old stock.
  • Storage: Keep dried adzuki beans in an airtight container in a cool, dark, dry place. They will keep for about a year. Stored in the fridge in a sealed jar, they can last even longer.
  • Organic vs. conventional: Either works well for food therapy. Organic beans are a good choice if you want to minimize pesticide exposure.

You can also find adzuki bean paste (usually sweetened) in Asian grocery stores, but for food-therapy purposes, whole dried beans give you the most control over how they are prepared.

Three Easy Ways to Use Adzuki Beans

1. Classic Adzuki Bean Congee (Hong Dou Zhou)

The simplest and most traditional preparation. Rinse 1 cup of adzuki beans and soak overnight. Drain, add to a pot with 6 cups of water and a small piece of fresh ginger, and simmer gently for 60–90 minutes until the beans are soft and the liquid turns thick and reddish. Sweeten lightly with a little rock sugar or a few goji berries if you like. Enjoy warm, especially on cool or damp days.

2. Adzuki Bean & Tangerine Peel Tea

For a lighter, tea-like drink: combine 2 tablespoons of adzuki beans, 1 strip of dried tangerine peel, and 4 cups of water. Simmer for 40 minutes, strain, and sip warm throughout the day. Traditionally this pairing is believed to support digestion and help the body feel less heavy and bloated.

3. Red Bean & Snow Fungus Dessert

Soak a handful of snow fungus until soft and tear it into small pieces. Simmer with 1 cup of soaked adzuki beans in plenty of water for about an hour, then add rock sugar to taste and a handful of goji berries for the last 10 minutes. This makes a nourishing dessert soup that is popular in Cantonese households.

Who Should Be Cautious?

Adzuki beans are a gentle everyday food, but a few groups are traditionally advised to use them in moderation:

  • People with frequent urination: Because the beans are traditionally believed to promote urination, those who already urinate very often may prefer smaller portions.
  • People with very cold digestion: Those with yang deficiency — marked by cold hands and feet, a pale tongue, and a tendency to feel chilly — are traditionally advised to balance adzuki beans with warming ingredients such as ginger, dates, or cinnamon.
  • Pregnant women: Any regular food-therapy practice during pregnancy should first be discussed with a qualified healthcare provider.
  • Anyone with persistent swelling: If you notice ongoing or sudden swelling, see a doctor — food therapy is complementary, not a substitute for medical evaluation.

As with all beans, cook adzuki beans thoroughly. Raw or undercooked beans contain lectins that can cause digestive discomfort, and they are not safe to eat raw.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are adzuki beans the same as red kidney beans?

No. Adzuki beans are small, deep-red beans with a sweet, nutty flavor, while red kidney beans are much larger and belong to a different species. In Chinese food therapy, hong dou (adzuki beans) and chi xiao dou (small red beans) are the ones traditionally used for draining dampness.

Can I eat adzuki beans every day?

In moderate amounts, yes — a small bowl of adzuki bean soup or porridge a few times per week is a common tradition. Because the beans have a mild diuretic effect, people who already urinate very frequently or feel easily dehydrated may want to enjoy them less often.

How long do adzuki beans need to soak before cooking?

A 4 to 8 hour soak (or overnight) softens the skins and shortens cooking time. If you are short on time, you can skip the soak and simmer the beans for about 60 to 90 minutes, topping up with water as needed.

Do adzuki beans help with water retention?

In TCM, adzuki beans are traditionally classified as sweet and neutral with an affinity for the heart and small intestine, and they are believed to help the body drain dampness and reduce mild puffiness. This is a traditional food-therapy view, not a medical claim, and persistent swelling should always be checked by a doctor.
