Honey and Ginger Drink — Simple Winter Warmth the TCM Way

🍵 Tea & Tonic · 4 min read

Why Honey and Ginger Are a Match Made in TCM

When winter arrives and the wind turns sharp, few drinks feel as comforting as a warm cup of honey and ginger. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), this humble pairing is more than a cozy treat — it is a small, everyday expression of a core principle: warming the middle to support the whole body. Ginger is one of the most widely used warming ingredients in the Chinese kitchen, while honey has been prized for centuries as a gentle, nourishing sweetener. Together, they are traditionally used to chase away chill, support digestion, and comfort the throat.

This article explores the TCM view of honey and ginger, gives you a simple recipe you can make in minutes, and explains when — and for whom — this drink is traditionally recommended. As always, treat this as educational information, not medical advice.

TCM Properties of Ginger and Honey

Ginger (Sheng Jiang) — The Warming Spark

In TCM food therapy, fresh ginger is classified as warm in nature and pungent in flavor. It is believed to enter the Lung, Spleen, and Stomach channels. Because it is warming and moving, ginger is traditionally used to:

  • Dispel cold — a classic choice when you feel chilled, have cold hands and feet, or catch a cold-type chill in winter.
  • Harmonize the Stomach — traditionally used to settle nausea, motion sickness, and queasiness after rich meals.
  • Support the Lungs — in TCM, ginger is commonly paired with other ingredients in formulas for coughs with clear or white phlegm.

The famous Han-dynasty physician Zhang Zhongjing used fresh ginger in many of his classical formulas, often alongside jujube dates, to protect the Stomach while other herbs did their work. That pairing — ginger and jujube — is still a backbone of Chinese food therapy today.

Honey (Feng Mi) — The Gentle Moistener

Honey in TCM is classified as neutral to slightly cool and sweet in flavor. It is believed to enter the Spleen, Lung, and Large Intestine channels. Traditional uses of honey include:

  • Moistening the Lungs — honey is a classic ingredient in remedies for dry coughs and dry, irritated throats.
  • Nourishing the Spleen and Stomach — its sweet flavor is said to harmonize digestion and soften the harshness of other ingredients.
  • Moistening the Intestines — traditionally used to ease dryness-related constipation.

Importantly, honey acts as a moderator in TCM — its sweetness is believed to slow down and harmonize the action of sharper ingredients. That is exactly why it works so well with ginger: the ginger provides warmth and movement, while the honey softens the edges and adds nourishment.

A Brief History: From Classical Formulas to the Home Kitchen

Ginger appears in the earliest surviving Chinese medical texts, including the Shennong Bencao Jing (Divine Farmer's Classic of Materia Medica), where it is described as a warming herb that helps disperse cold. Honey, too, has a long recorded history in Chinese pharmacy — it was used both as food and as a preparation medium for herbal pills, because its sweetness was believed to harmonize formulas.

The combination of ginger and honey as a home drink is a folk classic that appears across China, especially in the north, where winters are severe. Families traditionally prepare it in early winter, sometimes adding scallion whites (cong bai) when someone feels a chill coming on. It is the kind of wisdom that passed from grandmother to grandchild — simple, cheap, and rooted in the seasonal rhythm of eating and drinking what the weather demands.

How to Make Honey and Ginger Drink

This is the classic home method — quick, forgiving, and endlessly adjustable.

Ingredients

  • 2–3 slices of fresh ginger (about 10–15 g), unpeeled or peeled — your choice; the peel is slightly cooler, so peel it if you want a more warming drink
  • 1–2 teaspoons of raw honey (add after simmering, never to boiling water)
  • 2 cups (about 500 ml) of filtered water
  • Optional: 2–3 jujube dates or a few goji berries for extra sweetness and nourishment

Instructions

  1. Add the ginger slices and water to a small pot.
  2. Bring to a gentle simmer and let it bubble softly for 8–10 minutes. You want the water to take on a golden tint and a gentle spicy aroma.
  3. Remove from heat and let it cool until it is warm but no longer steaming hot — around 60°C (140°F) or below.
  4. Stir in the honey until fully dissolved. Adding honey to boiling water is traditionally discouraged because high heat is thought to alter its gentle nature (and it makes the drink less soothing).
  5. Pour into a mug and sip slowly. Enjoy the warmth spreading through your chest and stomach.

Three Easy Variations

  • For a stronger chill: add one or two scallion whites during the simmer and remove them before serving. In TCM, this is a traditional home remedy for the early stage of a wind-cold chill.
  • For extra nourishment: simmer with 2–3 red jujube dates and a small handful of goji berries. This version is sweeter and is traditionally used to support energy during long winters.
  • For an afternoon reset: add a slice of lemon in the last minute of simmering. Lemon adds a bright note; in TCM terms it is slightly sour and believed to help gather and calm the qi.

When to Drink It — and When to Hold Off

Traditionally, honey and ginger drink is recommended for people who feel cold easily, who experience cold-type digestive sluggishness, or who simply want a warming ritual on a winter day. It is commonly drunk in the morning to "wake up" the Spleen and Stomach, or in the early evening after coming in from the cold.

In TCM terms, this drink is best suited to cold patterns — symptoms like pale complexion, cold hands and feet, clear thin mucus, and a preference for warm drinks. It is traditionally considered less suitable for people with pronounced heat signs, such as a red face, yellow thick phlegm, a dry sore throat with heat, or a feeling of being overheated.

Who Should Be Cautious

  • People with diabetes or blood sugar concerns: honey is a concentrated sugar; use a tiny amount or skip it.
  • People with acid reflux or heartburn: ginger may relax the lower esophageal valve for some people. Test with a small amount.
  • People on blood-thinning medication: large amounts of ginger may interact with anticoagulants. A slice in tea is usually fine, but check with your doctor if you drink it daily.
  • Infants under one year: honey should never be given to babies under 12 months because of the risk of infant botulism.
  • Pregnant women: small amounts of ginger are widely considered safe and are traditionally used for nausea, but always confirm with your healthcare provider.

The Bottom Line

Honey and ginger drink is one of the simplest, most accessible tonics in Chinese food therapy. It is warming, gentle, and rooted in centuries of tradition — yet it asks nothing more of you than a pot, two ingredients, and ten minutes. Used thoughtfully, it is a lovely way to honor the season, support your digestion, and bring a little ancient wisdom into your modern kitchen.
