Pear and Lily Bulb Dessert Soup: A Gentle Autumn Recipe

🥘 Recipe · 6 min read
Quick answer

Pear and lily bulb dessert soup is a light, fragrant bowl made by gently simmering fresh pear with dried lily bulb, water, and optional rock sugar. It suits a cool afternoon when you want something warm, softly sweet, and easy to portion—not a concentrated tonic or a substitute for care. Start with one pear, 10–15 g dried lily bulb, and 500 ml water; simmer until the lily bulb is tender and adjust sweetness at the end.

Is this guide for you?

Choose this recipe if you enjoy delicate fruit desserts, want a warm alternative to cake, or are exploring seasonal Chinese kitchen traditions. It is especially practical when ripe pears are plentiful and you would like a make-ahead snack for two. If you are looking for a substantial breakfast, choose a grain-based congee instead. If you prefer a savory, protein-rich bowl, a herbal chicken soup is a better fit.

The TCM lens: tradition, not anatomy

In traditional Chinese food-therapy language, pear is commonly described as cool in nature, sweet in flavor, and associated with the Lung and Stomach channels. Lily bulb (bai he, 百合) is traditionally described as sweet and slightly cool, with associations that include the Lung and Heart channels. These terms belong to a traditional framework: “Lung,” “Heart,” qi, yin, and dampness are not anatomical claims or diagnoses here.

Autumn eating in TCM often emphasizes gentler, moistening foods because the season is traditionally linked with dryness. That is a cultural and theoretical food pattern, not proof that soup changes airway health or cures a cough. If a dry or painful throat persists, seek appropriate medical advice rather than relying on a dessert.

The modern food lens

Pear contributes water, carbohydrate, and fiber; its skin contains plant compounds, although this soup is often made with peeled fruit. Lily bulb adds carbohydrate and a little fiber, and its texture is the main culinary reason to use it. The recipe is naturally low in fat and protein. Evidence about a particular pear-and-lily soup for a specific health outcome is limited, and the amount of sugar, portion size, and the rest of your diet still matter.

Try it once: pear and lily bulb soup

Ingredients for 2 small servings

  • 1 firm-ripe pear, about 180–220 g, cored and cut into wedges
  • 10–15 g dried lily bulb, rinsed and soaked in warm water for 20 minutes
  • 500 ml water
  • 1–2 teaspoons rock sugar, optional; or 1 teaspoon honey added after cooling slightly
  • Optional: 2 dried red dates, pitted, for a deeper fruit note

Method

  1. Rinse the lily bulb carefully and remove any dark or hard pieces. Drain the soak water.
  2. Put water, lily bulb, and red dates if using into a small pot. Bring just to a boil, then cover and simmer gently for 20 minutes.
  3. Add pear wedges and simmer 8–12 minutes more, until a chopstick meets the lily bulb easily but the pear still holds its shape.
  4. Taste before sweetening. Add rock sugar during the final two minutes, or let the bowl cool for several minutes before stirring in honey.
  5. Serve warm as a small dessert. Refrigerate leftovers promptly and use within two days; reheat once until steaming.

What to observe: note the pear variety, sweetness, texture, and how satisfying one small bowl feels. Next time, use less sugar, swap in snow fungus, or add one thin ginger slice—not all changes at once.

Troubleshooting and personalization

  • Lily bulb stays hard: older dried pieces may need a longer soak and another 10–15 minutes of gentle simmering. Keep the heat low so the pear does not collapse.
  • Soup tastes flat: a ripe pear, two pitted red dates, or a tiny pinch of salt can add contrast. Avoid solving every flavor problem with more sugar.
  • Too sweet: dilute with hot water and serve a smaller portion next time. Honey and rock sugar are still added sugars.
  • Texture is too soft: add the pear later, or use firmer fruit. Asian pear gives a clean bite; Bartlett pear becomes softer and more perfumed.

Specific safety boundaries

Wash fresh produce and discard damaged lily bulb. Do not use lily bulb sold for ornamental planting; buy food-grade dried bai he from a reputable seller and follow its package directions. People with diabetes or other reasons to monitor carbohydrate intake should count the pear, dates, and sweetener within their overall plan. If you have food allergies, digestive sensitivity, are pregnant or nursing, take regular medicines, or have a chronic condition, ask a clinician or registered dietitian whether this recipe fits you. Stop eating it if you notice an allergic or unusual reaction. This educational recipe does not diagnose, prevent, or treat illness.

FAQ

What does it taste like?

Lightly sweet, floral, and softly textured. The pear is clean and juicy; lily bulb adds a tender, faintly starchy bite.

Can I skip the sweetener?

Yes. Ripe pear and red dates may be enough. Taste first, especially if you are watching added sugar.

Can I drink it every day?

It is better treated as an occasional dessert or snack in a normal serving. There is no universal TCM schedule, and personal tolerance and dietary needs come first.
