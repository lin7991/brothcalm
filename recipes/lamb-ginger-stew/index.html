Lamb and Ginger Stew (Dang Gui Sheng Jiang Yang Rou Tang)

🥘 Recipe · 9 min read
Quick answer

Lamb and ginger stew is a fragrant, savory one-pot meal for someone who wants a warming dinner rather than a concentrated herbal drink. The classic TCM formula Dang Gui Sheng Jiang Yang Rou Tang combines lamb, fresh ginger, and dang gui (Chinese angelica root); a home cook can also leave out dang gui and still make a satisfying food-based stew. Start with one small bowl at lunch or dinner, notice how rich food feels afterward, and adjust the fat, ginger, and portion before making it a regular dish.

Is this guide for you?

Choose this recipe if you enjoy lamb, have a cool-weather cooking goal, and want a meal with a clear protein-and-broth structure. It suits a weekend pot that can be shared, with the aromatic ginger cutting through the meat’s richness. If you are new to lamb, use shoulder with visible fat trimmed and add a little more daikon or carrot.

Another guide may fit better if you want something light, vegetarian, or quick. A bowl of plain congee is gentler in texture, while herbal chicken soup usually tastes milder. If you are exploring ginger by itself, compare this meal with the practical notes in our ginger ingredient guide.

The decision: which version should you make?

  • Choose the classic version if: you like a noticeable herbal aroma and already buy food-grade dang gui from a reputable Chinese grocer or herbal dispensary.
  • Consider the everyday version if: you are cooking for children, prefer familiar flavors, or do not know the source of the root. Omit dang gui; use ginger, scallion, and a little white pepper.
  • Start with: 450 g lamb shoulder, 30–40 g sliced ginger, 1–2 thin slices of dang gui at most, and four servings of broth. A small amount lets you evaluate the flavor without turning dinner into an intense herbal experiment.

The TCM lens: a traditional food framework

In traditional Chinese dietary theory, lamb is commonly described as warming and sweet. Fresh ginger is pungent and warming, while dang gui is traditionally categorized as warm, sweet, and pungent, with associations to the Liver, Heart, and Spleen channels. These are traditional descriptions of food energetics, not anatomy, laboratory measurements, or a diagnosis.

Classical Chinese medicine texts, including the Jin Gui Yao Lue (Essential Prescriptions from the Golden Cabinet), are associated with the lamb-and-ginger formula. Historical context can explain why the dish is still discussed, but it does not establish that a bowl produces a specific modern health outcome. In TCM language, cooks may choose it when they want a warming, nourishing style of meal and may choose lighter, less warming foods when they feel overheated, thirsty, or weighed down after rich dishes.

The modern food lens

Lamb contributes protein, iron, zinc, vitamin B12, and fat; the exact amounts depend on the cut and serving size. Ginger contributes flavor with very little energy and can make a reduced-salt broth taste lively. Dang gui is an herb, not a required nutrient, and the composition of commercial slices can vary. Traditional use and a food’s nutrient profile are different kinds of information; neither is a promise about symptoms or disease.

Evidence for concentrated angelica products is not the same as evidence for a few slices simmered in dinner. Research on ginger also uses different preparations and amounts. For this reason, treat the recipe as a comforting meal, not as a substitute for individualized care or prescribed medication.

Try it once: a four-bowl pot

Ingredients

  • 450 g lamb shoulder or leg, cut into 3 cm pieces
  • 30–40 g fresh ginger, scrubbed and sliced
  • 1–2 thin slices food-grade dang gui, optional
  • 2 scallions, 1 daikon or 2 carrots, 1.2 liters water, and 1 teaspoon salt to begin
  • Optional: 1 tablespoon Shaoxing wine, a pinch of white pepper, and chopped cilantro

Method

  1. Blanch the lamb in boiling water for 2 minutes, drain, and rinse. This gives the broth a cleaner aroma.
  2. Add lamb, ginger, dang gui if using, scallion whites, and water to a heavy pot. Bring to a boil, then skim and lower to a gentle simmer.
  3. Cover loosely and simmer 60–75 minutes, until the lamb is tender. Add daikon or carrot for the final 25–30 minutes.
  4. Remove the dang gui after tasting; its fragrance can become dominant. Season with salt, white pepper, and wine if desired. Finish with scallion greens or cilantro.

Serve about 250–300 ml broth with one portion of lamb and vegetables. Eat it warm with rice or noodles. Lunch or an early dinner is a practical first timing; drink water normally, and refrigerate leftovers within two hours. Reheat only the portion you plan to eat.

Substitutions: Use chicken thighs for a lighter pot, daikon for a clean sweetness, or half lamb and half mushrooms for less richness. Do not substitute an unknown “angelica” supplement for food-grade dang gui.

Troubleshooting and personalization

  • Too greasy: chill the broth and lift off the solidified fat, or trim more fat before cooking.
  • Too herbal: use no dang gui next time, or simmer it for only 20–30 minutes in a tea infuser.
  • Too strong or heavy: serve a smaller portion with vegetables and rice; choose chicken or tofu next time.
  • Too flat: add ginger, scallion, a little salt, or rice vinegar at the table instead of salting the whole pot heavily.

Keep a simple note after the meal: portion, time, richness, flavor, and how comfortable you felt over the next few hours. That observation is more useful than assigning yourself a TCM pattern from one dinner.

Specific safety boundaries

Use only food-grade, clearly labeled ingredients. Dang gui may not be appropriate with blood-thinning medicines or for people with bleeding concerns; ask a clinician or pharmacist before using it. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, upcoming surgery, a chronic condition, or regular prescription medication are good reasons to choose the ginger-only version until a qualified professional reviews the herb. Stop eating it if you develop an allergic reaction or unusual discomfort.

Do not use this recipe to delay evaluation of persistent fatigue, pain, fever, dizziness, heavy bleeding, or other concerning changes. Food can be part of daily care, but it cannot replace diagnosis or treatment.

FAQ

What does the finished stew taste like?

Think tender lamb, peppery ginger, soft root vegetables, and a savory broth. Dang gui adds a floral, bittersweet herbal note, so the ginger-only version is the safer flavor choice for a first try.

Can I eat it every day?

There is no need to make it a daily ritual. Rotate it with lighter soups, grains, beans, vegetables, and other proteins, and let portion size and personal tolerance guide you.

Is dang gui necessary?

No. It is the traditional herb associated with the named formula, but omitting it leaves a useful lamb-and-ginger meal. The recipe is still delicious without it.
