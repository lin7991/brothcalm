Ginseng Chicken Soup (Ren Shen Ji Tang) — A Traditional Qi-Focused Bowl

🥘 Recipe · 8 min read
Quick answer

Quick answer: Ginseng chicken soup is a savory, warming bowl traditionally used in Chinese food therapy when someone wants a substantial meal. In TCM theory, ginseng is associated with supporting qi, while chicken and broth make the dish filling and easy to eat. It is not a “more is better” tonic: start with a modest amount, one small bowl, and notice how you feel.

Is this guide for you?

This recipe suits a cold-weather lunch, a long day when you want a complete bowl, or a curious cook exploring ren shen ji tang (人参鸡汤) as traditional food rather than a concentrated supplement. If you want a lighter evening meal, a congee recipe may be a better fit. If you are comparing ginseng varieties, read our ginseng ingredient guide first. Food therapy is personal: a bowl that feels comforting to one person can feel too rich or warming to another.

The decision: which version should you make?

  • Choose this soup if: you enjoy slow-simmered meals, tolerate chicken, and want to taste ginseng in a balanced broth.
  • Consider another option if: you need a very low-sodium meal, dislike poultry, or want something quick. Plain chicken soup, vegetable soup, or mild ginger tea is easier to adjust.
  • Start with: 2–3 thin slices of food-grade ginseng for a whole chicken. Ginger, scallion, and a few goji berries add aroma without making the broth intensely herbal.

The TCM lens: what “qi tonic” means here

In traditional Chinese medicine, ginseng is commonly described as sweet and slightly bitter, warm or neutral depending on variety and tradition, and associated with the Lung and Spleen channels. Chicken is traditionally viewed as warming and nourishing. These are TCM food-therapy concepts: qi is a traditional functional idea, not a measurable anatomical substance, and “warming” does not mean raising clinical body temperature. Traditional practitioners may choose a recipe according to season, appetite, digestion, and an individual pattern, so this home soup is not a practitioner-designed prescription.

The modern food lens

Chicken contributes protein; broth contributes fluid and flavor; vegetables and rice can make the bowl more complete. The nutrition profile changes with the cut, skin, salt, and portion. Ginseng contains compounds called ginsenosides, but home soup is not equivalent to a standardized extract, and research on concentrated products cannot be transferred directly to this recipe. Evidence for specific outcomes from a bowl of soup is limited and context-dependent; dose, species, preparation, and individual response vary.

Try it once: a small, adjustable pot

Ingredients

  • 1 small whole chicken, 1.2–1.5 kg, or 4 bone-in thighs
  • 2–3 thin slices dried ginseng, about 3–5 g total
  • 4 slices fresh ginger and 2 scallions
  • 6–8 dried goji berries, optional
  • 2 liters water; salt at the table

Method

  1. Blanch chicken in boiling water for 2 minutes, drain, and rinse.
  2. Put chicken, ginseng, ginger, scallion whites, and water in a heavy pot. Bring to a boil, then lower to a quiet simmer.
  3. Cover loosely and simmer 60–90 minutes, skimming early foam. Add goji berries for the final 10 minutes.
  4. Remove ginseng if the flavor is strong. Taste one small bowl, then add a little salt and sliced scallion greens.

Timing and portion: Try 250–350 ml with lunch or an early dinner. The first time, do not stack it with ginseng capsules, energy drinks, or several stimulating products. Refrigerate leftovers promptly and use within 3–4 days, or freeze portions. Use chicken thighs for a smaller batch, omit goji for a less sweet broth, and never substitute an unknown wild root for identified food-grade ginseng.

Troubleshooting and personalization

  • Too bitter: use less ginseng next time, add water, and serve with rice and vegetables.
  • Too greasy: chill the broth and lift off solidified fat, or use skinless thighs.
  • Too salty: dilute with unsalted water and season individual bowls.
  • Warm, restless, or uncomfortable: stop the experiment and choose a plain meal next time. Note the amount, timing, caffeine, and other herbal products that day.
  • Want it lighter: halve the ginseng, add mushrooms or napa cabbage, and skim before serving.

Safety boundaries

Use clearly identified, food-grade ginseng from a reputable source. Ask a clinician or pharmacist before using it if pregnant or breastfeeding, managing diabetes or blood-pressure problems, living with a bleeding or clotting condition, preparing for a procedure, or taking medicines. Interactions are possible, including with blood thinners, diabetes medicines, stimulants, and some blood-pressure medicines. A soup serving is not automatically risk-free when several ginseng products are combined.

Do not use this recipe to delay care for persistent fatigue, fever, unexplained weight change, pain, or other concerning symptoms. Cook chicken fully, refrigerate leftovers promptly, and avoid serving herbal ingredients to children without professional guidance.

FAQ

Can I drink ginseng chicken soup every day?

There is no universal daily amount. Occasional small servings are a sensible starting point; review frequent use with a professional if you take medicines.

Is American ginseng the same as Asian ginseng?

No. They are different plants with different traditional descriptions and preparations. Follow the product label.

Can I make this soup without ginseng?

Yes. Chicken, ginger, scallion, and vegetables make a comforting soup on their own.
