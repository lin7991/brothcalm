Spinach and Pork Liver Soup — A Classic Blood-Building Bowl

🥘 Recipe · 6 min read
Quick answer

Spinach and pork liver soup is a savory, fast-cooking dish that pairs tender liver with leafy greens, ginger, and a light broth. In traditional Chinese food therapy, liver is often described as a rich Blood-nourishing food, while spinach is used as a softer, vegetable-based companion. For a first try, make one small bowl, cook the liver thoroughly, and use it as an occasional meal rather than a daily supplement.

Is this guide for you?

This recipe suits a curious cook who wants a warm, iron-containing meal and is comfortable with liver’s distinct mineral flavor. It is also useful if you want a 20-minute soup rather than a long-simmered tonic. Choose another guide if you dislike organ meats, need an individualized plan for low iron, are pregnant, or have been told to limit vitamin A or dietary cholesterol. Food cannot confirm or correct a deficiency; persistent fatigue, breathlessness, pallor, or other concerning changes deserve clinical evaluation.

If you want a milder everyday green soup, try watercress or spinach with tofu. If you prefer a more warming, slow-cooked meal, compare it with herbal chicken soup. For a broader look at food choices and traditional patterns, see BrothCalm’s food guide to Blood deficiency in TCM.

The decision: which bowl should you choose?

  • Choose spinach and liver soup if: you want a small, savory portion of liver, like ginger’s brightness, and can eat it occasionally.
  • Consider another option if: you need a vegetarian meal, want a lower-vitamin-A dish, or find liver’s flavor too intense. Tofu, egg, beans, or pork loin can provide a different protein experience.
  • Start with: half a serving at lunch, with rice or noodles and another vegetable. Notice taste, fullness, and how the meal sits with you; do not use sensations as a diagnosis.

TCM lens: what the tradition says

TCM terminology is a traditional framework, not a description of anatomy or a modern medical mechanism. In that framework, pork liver is commonly described as 性温或平 (warm or neutral depending on the school and preparation), with a 甘、苦 (sweet and bitter) flavor profile, and an affinity for the Liver channel. Spinach is often described as 性凉 (cool), with a sweet flavor and traditional associations with the Liver and Large Intestine channels.

That contrast explains the kitchen logic: ginger and a hot broth add warmth and aroma to the cooler greens, while the greens keep the dish from feeling as heavy as liver alone. Traditional writers may discuss Blood, Liver, yin, qi, or dampness; these are TCM pattern concepts, not claims that the soup changes blood counts, cleanses an organ, or manages an illness.

Modern food lens: useful facts and unknowns

Pork liver contains heme iron, vitamin B12, protein, and preformed vitamin A. Spinach provides folate, vitamin K, carotenoids, fiber, and non-heme iron. The body handles these forms of iron differently, and absorption varies with the rest of the meal and with individual circumstances. A squeeze of lemon or a side of bell pepper adds vitamin C and a fresh contrast, although it does not guarantee better iron status.

Nutrition tables are averages, not a personal assessment. Evidence for a particular soup improving a symptom or correcting anemia is limited and should not be assumed. Liver is nutrient-dense enough that “more” is not automatically better; portion size and frequency matter. If iron deficiency is suspected, a blood test and professional advice are more useful than self-prescribing liver.

Try it once: a two-serving bowl

Ingredients

  • 120–150 g fresh pork liver, thinly sliced
  • 3 packed cups washed baby spinach
  • 3 cups unsalted chicken or vegetable stock
  • 4 thin slices fresh ginger
  • 1 teaspoon soy sauce, optional, plus a pinch of salt
  • 1 teaspoon cornstarch and 1 teaspoon water, optional for a tender coating
  • To serve: scallions, white pepper, and lemon or bell pepper

Preparation

  1. Keep the liver chilled. Rinse briefly if needed, pat dry, and slice into thin, even pieces. Toss with the cornstarch slurry for a light coating if you like.
  2. Bring the stock and ginger to a gentle boil. Add spinach and cook for 30–60 seconds, just until wilted.
  3. Add the liver pieces one at a time so they do not clump. Return the soup to a simmer and cook until the center is fully opaque and the liver reaches a safe internal temperature. Do not serve it pink or raw.
  4. Season lightly with soy sauce, salt, and white pepper. Finish with scallions and lemon or bell pepper at the table.
  5. Serve one bowl with rice or noodles. Refrigerate leftovers promptly in a shallow container and reheat thoroughly; for best texture, make only what you expect to eat.

Timing: Make this at lunch or dinner, not as a concentrated morning shot. For a first trial, use half the bowl and wait to see how the richness and texture suit you.

Troubleshooting and personalization

  • Metallic flavor: use very fresh liver, trim visible connective tissue, keep slices thin, and add ginger, scallion, or a little lemon. Avoid soaking for hours, which can make the texture watery.
  • Tough liver: the slices were probably too thick or cooked too long. Slice thinner and add them at the very end, while still cooking fully.
  • Cloudy broth: a brief blanch and rinse can give a cleaner flavor, although it is optional for a quick home soup.
  • Too rich: reduce the liver to 60 g per person, increase spinach and broth, and serve with plain rice. Alternatively, swap in tofu or pork loin.
  • Too salty: use unsalted stock and season only after the liver and greens are cooked.

Specific safety boundaries

Buy liver from a reliable, refrigerated source and prevent cross-contamination. Cook it completely; do not rely on color alone. Because liver is high in preformed vitamin A, people who are pregnant or trying to conceive should ask their prenatal clinician about whether to avoid it and how much is appropriate. Anyone taking medication, managing a liver or metabolic condition, or following a clinician-directed iron plan should check before making liver a regular food. Children need age-appropriate portions and supervision. Stop and seek care for an allergic reaction or significant food-poisoning symptoms.

In short: this is a traditional-style, occasional soup—not a diagnosis, supplement, or replacement for medical care. Enjoy the small bowl for its flavor and nourishment, and let professional testing guide any concern about iron or fatigue.
