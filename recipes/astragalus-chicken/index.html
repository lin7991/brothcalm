Chicken with Astragalus and Goji — The Ultimate Qi-Building Bowl

🥘 Recipe · 8 min read
Quick answer

In Chinese food therapy, a slowly simmered chicken bowl is valued for its warmth, aroma, and ability to make everyday nourishment feel restorative. This version pairs chicken with astragalus root (huang qi) and goji berries (gou qi zi)—ingredients traditionally used in TCM kitchens when a gentle, nourishing soup is desired.

In Chinese food therapy, a slowly simmered chicken bowl is valued for its warmth, aroma, and ability to make everyday nourishment feel restorative. This version pairs chicken with astragalus root (huang qi) and goji berries (gou qi zi)—ingredients traditionally used in TCM kitchens when a gentle, nourishing soup is desired.

Rather than treating this dish as a medicine, think of it as a food-first recipe: light broth, tender meat, vegetables, and a modest amount of traditional herbs. Its appeal is especially clear in cooler weather, after a demanding day, or whenever a simple bowl of soup sounds more inviting than a complicated meal.

TCM perspective: warmth, flavor, and qi

Traditional Chinese Medicine describes astragalus as sweet and slightly warm, with an association with the Lung and Spleen meridians. It is traditionally used in food therapy to support qi, the body’s vital functional energy. Goji berries are commonly described as sweet and neutral to mildly warm, and are traditionally associated with the Liver and Kidney systems.

Chicken is considered a warming, nourishing food in many Chinese dietetic traditions. Ginger adds pungent warmth and helps give the broth a clean, lively finish, while scallion and shiitake mushrooms bring savory flavor. These descriptions belong to TCM theory, not a biomedical diagnosis, and individual responses can vary.

Ingredients for 4 servings

  • 700 g bone-in chicken thighs or drumsticks
  • 15–20 g dried astragalus root slices, from a reputable food or herb supplier
  • 2 tablespoons dried goji berries
  • 6 thin slices fresh ginger
  • 2 scallions, cut into sections
  • 150 g shiitake mushrooms, fresh or rehydrated
  • 1 medium carrot, sliced
  • 1.5 liters water or unsalted chicken stock
  • 1 tablespoon rice wine, optional
  • Salt to taste, added near the end

How to prepare the astragalus chicken bowl

1. Prepare the chicken and herbs

Rinse the chicken and pat it dry. If using dried astragalus, briefly brush away any loose particles and place the slices in a small muslin spice bag or tea filter. This makes them easy to remove before serving. Rinse the goji berries and set them aside; adding them later helps preserve their texture.

2. Build a clear broth

Place the chicken in a pot with the water, ginger, scallion, mushrooms, carrot, and astragalus bag. Bring it just to a boil, then skim away any foam. Lower the heat until the surface is gently bubbling. Cover partially and simmer for 45–60 minutes, until the chicken is tender and the broth smells fragrant.

3. Finish gently

Remove the astragalus bag. Stir in the goji berries during the final 8–10 minutes, along with rice wine if using. Taste and add a small amount of salt. Serve hot with rice, millet, or noodles. For a lighter bowl, remove the chicken skin and skim excess fat from the surface.

Serving rhythm and practical tips

A reasonable food-therapy portion is one bowl, about 350–450 ml, with chicken and vegetables. Enjoy it with a meal rather than replacing a varied diet with soup alone. One or two servings per week is a simple starting rhythm for most healthy adults; daily use is not necessary to appreciate the recipe.

For a pressure cooker, combine the chicken, astragalus, ginger, vegetables, and water and cook on high pressure for 15 minutes, followed by a natural release. Add goji berries after opening and simmer them in the hot broth for several minutes. If you prefer a vegetarian variation, use mushrooms, tofu, carrot, and winter squash, but remove astragalus before serving.

Choose astragalus slices that are dry, clean, and pleasantly sweet-smelling. Keep dried herbs and goji berries sealed in a cool, dark cupboard. Refrigerate leftover soup within two hours and use it within three days, reheating until steaming hot. You can also freeze individual portions for up to two months.

Flavor variations

  • More warming: add one star anise or two slices of fresh ginger, especially in cold weather.
  • Gentler and lighter: use chicken breast, extra mushrooms, and a handful of bok choy at the end.
  • More filling: add peeled Chinese yam or a small handful of rinsed rice during simmering.
  • Less sweet: use one tablespoon of goji berries and finish with black pepper and cilantro.

Who should use extra care?

Astragalus is an herb, not simply a seasoning, and quality and dose matter. People who are pregnant or breastfeeding, children, older adults taking several medicines, or anyone managing an autoimmune condition should ask a qualified clinician before using astragalus regularly. Those taking immune-modulating, blood-pressure, blood-sugar, or diuretic medicines should also seek individualized guidance.

Skip the recipe or use a plain chicken soup if you have a fever, acute illness, a known allergy, or digestive upset that worsens with rich foods. Stop if you notice rash, stomach discomfort, or another unexpected reaction. Traditional food energetics can be a useful cultural framework, but they do not replace diagnosis, treatment, or advice from a licensed professional.

Frequently asked questions

Can I drink astragalus chicken soup every day?

There is no need to drink it daily. A varied diet and occasional serving are more practical. If you want frequent astragalus use, ask a healthcare professional first, especially when taking medication.

Can I eat the astragalus slices?

They are generally removed after simmering because the slices are fibrous and woody. The soup is the usual food preparation; do not treat the discarded slices as a snack.

When is this bowl traditionally enjoyed?

Many families enjoy warming chicken soups in autumn and winter, or after periods of exertion. TCM food therapy emphasizes matching food choices to season, constitution, and digestion.

Does this recipe replace medical care?

No. It is a nourishing recipe inspired by TCM tradition, not a treatment. Seek professional care for persistent symptoms or health concerns.
