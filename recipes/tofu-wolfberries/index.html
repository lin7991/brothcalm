Silken Tofu with Wolfberries — A Cooling, Nourishing Dish

🥘 Recipe · 6 min read

On warm days, when rich stews feel too heavy and cold salads leave you unsatisfied, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) offers a gentle middle path: food that cools without chilling, nourishes without weighing you down. Silken tofu with wolfberries is exactly that kind of dish — soft, soothing, and quietly restorative.

In TCM food therapy, every ingredient carries a temperature and a flavor, and the art of eating well is about matching them to your body's needs. Silken tofu is considered cooling and moistening, while wolfberries (goji berries) are known as a classic yin-nourishing, liver-supporting food. Together, they make a light meal that is traditionally used to comfort the body during hot weather, after long days, or whenever you feel a little overheated and depleted.

What Is Silken Tofu?

Silken tofu (嫩豆腐, nèn dòufu) is the softest, most delicate form of tofu. Unlike firm tofu, which is pressed and dense, silken tofu is made by coagulating soy milk without pressing out the whey, so it holds a smooth, custard-like texture that almost melts on the tongue. It is sometimes sold in aseptic boxes that need no refrigeration until opened, which makes it a convenient pantry staple.

In TCM terms, tofu is traditionally described as cool in nature and sweet in flavor, entering the stomach and large intestine channels. It has long been used as a gentle, easy-to-digest source of nourishment — a food that "clears heat" without being harsh, and that supports the body's fluids. Because it is soft and mild, it is also a traditional choice for people recovering from illness, older adults, and anyone whose digestion prefers gentle foods.

Why Wolfberries?

Wolfberries — better known in the West as goji berries — are one of the most beloved ingredients in Chinese food therapy. They are considered neutral to slightly warm in nature and sweet in flavor, entering the liver and kidney channels. In TCM theory, wolfberries are traditionally used to:

  • Nourish the liver and kidneys — the foundation of vitality and longevity in TCM thought
  • Support the eyes — wolfberries are classically associated with clear vision
  • Nourish yin and blood — helping the body stay moist and calm
  • Gently replenish essence (jing) — a concept tied to deep, long-term vitality

Pairing a cooling food like tofu with a mildly warming, nourishing berry creates a balanced dish — the wolfberries temper the tofu's coolness, while the tofu keeps the overall meal light. This kind of pairing is at the heart of TCM food therapy: few foods are "good" or "bad" on their own; what matters is how they work together.

The Recipe: Silken Tofu with Wolfberries

This is a no-cook (or gently steamed) dish that comes together in about 15 minutes. It works beautifully as a light lunch, a starter, or a calming dinner on a hot evening.

Ingredients

  • 1 block (about 300 g) silken tofu
  • 2 tablespoons dried wolfberries (goji berries)
  • 1 teaspoon honey or maple syrup (optional, for sweetness)
  • 1 teaspoon toasted sesame oil
  • 1 tablespoon light soy sauce (or tamari for a gluten-free option)
  • 1 teaspoon rice vinegar
  • 1 spring onion, finely sliced
  • A few coriander leaves (optional)
  • 2–3 thin slices of fresh ginger (optional, for a warmer version)

Method

  1. Soften the wolfberries. Place the dried wolfberries in a small bowl, cover with warm water, and soak for 5–10 minutes until they plump up. Drain and set aside.
  2. Prepare the tofu. Carefully turn the silken tofu out onto a plate. Let any excess liquid drain off, then pat it dry very gently with a paper towel. Silken tofu is fragile — handle it with a wide spatula.
  3. Make the dressing. In a small bowl, whisk together the soy sauce, rice vinegar, sesame oil, and honey (if using). If you are making the ginger version, add the thin ginger slices to the dressing and let them infuse for a few minutes.
  4. Assemble. Spoon the drained wolfberries over the tofu, scatter the spring onion and coriander on top, then drizzle with the dressing.
  5. Serve. Enjoy immediately at room temperature, or chill for 20 minutes on a very warm day. For a gentler, warmer version, steam the dressed tofu over boiling water for 3–4 minutes before serving.

Variations

  • Wolfberry tofu soup: Cut the tofu into cubes, add to a light vegetable broth with soaked wolfberries and bok choy, and simmer gently for 5 minutes. This turns the dish into a soothing bowl of soup — try our bok choy tofu soup for a similar idea.
  • Cold sesame version: Swap the soy-vinegar dressing for a spoonful of tahini thinned with warm water, a drop of sesame oil, and a pinch of salt.
  • Floral touch: Add a few dried chrysanthemum petals to the soaking water of the wolfberries for an extra cooling, aromatic note.
  • More substantial: Serve over steamed rice with a portion of goji steamed eggs for a protein-rich, yin-nourishing meal.

When to Eat This Dish

In TCM thinking, food choices follow the seasons and the body's signals. Silken tofu with wolfberries is traditionally suited to:

  • Warm weather — when the body naturally runs hotter and lighter food feels right
  • Dryness — when skin, lips, or eyes feel parched, and moistening foods are called for
  • Evenings — as a light dinner that won't burden digestion before sleep
  • Recovery periods — when appetite is small and gentle nourishment is welcome

If you tend toward a cold constitution — cold hands and feet, a dislike of cold drinks, loose stools — enjoy this dish in moderation, choose the steamed version, and add ginger or a pinch of white pepper to warm it up.

Notes on Quality and Selection

Not all tofu is created equal. For the best texture and flavor:

  • Look for "silken" or "soft" tofu — often labeled 嫩豆腐 (nèn dòufu) in Asian grocery stores. Firm or extra-firm tofu will not give the same silky texture.
  • Choose good wolfberries. High-quality dried wolfberries are plump, bright red-orange, and slightly sweet with a mild tang. Avoid berries that look dull, gray, or overly dark.
  • Store properly. Keep unopened aseptic tofu at room temperature, and refrigerate after opening — it is best used within 2–3 days. Soaked wolfberries keep in the fridge for up to 3 days.

Things to Keep in Mind

As with any food, balance matters. A few gentle cautions:

  • Cold patterns: People with a marked cold constitution should not eat large amounts of raw, chilled tofu. Steaming or warming the dish, and adding ginger, helps offset the cooling nature.
  • Digestive sensitivity: Soy can be gas-forming for some people. If you are new to tofu, start with a small portion and chew well.
  • Allergies: Anyone with a soy allergy should avoid this dish entirely. Those managing hormone-sensitive conditions may wish to discuss soy intake with their healthcare provider.
  • Pregnancy: Well-cooked, fresh tofu is generally considered fine in TCM food therapy, but warm, freshly prepared versions are preferred over chilled ones. Always check with your midwife or doctor.

Silken tofu with wolfberries is a quiet reminder of what Chinese food therapy does best: taking two humble ingredients and combining them into something that feels like care. Whether you serve it as a cooling summer lunch or a gentle evening meal, it is a dish that lets your body — and your taste buds — breathe.
