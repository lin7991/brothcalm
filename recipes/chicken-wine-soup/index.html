Chicken Wine Soup (Huang Jiu Ji) — A Warming Postpartum Broth

🥘 Recipe · 7 min read

What Is Chicken Wine Soup (Huang Jiu Ji)?

Chicken wine soup — huang jiu ji (黄酒鸡), "yellow wine chicken" — is a savory, faintly sweet braise of chicken simmered in Chinese rice wine with ginger and toasted sesame oil. In southern China it is one of the best-loved dishes of the postpartum kitchen: warming, aromatic, and rich without feeling heavy. In a Hakka or Cantonese home in the month after a birth, a pot of it is often on the stove.

Like many dishes in Chinese food therapy, huang jiu ji sits between food and medicine. It is the meal a family cooks when someone needs warmth, strength, and steady nourishment — traditionally in TCM a gentle blood-nourishing tonic, believed to support recovery and circulation after childbirth or during a long, cold winter.

TCM Properties: Nature, Flavor, and Meridians

In TCM theory every food has a nature (性), a flavor (味), and organ meridians (归经) it is said to enter. The character of huang jiu ji comes from how its ingredients combine.

Nature: Warm and Gently Moving

Chinese rice wine is considered warm and fresh ginger warmer still, which pushes the whole pot toward the warm end of the spectrum — the reason it is traditionally eaten in winter, in the weeks after birth, or whenever someone feels cold, tired, and slow to warm up.

Flavor: Sweet, Pungent, and Aromatic

Chicken is sweet and neutral-to-warm; rice wine and ginger are pungent; jujube dates and goji berries add a round sweetness. Sweet flavors are traditionally said to build and tonify, while pungent ones are believed to move Qi and blood and to disperse cold.

Meridians: Spleen, Stomach, and Liver

Chicken is traditionally associated with the Spleen and Stomach, the organs TCM credits with turning food into Qi and Blood. Rice wine and dang gui are said to enter the Liver, which stores the Blood. Together, the dish is believed to warm the middle and nourish the Blood while keeping energy moving.

Traditional Use: A Southern Chinese Postpartum Custom

The Confinement Month (Zuo Yue Zi)

In Chinese tradition, the first month after birth — zuo yue zi (坐月子), "sitting the month" — is a period of deliberate rest and warming food. Cold drafts and cold drinks are avoided, and the diet leans on soups, congee, and slow-braised dishes. Chicken wine soup is a centerpiece of that menu, especially in Hakka, Guangdong, and Fujian households, where the cook adjusts the wine, ginger, and herbs to how the new mother feels. What the Classical Sources Say

Older texts give each ingredient its reputation. 《本草纲目》 (Bencao Gangmu, Li Shizhen, 1596) discusses rice wine as warming and gently moving, believed to circulate blood and dispel cold in cooking, and records ginger for warming the middle and easing a cold stomach. Jujube appears in the earlier 《神农本草经》 as a sweet, gently nourishing fruit for the Spleen.

These are historical attributions rather than a clinical protocol. Our guide to blood deficiency explains how Blood is understood in TCM theory.

The Classic Chicken Wine Soup Recipe

Serves four to six; about ninety minutes. Use a whole free-range chicken cut into large bone-in pieces — bone gives the broth body and, in TCM thinking, a deeper nourishing quality. A clay pot holds the heat best.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole free-range chicken (about 1.2–1.5 kg / 2.6–3.3 lb), cut into large pieces, skin on
  • 350–500 ml Chinese rice wine (huang jiu)
  • 60 g (2 oz) fresh ginger, peeled and sliced thick, or smashed
  • 3 tablespoons toasted sesame oil (black sesame oil if you can find it)
  • 8–10 dried jujube dates, briefly soaked
  • 1 small piece dang gui (angelica root), 5–8 g — optional
  • 1 tablespoon goji berries — optional
  • 1–2 tablespoons light soy sauce, or a pinch of sea salt
  • 400–600 ml water or light chicken stock (omit for a stronger, wine-forward pot)

Dang gui is the traditional Blood-nourishing partner for chicken; goji berries add sweetness. If either is new to you, start with half the amount.

Instructions

  1. Rinse the chicken and pat it dry. For a cleaner broth, blanch the pieces in boiling water for one minute, then drain and rinse away any scum.
  2. Heat the sesame oil in a heavy pot over medium heat. Toast it gently — a classic warming base, but it should not smoke.
  3. Add the ginger and fry one to two minutes, until fragrant and golden at the edges.
  4. Add the chicken and sear three to four minutes per side until lightly browned. Turning the pieces in the oil and ginger gives huang jiu ji its signature aroma.
  5. Pour in the rice wine. It will hiss and steam — that is exactly what should happen. Bring it to a gentle simmer.
  6. Add the jujube dates, the dang gui if using, and the water or stock. Cover and simmer gently 45–60 minutes, until the chicken pulls easily from the bone.
  7. Stir in the goji berries for the last five minutes only; they soften quickly and lose their bright character if cooked longer.
  8. Season with soy sauce or salt. For a brighter wine aroma, add two or three tablespoons of fresh rice wine off the heat just before serving.
  9. Rest the pot five minutes, then serve hot — broth, chicken, dates and all.

Variations

Dang Gui and Goji Version

The most traditional tonic version. Add 5–8 g of dang gui and a tablespoon of goji berries as written above. Dang gui is intensely aromatic and slightly bitter, so some cooks add it in the final twenty minutes to keep the flavor sweet rather than medicinal. In some family recipes a small spoonful of ejiao is stirred in at the very end — it is heavy, so use it sparingly.

Silkie Chicken and Longan

Swap the regular chicken for silkie chicken, the black-boned bird traditionally reserved for tonic soups, and add a handful of dried longan flesh with the jujube dates for a sweeter, softer, more calming pot.

A Lighter, Family-Friendly Pot

Reduce the wine to 150 ml and add extra stock. Use more jujube dates in place of dang gui for sweetness without bitterness. For an alcohol-free version that keeps the character of the dish, replace the wine entirely with stock, double the ginger, and finish with a teaspoon of rice vinegar for lift.

Practical Guidance: When, How Often, and How to Serve It

When to Eat It

Traditionally in TCM, chicken wine soup is eaten during the postpartum month, in the cold months, or whenever someone feels cold, pale, and depleted — the pattern traditionally described as blood deficiency. It is a winter dish first and a recovery dish second, and it suits people who run cold rather than hot.

How Often

In many families it appears several times a week through confinement. Otherwise, two or three servings a week is a comfortable rhythm, and one bowl at a sitting is plenty — this is a rich, wine-based pot.

How to Serve It

Serve it hot, broth included, over steamed rice or thin wheat noodles, with a plate of blanched greens to balance the richness. In TCM thinking, pairing a warming dish with cold drinks or raw salads works against its purpose; warm water or plain tea is the more traditional choice.

Storage

Cool the soup within two hours, then refrigerate it sealed for up to three days; the fat will set on top and can be lifted off. Reheat gently to a simmer — a few minutes of simmering also lets more alcohol cook off. It freezes for up to two months, though the aromas soften.

Cautions and Who Should Avoid It

Alcohol Content — the Most Important Point

Chinese rice wine is typically 10–20% alcohol by volume. Simmering reduces the alcohol but does not eliminate it; even after an hour of gentle cooking, some remains. If you are pregnant, consider huang jiu ji a dish to skip and use the alcohol-free variation instead. If you are nursing, note that alcohol passes into breast milk, and general guidance is to limit or avoid it around feeding times. Many families cook the wine off longer and serve the soup well before the next feed — but the safest course is to ask your healthcare provider about your own situation.

Heat Signs

Because the dish is warm and rich, people who run hot may find it too much. If you regularly notice a red face, thirst, mouth ulcers, a dry throat, constipation, or restlessness — the cluster TCM calls heat signs — a warming dish like this is traditionally considered the wrong fit, and lighter, cooler foods would be suggested instead.

Who Else Should Be Cautious

  • Anyone who must avoid alcohol for medical reasons, including people with a diagnosed liver condition — use the alcohol-free version.
  • People who flush, feel headachy, or feel unwell after alcohol, even in food.
  • Anyone with weak digestion who finds rich, oily food heavy — skim the fat and use less sesame oil.
  • Children, who should have the alcohol-free, low-ginger variation only.
  • Anyone taking medication or managing a chronic condition — check with a qualified healthcare provider first.

None of this makes the dish sound risky: it is everyday comfort food across much of southern China. It simply deserves the same care as any warming, alcohol-containing tonic.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is chicken wine soup made of?

The core is chicken, Chinese rice wine (huang jiu), fresh ginger, and toasted sesame oil, often with jujube dates, dang gui, and goji berries for a more tonic character.

Can you eat chicken wine soup while breastfeeding?

Traditional southern Chinese custom serves it to nursing mothers during confinement. Because huang jiu contains alcohol and alcohol passes into breast milk, modern guidance is cautious: many families simmer it longer or use a reduced-wine version and time feeds carefully.

Is chicken wine soup suitable during pregnancy?

It is generally not recommended, since Chinese rice wine is 10–20% alcohol and simmering does not remove all of it. The alcohol-free version, made with stock and extra ginger, keeps the warming character without the wine.

How often should you eat chicken wine soup?

During the traditional postpartum month it may be eaten several times a week. As an everyday winter dish, two or three servings a week is a reasonable rhythm, with one bowl at a sitting.

Who should avoid chicken wine soup?

People who must avoid alcohol, anyone who reacts poorly to it, those with clear heat signs such as thirst, mouth ulcers, or restlessness, and anyone who finds rich, oily food hard to digest. Children should only have an alcohol-free version.
