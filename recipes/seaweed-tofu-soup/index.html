Seaweed and Tofu Soup — A Light Yin-Nourishing Classic

🥘 Recipe · 6 min read

Some soups announce themselves with a deep, hours-long simmer. Seaweed and tofu soup is the opposite. It comes together in a little over twenty minutes, tastes clean and savoury, and has quietly held a place on Chinese dinner tables for centuries — not because it is dramatic, but because it does something useful. In the language of Chinese food therapy (食疗, shí liáo), this is a soup you reach for when the body feels overheated, dried out, or weighed down by one too many heavy meals.

Two humble ingredients carry the whole dish. Seaweed brings the ocean's salty minerality and a cooling, softening quality. Tofu brings gentle, easily digested protein and a moistening nature. Together they form a light broth that TCM tradition describes as yin-nourishing and dampness-dispersing — a bowl that supports the body without asking much of the digestion.

Why This Soup Has Endured for Centuries

Coastal China has always eaten from the sea, and seaweed entered the kitchen as both food and remedy. In classic materia medica texts such as the Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》), sea vegetables are grouped among ingredients believed to soften hardness and disperse accumulations. Pairing them with tofu was practical as much as therapeutic: tofu was cheap, widely made, and absorbed the flavour of the broth beautifully.

The result is a dish with an unusually gentle profile. It is low in oil, quick to digest, and warming or cooling depending on what you add to the pot. That flexibility is why this soup shows up in home cooking across seasons rather than being locked to a single time of year.

The TCM Properties of Seaweed (Hǎi Dài, 海带)

In TCM theory, seaweed is classified as cold in thermal nature and salty in flavour. The salty flavour is traditionally associated with the kidneys, and seaweed is believed to enter the liver, stomach, and kidney meridians.

Traditionally, seaweed is used to:

  • Clear heat — particularly heat that shows up as a dry throat, restlessness, or a feeling of internal warmth.
  • Soften hardness and disperse nodules — a classical description that covers the traditional use of sea vegetables for swelling and congestion.
  • Resolve phlegm and move fluids — which is where its reputation for gently addressing dampness comes from.
  • Support the kidneys — through the salty flavour's traditional affinity with that organ system.

Modern nutrition adds its own language to the same ingredient: seaweed is naturally rich in iodine, a mineral the thyroid gland uses to make hormones, and it also supplies soluble fibre and a range of trace minerals. Anyone with a thyroid condition should treat seaweed as a food to discuss with their doctor rather than a daily staple — a point we return to below.

The TCM Properties of Tofu (Dòu Fu, 豆腐)

Tofu is classified as cool to neutral in thermal nature with a sweet flavour, and it is traditionally associated with the stomach, spleen, and large intestine meridians. It is regarded as one of the gentlest proteins in the Chinese kitchen.

In traditional use, tofu is believed to clear heat, generate fluids, and tonify the spleen and stomach. Because it is soft and moist, it is often reached for when the body is dry or when digestion is too weak for heavier meats. In food therapy terms, tofu nourishes yin while staying light enough not to create dampness of its own — a rare combination.

Why Seaweed and Tofu Work So Well Together

Read the two profiles side by side and the logic appears quickly. Seaweed is cold, salty, and dispersing; tofu is cool, sweet, and moistening. Seaweed addresses excess and congestion; tofu addresses dryness and deficiency. Neither is heavy. In TCM terms, the pairing is a small, self-balancing system — the seaweed keeps the tofu from being cloying, and the tofu keeps the seaweed's coldness and salinity from being harsh.

The culinary result is equally balanced. Tofu is bland on its own and acts as a sponge for the broth. Seaweed delivers the savoury depth that makes a two-ingredient soup taste like it simmered all afternoon. This is why the duo appears again and again across the site's recipe collection, in dishes such as bok choy and tofu soup and tofu and vegetable soup, where it plays a supporting rather than starring role.

Seaweed and Tofu Soup: The Full Recipe

Ingredients (Serves 4)

  • 15 g (about 1 cup loosely packed) dried wakame or dried kelp (kombu), or 150 g fresh seaweed
  • 400 g soft or medium-firm tofu, cut into 2 cm cubes
  • 1 litre water or light unsalted vegetable stock
  • 3 slices fresh ginger (optional but recommended for balance)
  • 2 spring onions (scallions), white parts sliced, green parts reserved for garnish
  • 1 teaspoon light soy sauce, or to taste
  • ½ teaspoon toasted sesame oil
  • ¼ teaspoon white pepper, or to taste
  • Salt if needed (remember: seaweed is already salty)
  • Optional: 4 shiitake mushrooms, thinly sliced

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Rehydrate the seaweed. Place the dried seaweed in a bowl of cold water for 5–10 minutes until it unfurls and softens. Drain, rinse briefly under running water to remove any grit, and cut into bite-sized pieces. If you are using fresh seaweed, simply rinse and cut.
  2. Build the base. Pour the water or stock into a medium pot and add the ginger slices and the white parts of the spring onion. Bring to a gentle simmer over medium heat and let it infuse for 5 minutes — this is the step that keeps the finished soup from tasting flat.
  3. Add the seaweed. Stir in the seaweed and simmer for 5 minutes. The broth will begin to take on a faint briny sweetness.
  4. Slide in the tofu. Gently add the tofu cubes. Do not stir vigorously — tofu breaks easily. Simmer uncovered for 5–7 minutes so the cubes warm through and absorb the broth.
  5. Season at the end. Turn off the heat, then add the soy sauce, sesame oil, and white pepper. Taste before adding salt; seaweed varies widely in salinity.
  6. Serve. Ladle into bowls and scatter the reserved green spring onion over the top. Serve hot as a light first course or alongside steamed rice.

Variations and Adaptations

  • Warming version (for cold constitutions): Increase the ginger to 5 slices, add 2 cloves of crushed garlic and a pinch of white pepper, and finish with a few drops of chilli oil. This tempers the cooling nature of the seaweed and tofu.
  • Dampness-clearing version: Add 30 g of Job's tears (coix seed) or a handful of adzuki beans and simmer 25 minutes before adding the seaweed. This leans into the soup's traditional reputation as a light, drying dish.
  • Protein-forward version: Add 100 g of thin pork loin slices or a beaten egg drizzled in at the end for a more substantial meal.
  • Miso version: Dissolve 1 tablespoon of white miso into the pot after the heat is off, for a deeper, rounder flavour.
  • Mushroom version: Simmer sliced shiitake with the ginger base to add an earthy, immune-supporting note. Shiitake is traditionally believed to tonify qi, which softens the soup's cooling profile.

When to Eat This Soup

Seaweed and tofu soup fits most naturally into warm weather and late summer, when appetite drops and heavy food feels like too much. It is also a practical reset after a stretch of rich, oily, or celebratory meals — the kind of food that leaves a person feeling puffy and sluggish the next day.

Because the soup is light and hydrating, it is often served as a first course rather than the whole meal, with rice, a steamed vegetable, and a small amount of protein completing the table. In TCM food therapy, no single dish is meant to carry dinner on its own; balance is built across the meal and across the week.

If you are working through a seasonal transition, this soup pairs neatly with the broader guidance in our spring detox eating plan. Two to three servings a week is a comfortable traditional rhythm.

Who Should Be Careful

The cooling nature of this soup is the whole point — and also the main thing to watch. Individuals with a cold or deficient constitution, who tend to feel cold, have loose stools, low energy, or weak digestion, may find that this soup leaves them feeling chilled or unsettled. If so, either skip it or apply the warming variation above, and enjoy it in smaller portions.

Seaweed deserves its own note. It is naturally high in iodine, and regular large servings may deliver more than some people need. Those with hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, or anyone taking thyroid medication should speak with a qualified healthcare provider before making seaweed a regular part of the diet.

Finally, if you are pregnant or nursing, or managing a chronic condition such as kidney disease or hypertension — where sodium intake matters — check with your healthcare provider, because seaweed is naturally salty. Soy is also a common allergen; replace the tofu with silken egg custard if needed.

As with all TCM food therapy, this soup is a complementary practice. It is believed to support general wellbeing and is not a substitute for diagnosis or treatment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the TCM properties of seaweed?

In TCM, seaweed (海带, hǎi dài) is classified as cold in thermal nature with a salty flavour. It is believed to enter the liver, stomach, and kidney meridians. Traditionally, seaweed is used to soften hardness, resolve phlegm, clear heat, and move fluids — which is why it is associated with gently easing dampness.

What are the TCM properties of tofu?

Tofu (豆腐, dòu fu) is considered cool and neutral with a sweet flavour, and is traditionally associated with the stomach, spleen, and large intestine meridians. It is believed to clear heat, generate fluids, and tonify the spleen and stomach while being gentle enough for everyday eating.

Is this soup good for people who feel heavy or puffy?

Traditionally, yes — this soup is regarded as a light, low-oil dish that may support the body in resolving dampness. Seaweed is believed to soften and disperse, while tofu supplies gentle protein without heaviness. Diet alone is not a weight-management strategy, so treat this as one supportive habit among many.

Who should be careful with seaweed and tofu soup?

Because both ingredients are cooling, people with a cold or deficient constitution may want to limit it or balance it with ginger, scallion, and white pepper. Seaweed is high in iodine, so anyone with a thyroid condition or taking thyroid medication should consult their healthcare provider first.

How often can I eat it?

As a light everyday soup, it is traditionally enjoyed two to three times a week, most often in warm weather or as a reset after rich meals. Because seaweed is iodine-rich, enjoy it in moderation rather than as a daily staple.

Final Thoughts

Seaweed and tofu soup is a reminder that Chinese food therapy does not have to be complicated. Two ingredients, a litre of water, a few slices of ginger, and twenty minutes produce a bowl that is cooling without being harsh, nourishing without being heavy, and deeply savoury without a single drop of stock concentrate.

Make it once and taste how the tofu takes on the sea. Make it a few times and you may notice when your body starts asking for it — usually right when you need something light.
