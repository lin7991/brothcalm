Black Sesame Paste (Hu Ma Hu) — Sweet Kidney-Building Breakfast

🥘 Recipe · 7 min read
Quick answer

Few breakfasts are as comforting as a warm bowl of black sesame paste — in Chinese, hu ma hu (芝麻糊). Smooth, sweet, and deeply fragrant, it is one of the most beloved traditional foods in Cantonese dessert houses and home kitchens alike. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), black sesame is not simply a flavorful seed: it is considered a gentle, everyday tonic for the Kidneys and Liver, and it has been eaten for thousands of years to support lustrous hair, strong bones, and steady energy.

What Is Black Sesame Paste (Hu Ma Hu)?

Hu ma hu is a smooth, pudding-like porridge made by grinding toasted black sesame seeds with rice (or glutinous rice) and sweetener, then simmering the mixture until thick and silky. The name comes from the Chinese words for sesame (hu ma, 芝麻) and paste (hu, 糊). It is a classic in southern Chinese dessert tradition, where it is often served warm alongside red bean paste or sweet almond soup.

Unlike many sweet desserts, hu ma hu is traditionally regarded as a nourishing food rather than an indulgence. Because black sesame is believed to tonify the Kidneys and enrich the Blood, a small bowl in the morning is traditionally seen as a gentle way to start the day with stored vitality — especially for people who feel tired, notice early greying hair, or want extra support during cooler months.

Black Sesame in TCM: Properties and Meridians

In TCM food therapy, every ingredient carries a thermal nature, a taste, and a set of affiliated organ systems (meridians). Black sesame is classified as follows:

  • Nature: neutral to slightly warm (平/微温) — safe for most constitutions across the seasons
  • Taste: sweet (甘) — sweet foods are traditionally linked to harmonizing the Spleen and nourishing the body
  • Meridians: Liver (肝) and Kidney (肾)
  • Actions (in TCM theory): tonifies the Liver and Kidneys, nourishes Blood and Essence (jing), and moistens the Intestines

Because the Kidneys are said to govern the bones, the ears, and the lower back, while Liver Blood is said to nourish the hair and eyes, black sesame is traditionally recommended in TCM for people who want to support hair quality, joint comfort, and overall vitality — always as part of a balanced diet rather than a standalone remedy.

Classical History and Traditional Uses

Black sesame has an unusually long written history in Chinese medicine. It appears in the Shennong Bencao Jing (《神农本草经》), the ancient herbal classic compiled around the Han dynasty, where it is listed among "top grade" (上品) substances — foods and herbs considered safe for long-term daily use. The text describes it as strengthening the body, boosting energy, and nourishing the brain and marrow.

Later texts, including Li Shizhen's Bencao Gangmu (《本草纲目》, 16th century), continue the theme, noting that black sesame is traditionally used to support hair that is thinning or greying early, to moisten dryness, and to gently move the bowels. In folk tradition, black sesame and walnuts are a famous pairing believed to support memory and hair — which is why the two so often appear together in porridges, pastes, and seasonal sweets.

It is worth noting that classical texts describe sesame as a food first and a medicine second. The traditional spirit of hu ma hu is prevention and daily nourishment, not treatment — an approach TCM calls "food as medicine" (yao shan, 药膳).

The Traditional Recipe: Hu Ma Hu

Authentic black sesame paste has two essential elements: toasted sesame for deep aroma, and a small amount of rice or glutinous rice to give the paste its signature velvety texture. Toasting the seeds is not optional — raw sesame tastes flat by comparison, while gentle toasting releases the oils that make hu ma hu fragrant.

Ingredients (serves 2)

  • 1 cup (120 g) raw black sesame seeds
  • 1/4 cup (50 g) white rice or glutinous rice, rinsed and soaked 2 hours (or overnight)
  • 3 cups (700 ml) water
  • 2–3 tbsp rock sugar or brown sugar, to taste
  • Optional: 1/2 cup milk or oat milk for a creamier finish

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Toast the sesame. Place the black sesame seeds in a dry skillet over medium-low heat. Stir constantly for 3–5 minutes until fragrant and the seeds begin to pop softly. Remove immediately to a plate to cool — sesame burns quickly and turns bitter.
  2. Grind the sesame. Once cool, grind the toasted seeds in a blender or food processor until they form a fine, slightly oily powder. For the smoothest result, blend with a little of the measured water.
  3. Blend with rice. Drain the soaked rice and add it to the blender with the sesame and 1 cup of water. Blend on high for 1–2 minutes until completely smooth.
  4. Simmer. Pour the mixture into a saucepan, add the remaining water, and bring to a gentle simmer over medium heat, whisking constantly to prevent sticking.
  5. Sweeten. After 8–10 minutes, when the paste has thickened to a silky porridge, stir in the rock sugar until dissolved. Add milk if you prefer a creamier bowl.
  6. Serve warm. Ladle into bowls and enjoy immediately. A small sprinkle of toasted sesame or crushed walnuts on top adds texture.

Recipe Variations

  • Walnut sesame paste: Replace one-third of the sesame with raw walnuts for the classic memory-and-hair pairing.
  • Red date sweetness: Simmer 3–4 pitted red dates (jujube) with the paste and blend before serving for natural sweetness and extra Blood-nourishing character.
  • Goji twist: Stir a small handful of soaked goji berries into the warm paste — a popular combination in TCM-inspired breakfast bowls.
  • No-rice version: For a lighter texture, use only sesame, water, and sweetener, and thicken with a little arrowroot starch.

Wellness Benefits — What TCM and Modern Nutrition Say

In TCM tradition, hu ma hu is eaten for three main purposes. Here is how practitioners describe them, alongside what modern nutritional research observes.

May Support Hair Quality and Scalp Health

Traditional use: black sesame is one of the most famous TCM foods for hair — traditionally recommended when hair is dry, brittle, or greying early, because it is believed to nourish Liver Blood and Kidney Essence. Modern note: sesame seeds are a concentrated source of copper, zinc, iron, and healthy fats, nutrients involved in normal hair structure and pigmentation. Eating it as food may support overall nutrient intake, though no food alone can reverse hair loss.

Traditionally Used to Nourish the Kidneys and Support Vitality

Traditional use: because black sesame enters the Kidney meridian, TCM texts describe it as supporting the lower back, knees, and the body's deep energy reserves — useful for people who feel fatigued or depleted. Modern note: sesame provides quality plant protein, magnesium, and B vitamins that contribute to normal energy metabolism. A warm, nutrient-dense breakfast is also a practical way to start the day steadily.

May Support Bone and Joint Comfort

Traditional use: the Kidneys are said to govern the bones, so Kidney-nourishing foods like black sesame are traditionally included in diets for aging gracefully. Modern note: unhulled sesame is rich in calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium — key minerals for maintaining bone density. Grinding the seeds (as hu ma hu does) makes these minerals more accessible than eating whole seeds.

A Gentle Way to Moisturize and Support Digestion

Traditional use: black sesame is considered moistening, and is traditionally used to ease dry stools and dry skin, especially in older adults. Modern note: the seeds' natural oils act as a mild lubricant in the digestive tract. For best tolerance, start with a small serving and drink enough water through the day.

Precautions and Who Should Be Cautious

Hu ma hu is a food, not a medicine, and for most people it is perfectly safe in normal breakfast portions. Still, a few groups should be mindful:

  • People with loose stools or Spleen deficiency: because black sesame is moistening and oily, TCM tradition advises people who already have loose stools or weak digestion to eat it in small amounts.
  • Anyone watching calories: sesame is calorie-dense, and the paste often includes sugar — a small bowl is usually enough.
  • Nut and seed allergies: sesame is a common allergen. If you have never eaten it, try a tiny amount first.
  • People on blood-thinning medication: sesame is a source of vitamin K, which plays a role in blood clotting. Keep portions consistent and consult your healthcare provider if this applies to you.
  • Infants: whole seeds and thick pastes are a choking hazard for very young children; smooth, well-thinned paste may be offered only with professional guidance.

As with any traditional food therapy, hu ma hu is a complement to — never a replacement for — professional medical care. If you are managing a chronic condition, are pregnant, or are unsure whether sesame suits you, talk to a qualified healthcare provider first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I eat black sesame paste every day?

Yes, in traditional practice a small daily serving is common — typically 2–3 tablespoons of sesame per person. Because the paste is calorie-dense and naturally sweet, most TCM-oriented nutrition guidance suggests treating it as a nourishing treat rather than a large staple, and balancing it with vegetables, protein, and whole grains across the day.

Does black sesame really help with grey hair?

In TCM tradition, black sesame is believed to nourish the Kidney and Liver systems that classical theory links to hair health, and it has been used for centuries as part of diets for premature greying. Modern research has not proven that any food can reverse greying, but sesame's copper, iron, and B-vitamin content supports the nutrients involved in normal hair pigment. It is best seen as one supportive habit, not a guaranteed fix.

Who should not eat black sesame paste?

Traditional guidance suggests people with chronic loose stools or Spleen deficiency eat it in moderation, since sesame is moistening and oily. People with sesame allergy should avoid it entirely, and anyone on blood-thinning medication should keep intake consistent and check with their doctor. Infants should not be given whole seeds or thick paste.

Is store-bought black sesame paste as good as homemade?

Good-quality store-bought versions can be convenient and delicious, but many contain added sugar, maltodextrin, and only a modest amount of sesame. Homemade hu ma hu lets you control the sweetness, use toasted seeds at their freshest, and adjust the texture — and it takes only about 20 minutes from start to finish once the rice is soaked.
