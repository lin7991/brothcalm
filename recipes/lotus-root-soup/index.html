Lotus Root Soup (Ou Tang): A Gentle Autumn Recipe

🥘 Recipe · 8 min read
Quick answer

Lotus root soup is a mild, savory-sweet way to use fresh lotus root in a warming meal; add pork ribs for a fuller broth or keep it plant-based with mushrooms. In traditional Chinese food therapy, lotus root is discussed differently depending on whether it is raw or cooked, but these ideas are cultural frameworks—not medical effects. Start with a modest bowl alongside a meal and choose the version whose texture and richness you enjoy.

Is this guide for you?

This recipe suits cooks who have fresh lotus root and want a simple soup rather than a medicinal formula. It is useful when comparing a light vegetable broth with a heartier pork soup. For a grain-based meal, see congee; for another mellow soup, compare Chinese yam and goji soup.

The decision

  • Choose this if: you like crisp-tender roots and a gently sweet broth.
  • Consider another option if: you want creamy soup, dislike lotus root’s texture, or need a very quick meal.
  • Start with: the vegetarian pot below; add pork only for a richer broth.

TCM lens: tradition, not anatomy

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) food descriptions often list lotus root as sweet and associate it with Lung, Stomach, and Liver channel frameworks. Traditional practice distinguishes raw from cooked root: raw is commonly described as cooler, while cooking is said to make it gentler. 性、味、归经 are traditional classification terms, not claims about modern anatomy. Qi, heat, and balance likewise belong to this traditional model.

Modern food lens

As food, lotus root provides carbohydrate and fiber and contributes crunch and mild sweetness. Nutrition varies with size, storage, and preparation. A bowl of soup should not be presented as a treatment or guaranteed health outcome. Sodium and richness depend largely on stock, seasoning, and optional meat.

Try it once: simple lotus root soup

3–4 servings: 400 g fresh lotus root, 1.2 L water or low-sodium vegetable stock, 5 dried shiitake mushrooms (optional), 3 ginger slices, and salt to taste. For meat, add about 300 g pork ribs, blanched first. Rinse and peel root if desired; cut into 1 cm slices and briefly soak in cool water. Simmer mushrooms and ginger for 10 minutes, add lotus root, then cook gently 25–35 minutes until tender but intact. Salt near the end. With ribs, simmer until fully cooked and tender, handling raw meat safely.

Serve warm with lunch or dinner. Follow package directions for dried lotus root. Make a small batch and notice whether you prefer thinner slices, more ginger, or less salt. Refrigerate leftovers promptly and reheat until steaming hot.

Troubleshooting and personalization

  • Too firm? Simmer longer; older roots may take more time.
  • Flat broth? Add a little ginger or mushroom, then adjust salt gradually.
  • Want lighter? Skip ribs, use low-sodium stock, and pair with rice or greens.
  • Root darkens? Brief soaking can slow browning; discoloration alone does not indicate a medicinal property.

Specific safety boundaries

Wash fresh produce; discard moldy, slimy, or off-smelling pieces. Keep raw pork and utensils separate from ready-to-eat ingredients and cook thoroughly. Avoid allergens; omit peanuts when needed. If pregnant, managing a medical condition, or following a prescribed diet, ask your clinician about relevant restrictions. This is food information, not a substitute for medical assessment or care.

Frequently asked questions

Can I make it without meat?

Yes. Mushrooms and ginger make a satisfying plant-based broth.

How often can I eat it?

Enjoy it as an ordinary meal; no therapeutic schedule is established here.

Can I prepare it ahead?

Yes. Refrigerate promptly and reheat thoroughly; the root softens as it sits.
