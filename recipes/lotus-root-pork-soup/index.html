Lotus Root and Pork Rib Soup — Autumn Comfort Broth

🥘 Recipe · 8 min read

When the leaves turn and the air carries the first real chill of autumn, many kitchens across China reach for the same pot. Lotus root and pork rib soup (lián'ǒu páigǔ tāng, 莲藕排骨汤) is one of the most beloved seasonal broths in Chinese home cooking — humble ingredients, an unhurried simmer, and a sweet, mellow broth that seems to hold the whole season in one bowl.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), autumn is the season of the Lungs and of dryness. As the weather cools, it also becomes drier, and that dryness is believed to affect the body's fluids — showing up as a scratchy throat, dry lips, rough skin, or a cough that lingers. Lotus root and pork rib soup is traditionally regarded as a gentle answer to all of this: the lotus root moistens, the ribs nourish, and the slow-cooked broth restores a sense of deep comfort. In this guide, we explore the TCM properties behind each ingredient, who may benefit most, and exactly how to make this autumn classic at home.

Lotus Root in TCM: Sweet, Cool, and Moistening

Lotus root (lián'ǒu, 莲藕) is the crisp rhizome of the lotus plant, and it has been used in Chinese food therapy for centuries. In TCM terms, fresh lotus root has a sweet flavor and a cool nature, and it is traditionally said to enter the Lung, Stomach, and Liver meridians.

What makes lotus root unusual is how its character changes with cooking:

  • Raw lotus root is cooling and fluid-generating. Thinly sliced or juiced, it is traditionally used to clear heat, generate fluids, cool the blood, and ease a dry, irritated throat. It is also associated with stopping certain types of bleeding, such as nosebleeds or gum bleeding in heat patterns.
  • Cooked lotus root becomes warming and tonifying. Once simmered, its nature shifts toward the Spleen and Stomach — it is believed to strengthen digestion, support the Spleen's transformation of food into qi and blood, and gently nourish. This is the form used in soup.

Because cooked lotus root is rich in starch and fiber, it also gives the broth body without heaviness, and the slices hold their shape beautifully after a long simmer — soft enough to bite, firm enough to enjoy.

Pork Ribs in TCM: A Gentle Yin-Nourishing Meat

Pork (zhū ròu, 猪肉) is viewed differently from chicken or beef in TCM. It has a sweet and salty flavor and a neutral to slightly cooling nature, and it is traditionally said to enter the Spleen, Stomach, and Kidney meridians.

Its chief reputation is as a yin-nourishing and fluid-generating food. In TCM theory, pork is believed to moisten dryness, nourish the blood, and support the body's essential fluids — which makes it a natural partner for dry autumn weather. The ribs themselves, with their surrounding meat and marrow, are also considered mildly supportive to the Kidney and its store of essence (jīng, 精), which in TCM is linked to the bones and to deep vitality.

Because pork is moistening rather than heavily warming, it suits people who feel dry but do not need aggressive heat-clearing — exactly the profile of many autumn discomforts.

Why This Pairing Works for Autumn

Put the two together and you have a study in balance:

  • Lotus root addresses the Lungs and dryness. Its cool, moistening character is traditionally used to protect the fluids of the Lungs — the organ most vulnerable to autumn dryness.
  • Pork ribs anchor with gentle nourishment. The ribs provide a mild, neutral source of yin and fluids, keeping the soup from being overly cooling and giving it lasting depth.
  • The slow broth concentrates the benefit. Long simmering draws minerals and gelatinous richness from the bones, producing a broth that is easy to digest and traditionally regarded as restorative for the Stomach and Spleen.

The result is a soup that is neither aggressively hot nor strongly cold — in TCM language, it is harmonizing, which is exactly what many people need as the seasons shift.

Complete Recipe: Lotus Root and Pork Rib Soup

This version follows the classic Cantonese-style clear broth. It needs almost no skill — only patience and a good pot.

Ingredients

  • 500 g pork ribs (meaty spare ribs work best), cut into 5 cm pieces
  • 500 g fresh lotus root, about 2 medium segments
  • 1 thumb-sized piece of fresh ginger, sliced
  • 3 dried red dates (jujube), pitted
  • 1 tablespoon goji berries (optional)
  • 2.5 liters cold water
  • Salt, to taste

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Blanch the ribs. Rinse the ribs, place them in a pot with cold water, and bring to a boil. Let them bubble for 3 minutes, then drain and rinse off the scum. This step makes the final broth clean and clear.
  2. Prepare the lotus root. Peel the lotus root and cut it into 1 cm thick rounds. If the holes look muddy, rinse them under running water. Soak the slices in cold water with a splash of vinegar until cooking — this keeps them from darkening.
  3. Start the soup. Add the blanched ribs, lotus root slices, ginger, and red dates to a large pot. Pour in 2.5 liters of cold water and bring to a boil over high heat.
  4. Skim and simmer. Skim off any foam that rises, then lower the heat to a gentle simmer. Cover and cook for about 1.5 hours, until the lotus root is tender and the ribs are soft enough to pull apart.
  5. Finish and season. Add the goji berries in the last 10 minutes, if using. Season with salt to taste, skim off excess surface fat, and serve warm with the lotus root and ribs in each bowl.

Cook's Notes

  • Choose your lotus root by color. In Chinese markets, pale, moist lotus root with clean holes is best for soup. Very starchy root goes slightly floury when cooked, which many people enjoy; crisper root stays crunchier.
  • Cold water, always. Start the ribs in cold water and bring everything up slowly — this draws out impurities gradually and yields a clearer broth.
  • Skim twice. Skim at the boil and again halfway through simmering for the cleanest, most comfortable bowl.

Three Variations Worth Trying

1. Lotus Root and Peanut Pork Rib Soup

Add 100 g of raw peanuts (skins on) with the ribs. Peanuts are traditionally considered spleen-supporting and are believed to help the soup moisten without becoming greasy. Simmer 30 minutes longer.

2. Lotus Root, Corn, and Carrot Soup

Add one sweet corn cob cut into rounds and one carrot cut into chunks. Corn is sweet and neutral, and in TCM is believed to support the Spleen and Stomach; the vegetables make the broth lighter and naturally sweet — a favorite for children.

3. Red Date and Goji Version

For a more blood-nourishing slant, increase the red dates to 6 and the goji berries to 2 tablespoons. Red dates tonify qi and blood, while goji berries nourish Liver and Kidney yin — a traditional combination for those who feel tired as well as dry.

When to Drink It and How Often

Lotus root and pork rib soup is at its most traditional from early autumn through early winter, when dryness is the dominant climate influence. In TCM seasonal eating, matching the season's challenges with food is a core principle: autumn asks for moisture, and this soup answers it.

For most people, once or twice a week is a sensible rhythm. It makes an excellent weekend lunch or a gentle dinner, and the leftovers keep well in the refrigerator for two to three days. Because the broth is rich, a serving of about one to one and a half bowls is plenty for most adults.

Precautions and Who Should Be Cautious

Lotus root and pork rib soup is a mild, everyday food for most people, but a few groups are traditionally advised to take care:

  • Richness and metabolic concerns. Because pork ribs are fatty, people managing gout, high blood lipids, or weight should skim the fat thoroughly, remove the skin if desired, and keep portions moderate.
  • Cold-damp digestion. Those prone to loose stools, bloating, or a heavy, sluggish feeling are traditionally advised not to overdo rich meat broths. Keep the ginger generous, eat the cooked lotus root in moderation, and watch how your body responds.
  • Warming needs. If you feel deeply cold all the time, this neutral soup is not the most warming choice — you may prefer ginger-heavy chicken soup or lamb dishes in deep winter.
  • Pregnancy. The soup is commonly enjoyed during pregnancy, but every pregnancy is different — confirm with your healthcare provider, especially regarding salt and portion size.

As always in TCM food therapy, the best guide is your own body. If the soup leaves you comfortable, warm, and settled, autumn has found its bowl.
