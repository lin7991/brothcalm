Five-Red Soup (Wu Hong Tang) — The Classic Blood-Building Dessert Soup

🥘 Recipe · 8 min read

In the home kitchens of southern China, few bowls carry as much quiet symbolism as wǔ hóng tāng (五红汤) — Five-Red Soup. Five ingredients, five shades of crimson and russet, simmered into a sweet, glossy dessert soup that grandmothers have handed down for generations. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), red foods have long been linked with Blood, and this humble soup is one of the most beloved "blood-building" tonics in the Chinese food-therapy tradition. It is offered after childbirth, after a long illness, and on cold evenings — a warm bowl of nourishment that is traditionally believed to help restore color, energy, and a sense of calm.

Unlike bitter medicinal brews, Five-Red Soup tastes like a treat. That is precisely the point: in TCM, one of the gentlest ways to support the body is through the everyday foods we actually enjoy. Below, we unpack the energetics of each of the five reds, the logic of the formula, and a complete step-by-step recipe you can make at home.

TCM Properties: Meet the Five Reds

Red dates (hóng zǎo, 红枣)

Red dates are the sweet heart of the soup. In TCM they are classified as sweet and warm, entering the Spleen and Stomach channels. They are traditionally used to tonify Qi, nourish Blood, and calm the Shen (spirit), which is why they appear so often in restorative recipes for fatigue, poor appetite, and restless sleep. Their dense natural sugars also make Five-Red Soup taste rich without needing much added sweetener. Learn more in our jujube date ingredient guide.

Goji berries (gǒuqǐ zǐ, 枸杞子)

Goji berries are sweet and neutral in TCM, entering the Liver and Kidney channels. They are traditionally considered one of the finest gentle blood and Jing (essence) nourishers, and are commonly paired with red dates in restorative cooking. Goji also have a long association with eye health and with supporting calm, grounded energy. See our goji berry guide for selection and storage tips.

Red-skinned peanuts (hóng yī huāshēng, 红衣花生)

Peanuts with their papery red skin intact are the third red. In Chinese food therapy the kernel is sweet and neutral, entering the Spleen and Lung channels, where it is traditionally used to support digestion and moisture. The red skin holds a special reputation: folk TCM considers it a gentle supporter of the Blood. Look for raw peanuts that still carry their red skins, and keep them whole when cooking.

Red beans (hóng dòu, 红豆)

Red beans (adzuki beans) are sweet and slightly sour with a neutral temperature in TCM. They are best known as a gentle way to help the body drain dampness and support healthy digestion and urination. In Five-Red Soup they play a quiet but essential role: their earthiness balances the sweetness of the dates and sugar, so the rich nourishment is easier for the Spleen to process without leaving you feeling heavy. Our red bean guide covers how to prepare them.

Brown sugar (hóng táng, 红糖)

The fifth red is not an herb at all — it is unrefined cane sugar that keeps its deep molasses color and mineral-rich character. In TCM, brown sugar is classified as sweet and warm, entering the Spleen and Stomach channels. It is traditionally used to warm the Middle Burner and harmonize a formula, which is why it is so often stirred into ginger tea and postpartum broths. Some regional versions of Five-Red Soup use rock sugar instead for a milder, less warming finish — see our ginger brown sugar tea guide for another classic use of this warming sweetener.

Why Five Reds? The Logic Behind the Formula

Five-Red Soup is not a random collection of red foods — it follows recognizable TCM principles. In Five Element theory, red is the color associated with the Heart and with Blood, so a formula built from red ingredients is, by tradition, a formula directed at the blood and circulation. But the deeper logic is energetic.

In TCM, Blood is not made from iron supplements alone: it is refined from the Qi of food, transformed by the Spleen and Stomach. That is why the classic blood-supporting kitchen remedies are sweet, warm, and easy to digest — they nourish the very organs that manufacture Blood. Five-Red Soup stacks two gentle blood nourishers (dates and goji), a Spleen-tonifying kernel that protects digestion (peanuts), a mild dampness drainer that keeps the bowl from becoming cloying (red beans), and a warming harmonizer (brown sugar). The result is a balanced, moderately warming tonic that supports the Spleen while it nourishes the Blood. Traditional Uses: When This Soup Is Traditionally Reached For

Five-Red Soup belongs to the Chinese tradition of diàn bǔ — gentle, food-based nourishment for people who are run down rather than acutely ill. Families traditionally prepare it in these situations:

  • After childbirth. New mothers are considered depleted in both Qi and Blood after delivery, and warm sweet soups are a staple of the Chinese postpartum month. See our postpartum food therapy guide for the wider tradition.
  • Around heavy or tiring periods. Because it is traditionally regarded as a blood nourisher, it is often served in the days after menstruation to support recovery. More in foods for menstrual health.
  • When someone looks "blood deficient." In TCM terms this can mean a pale complexion, fatigue, dizziness, scanty sleep, or cold hands — the pattern is discussed in our TCM foods for anemia guide.
  • During convalescence and in cold weather. The soup is warming and comforting, and it is a familiar sight on winter tables and during recovery from exhausting illness.

As with all TCM food therapy, Five-Red Soup is a supportive food, not a treatment. It works best as part of a varied, balanced diet and a sensible rhythm of rest and activity.

The Complete Recipe: Five-Red Soup (Wu Hong Tang)

This is the classic home version — forgiving, flexible, and easy to scale. The key steps are soaking the beans and peanuts in advance and adding the goji berries only at the very end so they keep their shape.

Ingredients

  • 10 red dates (jujube), pitted and halved
  • 2 tablespoons goji berries
  • 60 g (about ¼ cup) raw peanuts with red skin
  • 60 g (about ⅓ cup) dried red beans (adzuki beans)
  • 2–3 tablespoons brown sugar, to taste
  • 1.5 liters (about 6 cups) water

Method

  1. Soak. Rinse the red beans and peanuts, then soak them in cool water for at least 4 hours — overnight is best. This softens them and shortens the cooking time.
  2. Simmer the beans and peanuts. Drain and add them to a pot with the water. Bring to a boil over high heat, then reduce to a gentle simmer, partially covered, for 40–50 minutes until the beans are tender.
  3. Add the dates. Stir in the halved red dates and simmer for another 15 minutes, until they are plump and soft.
  4. Finish with goji and sugar. Add the goji berries and brown sugar, stir, and simmer for 5 more minutes. Taste and adjust the sweetness.
  5. Serve warm. Ladle into bowls and enjoy while warm. The soup keeps in the refrigerator for 2–3 days and reheats gently on the stove.

Variations and serving notes

  • Add longan. A handful of dried longan (about 8–10 pieces) deepens the sweetness and is a classic pairing with dates for calm, restorative energy. See our longan guide.
  • Add an egg. Some families poach an egg in the finished soup for extra protein — a favorite during the postpartum month.
  • Swap the sweetener. Use rock sugar instead of brown sugar for a milder, less warming bowl.
  • Scale it. Double the recipe and refrigerate; reheat individual portions as needed.

Who Should Be Cautious (and When to Skip It)

Five-Red Soup is gentle, but it is sweet and warming, and it is not for every constitution or every moment. Traditional cautions include:

  • Blood sugar concerns. The dates and brown sugar make this a high-sugar dish. People with diabetes or insulin resistance should enjoy it rarely, in small portions, or skip the added sugar entirely.
  • Damp-heat and phlegm. If you feel heavy, bloated, congested, or have a thick yellow coating on the tongue — signs TCM reads as damp-heat — a rich sweet soup may not be the best choice. It is better suited to a cooler, drier day.
  • Active fever or acute illness. During a cold with fever or sore throat, TCM generally favors lighter, clearer foods; save the warming tonic for recovery.
  • Peanut allergy. Peanuts are a common allergen — omit them (the soup still works with the remaining four reds plus a few more dates).
  • Pregnancy. Many traditions reserve this soup for after childbirth. If pregnant, consult your healthcare provider before making it a regular part of your diet.

When in doubt, start with one small bowl and notice how you feel. Food therapy is personal, and your own body is the best guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drink Five-Red Soup every day? Traditionally it is treated as a nourishing tonic rather than an everyday drink — a small bowl two to four times a week is the customary pattern. Because it is sweet and warming, daily large servings can feel heavy and may not suit everyone.

Is Five-Red Soup good for anemia? In TCM it is traditionally used to support the pattern called Blood deficiency — fatigue, pallor, dizziness — as a nourishing everyday food. It is not a treatment for clinically diagnosed anemia; if you suspect anemia, see a doctor for testing and follow their advice alongside any dietary support.

Can I drink Five-Red Soup during pregnancy? The tradition is to serve it after childbirth, when the body is depleted. Pregnancy is a time of shifting constitution, so check with your healthcare provider before making this sweet warming soup a regular habit.

Can I make it without sugar? Yes — the red dates provide plenty of natural sweetness. Omit the brown sugar, or swap in a little rock sugar. The texture will be lighter but the soup remains warming and nourishing.

What else can I add? Dried longan, a poached egg, black sticky rice, or a switch to rock sugar are all traditional variations. Keep the base of dates, goji, peanuts, and beans, and adjust around your own taste and constitution.
