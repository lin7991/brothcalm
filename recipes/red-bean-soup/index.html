Red Bean Soup (Hong Dou Tang) — Clearing Dampness in a Bowl

🥘 Recipe · 6 min read

Why Red Bean Soup Is the Classic Damp-Clearing Bowl

If there is one bowl Chinese families reach for when the body feels heavy, puffy, and slow, it is hong dou tang — red bean soup. Long before it became a comforting dessert, it was a kitchen standby of TCM food therapy: a small, quietly sweet legume simmered in water until the broth turns faintly pink, taken for how it makes the body feel afterwards.

In TCM theory, that heavy, sluggish feeling is described as dampness (湿, shi) — fluid that the Spleen and its transforming functions have not moved along as efficiently as they should. Adzuki bean is traditionally regarded as one of the gentlest ingredients for encouraging that movement: it drains slowly and steadily rather than forcefully, which is why it appears in everyday soups rather than dramatic preparations. If dampness is a new idea for you, start with our explainer on dampness in TCM.

Red bean soup is also forgiving: it takes well to sweet and savoury preparations alike, works alongside most other damp-clearing ingredients, and costs very little.

A Note on the Bean — Hong Dou vs. Adzuki

  • 赤小豆 (chi xiao dou) — the smaller, slimmer medicinal red bean. Firmer, less sweet, and slower to soften. This is the bean classical texts describe for food therapy, and the traditional choice for dampness.
  • 红豆 (hong dou) — the rounder adzuki bean sold in most Western supermarkets. Sweeter and starchier, it breaks down into a soft paste, which is why it stars in syrupy dessert soups.

Either works below, and many households mix them: use chi xiao dou for a lighter savoury broth, the rounder adzuki for a thick dessert soup. See our red bean ingredient guide for more on buying and storing.

TCM Properties of Red Bean

Traditional references describe adzuki bean with a consistent profile.

Property Detail What it means in practice
Nature (性) Neutral, leaning slightly cool Gentle enough for regular use; the cool lean suits warm, humid weather.
Flavour (味) Sweet and sour Sweet is said to harmonise the middle; the sour note is mildly astringing.
Meridians (归经) Heart, Small Intestine, Spleen Links the bean to the body's fluid pathways and to draining downward.
Direction of action Descending, draining Traditionally is believed to move water downward, not to warm or tonify.

Nature: Neutral with a Cool Lean

Adzuki bean is not a cold ingredient in the way mung bean is. It sits close to neutral with a slight cool lean, so it suits a wider range of constitutions than purely cooling foods. It is used more often in humid weather, and in cold weather is usually cooked with ginger or chen pi to balance that lean.

Flavour: Sweet and Sour

In the five-flavour framework, sweet is associated with the Spleen and Stomach and with a nourishing, harmonising quality, while sour is linked with the Liver and a gentle astringing effect. Together they are said to give adzuki bean its balance: it drains without scraping the body out.

Meridians: Heart, Small Intestine, Spleen

The Small Intestine meridian is traditionally linked with separating clear and turbid fluids, and the Spleen with their transformation and transport — the theoretical basis for adzuki bean's association with dampness. Its link to the Heart is why it also appears when restlessness sits alongside fluid stagnation.

Traditional Uses and Classical References

Red bean has one of the longest documented histories of any food in the Chinese materia medica. The Shennong Bencao Jing (《神农本草经》), compiled in the Han dynasty, lists 赤小豆 among its substances, describing it in terms of encouraging the free passage of water. Later, Li Shizhen's Ben Cao Gang Mu (《本草纲目》, 1578) gives the bean a detailed entry, noting its use in soups and gruels and its pairing with chen pi, job's tears, and ginger.

The practical uses recorded since have stayed remarkably consistent:

  • Dampness and heaviness — the classic application, especially in humid weather and late summer.
  • Puffiness and swelling sensations — traditionally addressed with red bean soups and congees, often with job's tears or poria.
  • Digestive sluggishness — a light soup is traditionally taken when the appetite feels dull and the abdomen full and heavy.

These are traditional uses within TCM theory, not medical outcomes.

Red Bean Soup Recipe (Hong Dou Tang)

This is the everyday version — a plain, gently savoury-sweet broth that forms the base for both the dessert and the savoury variations further down.

Ingredients

Ingredient Metric US measure
Dried adzuki or chi xiao dou beans 200 g 1 cup
Water (for cooking) 1.5 L 6 cups
Dried chen pi (tangerine peel), optional but recommended 5 g, about 2 strips 1 tbsp, torn
Fresh ginger, bruised (for cooler weather) 10 g 2 thin slices
Rock sugar, for the sweet version 40–60 g 3–4 tbsp, to taste
Fine salt, for the savoury version 2 g ⅓ tsp

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Rinse and sort. Rinse the beans under cold water and pick out any stones or shrivelled beans.
  2. Soak (optional). Cover with water and soak 2–4 hours, or overnight; this shortens cooking and gives a clearer broth. Discard the soaking water and rinse.
  3. Start the pot. Put the beans in a heavy pot with the 1.5 L (6 cups) of fresh water. Add the chen pi and ginger if using, and bring to a rolling boil over high heat.
  4. Simmer. Reduce the heat to low, cover with the lid slightly ajar, and simmer gently 60–75 minutes, until the beans are soft and beginning to split. Stir occasionally and top up with hot water as needed.
  5. Adjust the texture. For a brothy soup, stop here. For a thicker, dessert-like consistency, simmer uncovered another 20–30 minutes, pressing some beans against the side of the pot.
  6. Season. For the sweet version, stir in the rock sugar off the heat and let it dissolve. For the savoury version, season with salt instead and simmer two minutes more.
  7. Rest and serve. Take the pot off the heat and rest covered 10 minutes. Serve warm; the soup keeps 3 days refrigerated and freezes up to 2 months.

Cooking Time at a Glance

  • Pressure cooker: 25–30 minutes at high pressure, plus natural release.
  • Stovetop, soaked beans: 60–75 minutes.
  • Stovetop, unsoaked beans: 90–120 minutes.
  • Slow cooker: 4–6 hours on low.

Variations

Sweet Dessert Version with Chen Pi and Rock Sugar

This is the hong dou tang most people picture: glossy, faintly smoky from the peel, and lightly sweet. Cook the beans as above until very soft, then add 5 g of dried chen pi — soaked 20 minutes and scraped of its inner white pith if thick — and 40–60 g of rock sugar in the last 15 minutes. The peel is what lifts the soup out of plain sweetness and is traditionally included to support the Spleen while the bean drains.

Savoury Version with Job's Tears and Barley

In the humid south of China, red bean soup is just as often cooked without sugar at all. Add 60 g (⅓ cup) of coix seed (job's tears, yi mi) and 40 g (¼ cup) of pearl barley along with the beans, increase the water to 2 L (8 cups), and simmer 75–90 minutes. Season with salt at the end and finish with a scatter of spring onion. The result is a light, grainy, savoury soup that many people find easier to drink regularly through damp seasons. A slice or two of ginger is a good addition if you tend to feel cold.

Other Traditional Pairings

Traditional companions include poria (fu ling), lotus seed, Chinese yam (shan yao) added near the end of cooking, and euryale seed (qian shi) — all neutral, gently supportive ingredients that sit well alongside a draining bean.

If you enjoy this style, our mung bean soup is the cooling counterpart — better suited to hot summer weather, when the body feels hot rather than heavy.

Who Red Bean Soup Suits

In TCM food therapy, red bean soup is traditionally considered a good fit for people who feel heavy, puffy, or slow in humid weather; who notice a dull, full sensation in the abdomen; and who want a light, food-based routine through damp seasons.

Who Should Be Cautious

Like every food in TCM food therapy, red bean soup is not for everyone at every time:

  • Cold or deficient constitution. If you often feel cold, have loose stools, or tire easily, a draining food can feel like too much. Keep portions small, add ginger or chen pi, and avoid drinking it iced.
  • Pregnancy. Adzuki bean is traditionally described as mildly draining, and many practitioners suggest pregnant women keep portions modest or skip it, especially in the first trimester. Speak with your own medical practitioner first.
  • Do not over-consume. Two to three bowls a week is a comfortable rhythm for most adults; large daily bowls over long periods are not traditional practice.
  • During a cold with chills. Traditional practice generally sets draining foods aside until the acute stage passes.
  • Chronic conditions or medication. Check with your medical practitioner before making red bean soup a routine.
  • Cook beans thoroughly. Raw and undercooked beans contain compounds that can upset digestion; simmer until fully soft.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is red bean soup used for in TCM?

In TCM food therapy, red bean soup is traditionally used to support the body's fluid balance. Adzuki bean is believed to gently drain dampness, so the soup is traditionally taken in humid weather or whenever the body feels heavy, puffy, or sluggish. It is a food, not a remedy.

Is adzuki bean the same as the red kidney beans used in chilli?

No. The red bean used in hong dou tang is the small adzuki bean, known in Chinese as hong dou or chi xiao dou. Kidney beans are larger, darker, and starchier. Adzuki beans cook down to a soft, lightly sweet paste, which is why they suit soups and sweet dessert versions so well.

How often can I drink red bean soup?

For most adults, two to three bowls a week during damp or humid seasons is a comfortable amount in traditional practice. Because adzuki bean is mildly draining, daily large bowls are not recommended for long stretches. If you tend to feel cold, keep portions smaller and add ginger or chen pi.

Is red bean soup safe during pregnancy?

Adzuki bean is traditionally considered mildly draining, and many TCM practitioners advise pregnant women to keep portions modest or skip it altogether, especially in the first trimester. Always check with your own medical practitioner before adding it to your diet while pregnant or nursing.
