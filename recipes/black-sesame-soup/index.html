Black Sesame Soup (Hu Ma Hu) — A Gentle TCM Dessert

🥘 Recipe · 7 min read
Quick answer

Choose black sesame soup when you want a warm, nutty dessert with a silky texture and a modest amount of sweetness. Traditionally in TCM, black sesame (hei zhi ma, 黑芝麻) is described as sweet and neutral and is associated with nourishing Liver and Kidney systems; these are traditional categories, not a diagnosis or proven treatment. Start with one small bowl, use less sugar than a commercial mix, and notice how your digestion responds.

Is this guide for you?

This recipe suits a chilly evening, a small after-dinner dessert, or anyone who wants a spoonable sesame treat rather than a heavy cake. It is also useful if you are comparing whole-seed cooking with a ready-made powder. Choose the companion black sesame porridge if you want a breakfast with rice and a more substantial texture. If you prefer a fruit-and-fungus dessert, the snow fungus dessert is a lighter direction.

This is not a food-based answer to unexplained hair changes, fatigue, bowel changes, or any other persistent concern. For those situations, food can be part of a varied eating pattern, while a qualified health professional helps you investigate the cause.

The TCM lens: a traditional food-therapy description

In traditional TCM food language, black sesame is commonly described as 甘、平—sweet in flavor and neutral in thermal nature—and associated with the Liver and Kidney meridians. TCM writings also discuss jing (essence), qi, Blood, yin, and dampness as pattern concepts. In this context, “nourishing Blood” or “supporting Kidney essence” describes a traditional framework for choosing foods; it does not refer to a laboratory blood value, an anatomical kidney function, or a proven treatment.

From that viewpoint, sesame’s rich oiliness is why it is often paired with a modest portion and warm cooking. A person who feels heavy after oily foods or tends toward loose stools may prefer less sesame and more rice or water. That is a traditional personalization idea, not a self-diagnosis. TCM constitutions are best discussed with a trained practitioner.

The modern food lens: nutrition is not a promise

Black sesame provides calories, unsaturated fat, protein, fiber, calcium, iron, magnesium, copper, and plant compounds such as lignans. The exact amount depends on the seed, grinding, serving size, and added sugar. These are nutrition facts, not evidence that a bowl changes hair color, reverses hair loss, or treats a condition. Research on sesame foods and specific health outcomes is limited and does not make this dessert a proven remedy. What remains unknown includes how an individual will respond, especially when the recipe is used alongside medicines or a restricted diet.

Try it once: a small pot of hu ma hu

Ingredients — 2 small bowls

  • 60 g (about 6 tablespoons) raw black sesame seeds, or 45 g plain ground sesame powder
  • 20 g (2 tablespoons) glutinous rice flour; use oat flour for a less sticky texture
  • 450 ml water, plus 2 tablespoons cold water for mixing
  • 1–2 tablespoons rock sugar or maple syrup, added gradually
  • A tiny pinch of salt, optional

Method

  1. Put whole sesame in a dry skillet over medium-low heat. Stir for 2–3 minutes until fragrant, then remove it before the seeds darken further. Cool completely.
  2. Grind the seeds to a fine meal. If using powder, skip this step. Mix the rice flour with the cold water into a smooth slurry.
  3. Whisk the sesame and remaining water in a saucepan. Bring it just to a simmer, then slowly whisk in the slurry.
  4. Cook on low for 5–8 minutes, stirring often, until glossy and thick enough to coat a spoon. Add water for a thinner soup.
  5. Turn off the heat, sweeten lightly, and taste. Divide into two small bowls and eat warm, not scalding.

Timing and observation: Try it after a normal meal or as a small afternoon snack rather than on an empty stomach the first time. Notice fullness, thirst, skin or throat irritation, and stool changes over the following day. No special “dose” is required for a food; the recipe’s small bowl is a serving suggestion, not a therapeutic prescription.

Personalize without overcomplicating it

  • Less rich: use 35–45 g sesame and 500 ml water. It will be thinner but still aromatic.
  • Less sweet: use one teaspoon of maple syrup and let the roasted flavor lead.
  • More filling: serve with a spoonful of cooked rice, or compare it with walnut sesame porridge.
  • No glutinous rice flour: simmer longer, or blend one tablespoon of cooked rice into the liquid.
  • Keep the flavor clean: do not add several medicinal herbs simply to make the recipe sound stronger. If you want a structured TCM food plan, consult a qualified practitioner.

Troubleshooting

  • Grainy texture: grind the seeds longer and pass the mixture through a fine sieve before simmering.
  • Burnt or bitter taste: the seeds were toasted too long. Start again with low heat; sesame can go from fragrant to bitter quickly.
  • Too thick: whisk in hot water one tablespoon at a time. The soup thickens as it cools.
  • Too rich: reduce the sesame next time and keep the portion smaller. Do not treat discomfort as a sign that you should push through.

Specific safety boundaries

  • Do not eat this recipe if you have a sesame allergy. Stop and seek appropriate help for allergy symptoms.
  • Sesame is energy-dense, so keep added sugar and serving size modest if you are monitoring calories or blood glucose.
  • If you have ongoing diarrhea, significant digestive symptoms, difficulty swallowing, or a medically restricted diet, ask a clinician or registered dietitian before making it routine.
  • During pregnancy or nursing, food amounts may be different from concentrated herbal or supplement amounts. Ask your healthcare professional before adding extracts or medicinal herbs.
  • Children should be supervised around hot soup, and all diners should let it cool to a comfortable temperature.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use ready-ground sesame?

Yes. Choose plain, unsweetened powder and whisk it in gradually. Freshly toasted seeds have the best aroma, but powder is a sensible shortcut.

How often can I eat it?

A small bowl occasionally or a few times weekly can fit a varied diet for many people. Reduce the amount if it feels too rich or changes your digestion.

Does it improve hair?

Traditional TCM associates black sesame with Blood and Kidney-essence nourishment. Modern nutrition identifies useful nutrients, but this dessert is not a treatment for hair loss or premature graying. Persistent changes deserve professional evaluation.

Is hu ma hu the same as black sesame porridge?

They overlap, but hu ma hu is usually a smooth, sweet sesame paste-like soup, while porridge generally contains more rice and is served as a fuller meal. Choose based on texture, sweetness, and how filling you want the bowl to be.
