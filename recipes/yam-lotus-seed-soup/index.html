Chinese Yam and Lotus Seed Soup — Gentle Spleen-Nourishing Comfort

🥘 Recipe · 7 min read

Some soups are made to impress. Others are made to settle — the kind of bowl you reach for after a long week, when your stomach feels quiet and your energy feels low. Chinese yam and lotus seed soup is that second kind. It has no fiery spices, no dramatic aromatics. Instead, it leans on two humble ingredients that TCM has respected for centuries as gentle supporters of the spleen and stomach.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the spleen is not just an organ — it is the system that transforms food into qi (vital energy) and blood. When the spleen is content, digestion feels effortless, energy holds through the afternoon, and even your complexion tends to look brighter. When it is strained — by irregular meals, too much cold food, or long periods of worry — you may notice bloating, loose stools, fatigue, and a heavy feeling after eating. This is where the quiet pair of Chinese yam and lotus seed comes in.

Why These Two Ingredients Belong Together

Chinese yam and lotus seed are a classic TCM pairing, and their combination is far from accidental. Traditional practitioners describe their relationship as mutually reinforcing: yam gently tonifies the spleen and stomach, while lotus seed calms the heart and anchors the spirit. Together they create a soup that feels both grounding and soothing.

Both ingredients are considered neutral in temperature — neither hot nor cold — which makes this soup unusually versatile. It is traditionally enjoyed across seasons, from the damp days of late spring to the cooling evenings of autumn.

TCM Properties of Chinese Yam (Huai Shan)

Known in Chinese as huai shan (怀山药), Chinese yam is one of the most frequently cited spleen tonics in the classical literature. It appears in the Shennong Bencao Jing (Divine Farmer's Materia Medica), one of the oldest surviving Chinese medical texts, where it is described as a food that nourishes without being heavy.

Traditional characteristics

  • Nature: neutral
  • Flavor: sweet
  • Channels: spleen, lung, kidney
  • Traditional role: supports the spleen and stomach, moistens the lungs, and is believed to support the kidneys

Because yam is both a food and a medicinal herb, it sits comfortably in the tradition of "medicine and food share the same origin" (药食同源). Unlike stronger herbs, it is gentle enough for children and older adults when cooked well.

TCM Properties of Lotus Seed (Lian Zi)

Lotus seed, or lian zi (莲子), comes from the seed head of the sacred lotus — a plant that grows in mud yet produces pristine flowers, which may be why TCM associates it with purity and calm. The seed's sweet, neutral character is traditionally linked to the spleen and heart.

Traditional characteristics

  • Nature: neutral
  • Flavor: sweet, slightly astringent
  • Channels: spleen, kidney, heart
  • Traditional role: supports the spleen, calms the spirit, and is believed to help "anchor" the heart

The astringent quality of lotus seed is valued in TCM for helping the body hold on to what it needs — which is why it is sometimes included in dietary guidance for loose stools. Its calming association with the heart also makes it a common addition to soups and desserts meant for restful evenings.

How to Make Chinese Yam and Lotus Seed Soup

This recipe makes about 4 servings. It is a gentle, low-effort soup — most of the work is simmering time.

Ingredients

  • 1 medium Chinese yam (about 300 g), peeled and sliced into 1 cm rounds
  • 1 cup dried lotus seeds (about 100 g), soaked for 1 hour
  • 6–8 red dates (jujube), pitted
  • 2 slices fresh ginger
  • 1.5 liters water (or light vegetable stock)
  • A small pinch of sea salt, to taste

Steps

  1. Soak the lotus seeds. Place them in warm water for 1 hour, then drain and rinse. If you prefer a softer texture, soak for 2 hours.
  2. Prepare the yam. Peel it carefully — raw yam sap can irritate sensitive skin, so gloves are a good idea. Slice into even rounds so they cook at the same pace.
  3. Combine and simmer. Add the water, yam, lotus seeds, red dates, and ginger to a pot. Bring to a gentle boil, then reduce the heat to a low simmer.
  4. Cook until tender. Simmer for about 40 minutes, or until the yam is soft and the lotus seeds yield easily when pressed.
  5. Season and serve. Add a tiny pinch of salt, taste, and serve warm. The broth should be light and slightly sweet from the red dates.

Variations

  • Richer broth: replace water with chicken or pork bone broth for a more nourishing bowl.
  • Congee-style: add ½ cup of rinsed rice and cook for an extra 30 minutes to turn this into a comforting yam and lotus seed porridge.
  • Sweet version: omit the ginger and salt, add a little rock sugar, and serve as a warm dessert soup.
  • Add goji berries: stir in a small handful in the last 10 minutes for a gentle lift of sweetness and color.

Who This Soup May Suit Best

Because of its neutral, gentle nature, this soup is traditionally considered suitable for a wide range of people. It may be particularly fitting for:

  • Anyone who feels tired after meals — a classic sign of spleen strain in TCM thinking
  • People with a tendency to bloating or heavy digestion
  • Those who prefer mild flavors and find spicy or rich foods overwhelming
  • Children and older adults who need easily digestible nourishment (check with your pediatrician or doctor first)
  • Restless evenings — the lotus seed's traditional association with calming the spirit makes this a soothing supper

Who Should Be Cautious

  • Constipation: lotus seed is traditionally viewed as slightly astringent, so people prone to constipation may want to use fewer seeds or skip them.
  • Blood sugar concerns: Chinese yam is a starchy root and red dates are naturally sweet — portion size matters if you are watching carbohydrates or blood sugar.
  • Allergies: anyone with a known sensitivity to yam, lotus, or jujube should avoid this soup.
  • Pregnancy and chronic conditions: always check with a qualified healthcare provider before adding any new food therapy to your routine.

How to Choose Your Ingredients

Chinese yam: Look for firm, unblemished roots with smooth skin. Fresh yam is best for this soup; if you can only find dried yam slices (huai shan pian), soak them for 30 minutes before cooking.

Lotus seeds: Dried lotus seeds keep well in an airtight jar. Some packets contain the bitter green center (lotus plumule); it is fine to leave it in, but the soup will be slightly more bitter. Removing it gives a sweeter, milder result.

Red dates: Plump, glossy dates with a sweet aroma are the best choice. They add natural sweetness and a warm red tone to the broth.

When to Drink It

This soup works as a light dinner or a mid-day bowl when you want something comforting but not heavy. In TCM thinking, the spleen is most active in the morning and early afternoon, which is why many practitioners suggest enjoying nourishing foods earlier in the day. A small evening bowl is also fine — just keep it warm and eat slowly.

The Bottom Line

Chinese yam and lotus seed soup is the kind of dish that earns its place through repetition rather than drama. It is simple to make, gentle on the stomach, and rooted in a food therapy tradition that has survived for thousands of years. Whether you are new to TCM or simply looking for a quieter kind of comfort, this soup is a lovely place to begin.

Enjoy it warm, with a deep breath and a slower pace — that, too, is part of the medicine.
