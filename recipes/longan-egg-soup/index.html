Longan and Egg Sweet Soup — A Warming Blood-Building Bowl

🥘 Recipe · 7 min read
Quick answer

In Cantonese kitchens, few bowls are as quietly comforting as longan and egg sweet soup (桂圆鸡蛋糖水, guìyuán jīdàn tángshuǐ). It looks unassuming — a pale golden broth with silky strands of egg drifting through soft, honey-sweet longan — yet it has been simmered in Chinese homes for generations as a gentle way to restore energy, warm the hands and feet, and steady a busy mind before sleep.

It looks unassuming — a pale golden broth with silky strands of egg drifting through soft, honey-sweet longan — yet it has been simmered in Chinese homes for generations as a gentle way to restore energy, warm the hands and feet, and steady a busy mind before sleep.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) food therapy groups this dessert under the idea of nourishing blood and calming the spirit. Longan brings sweetness and warmth; the egg adds quiet substance. Together they make a bowl that is believed to support people who feel drained, pale, forgetful, or simply worn out by long days and short nights. Best of all, it takes about half an hour and only a handful of ingredients.

Longan in TCM: A Sweet, Warming Blood Tonic

Dried longan — the amber flesh of the Dimocarpus longan fruit — is one of the most beloved sweet tonics in Chinese food therapy. In TCM terms, it is classified as sweet in flavor and warm in nature, and it is traditionally said to enter the Heart and Spleen channels. This pairing matters: the Spleen is seen as the source of blood and energy (qi), while the Heart is said to "house the spirit" (Shen). A food that gently supports both is thought to build blood at its source and calm the mind at the same time.

Classical texts describe longan in glowing terms. Li Shizhen's Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》) praises it for its nourishing qualities, and it has long been recommended for people who feel fatigued, experience poor memory, or notice a pale complexion and dry lips — the cluster of signs TCM calls blood deficiency. For a fuller look at its history, properties, and buying tips, see our longan ingredient guide.

Why the Egg Belongs in the Bowl

The egg is easy to overlook, but it does real work in this recipe. TCM texts describe eggs as sweet and neutral, with a traditional role in nourishing yin and blood. Where longan supplies warmth and sweetness, the egg contributes a grounding, protein-rich richness that makes the soup feel like a meal rather than a treat.

How you add the egg changes the dish. Whisked and drizzled in, it forms feather-light ribbons (egg flower) that are gentle on digestion — the version most often given to children, the elderly, and anyone recovering from illness. Poached whole, it becomes a more substantial, yang-warming bowl that has traditionally been served to new mothers and people who feel deeply depleted. A Traditional Bowl for Postpartum and Recovery

Across southern China, a version of longan and egg sweet soup has long been part of the zuò yuèzi (坐月子) confinement period — the month after childbirth when new mothers are cared for with warm, nourishing foods. Longan and red dates were offered to help restore energy after the exertion of labor, and eggs provided easy-to-digest protein. Modern Cantonese grandmothers still prepare it for daughters and daughters-in-law returning home with a newborn, often adding ginger to warm the body further. You can read more about this tradition in our guide to postpartum foods in TCM.

It is not only for new mothers, though. In TCM thinking, this soup is traditionally suited to anyone carrying the signs of blood deficiency: fatigue that rest does not fully erase, dizziness, dull or dry hair, brittle nails, scanty menstruation, poor concentration, and sleep that feels shallow. Because the soup is warm and sweet, it is also a natural match for foods traditionally used to support healthy blood, as part of an overall balanced diet.

The Recipe: Longan and Egg Sweet Soup

This is the classic home-style version — no special equipment, no long simmering. Plan for about 30 minutes from stove to bowl.

Ingredients

  • 20 dried longan arils (about 30 g)
  • 6–8 dried red dates (jujube), pitted
  • 2 eggs
  • 3 cups water (750 ml)
  • 3–4 thin slices fresh ginger (optional)
  • 1 tablespoon goji berries (optional)
  • Rock sugar, to taste (optional)

Choosing your ingredients: Look for longan arils that are plump, amber, and fragrant — not brittle or grey. Red dates (jujube dates) should be glossy and full; pitting them keeps the soup sweeter and easier to eat. If you prefer a cleaner taste, skip the ginger; if you want extra warmth on a cold day, keep it in.

Method

  1. Rinse and soak. Rinse the longan arils and red dates, then soak them in warm water for about 10 minutes to soften and plump them.
  2. Simmer the base. Bring the water to a gentle simmer in a small pot. Add the longan, red dates, and ginger if using. Simmer uncovered for 15–20 minutes, until the liquid is fragrant and lightly golden.
  3. Add the egg. Whisk the eggs in a small bowl. For egg-flower style, drizzle them slowly into the simmering soup while stirring gently in one direction — they will set into silky ribbons in seconds. For a poached version, slide in the whole eggs and let them cook for about 3 minutes until the whites are set but the yolks are still soft.
  4. Sweeten and serve. If using goji berries or rock sugar, add them in the final 2–3 minutes and stir until dissolved. Ladle into warm bowls and sip slowly while it is still hot.

Variations to Try

  • Goji berry version: Add 1 tablespoon of goji berries at the end for an extra layer of nourishment — the same pairing you will meet in goji longan tea.
  • Black glutinous rice balls: Drop a few small sesame-filled rice balls into the soup for a festive, filling dessert.
  • Brown sugar instead of rock sugar: Brown sugar brings a deeper, more robust warmth that many people prefer in autumn and winter.
  • Lotus seeds: Add 15 g of pre-soaked lotus seeds with the longan for a version traditionally associated with calmer sleep.

When to Enjoy It — and Who Should Take Care

In the rhythm of the day, TCM food therapy usually places this soup in the late afternoon or evening, when the body is naturally winding down. A small warm bowl an hour or two before bed is traditionally thought to support restful sleep; during the day it makes a satisfying pick-me-up that is gentler than coffee. Two to three bowls per week is a sensible rhythm for most people.

Because longan is warm and the soup is sweet, a few groups may want to go slowly. People who tend toward excess heat — frequent mouth ulcers, a dry throat, acne, or constipation — may find it too warming if eaten daily. Those managing blood sugar should keep portions small and skip the added sugar, since longan and red dates are naturally sweet. Anyone who feels damp (heavy limbs, a coated tongue, loose stools) may prefer it less often or with a slice of ginger. Pregnant women, especially in the later months, are traditionally advised to eat longan only in small amounts, and eggs must always be cooked through. As with any dietary change, check with your healthcare provider if you have a medical condition.

If this soup appeals to you, you will likely also enjoy the closely related longan and red date soup, which trades the egg for a lighter, purely fruit-based sweetness — equally warming, equally easy to make.
