Lamb and Daikon Soup — Warming Winter Broth for Qi and Blood

🥘 Recipe · 7 min read

When the wind turns sharp and the cold settles into the bones, kitchens across northern China fill with the scent of lamb and daikon soup. It is one of the great winter dishes of the Chinese table: a slow-simmered broth of tender lamb, sweet translucent radish, and warming aromatics — the kind of pot you put on in the afternoon and lift the lid on three hours later, when the whole house feels warmer than it did before.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) food therapy, this pairing is more than hearty home cooking. Lamb (yáng ròu, 羊肉) is traditionally regarded as the most warming of all common meats — a food for the deepest, coldest weeks of the year. Daikon (bái luó bo, 白萝卜) is its opposite in character: cool, sweet, and descending. Together they form one of the most elegant balancing acts in the Chinese kitchen — a soup believed to warm the body without overheating it, and to nourish the Blood without weighing down the digestion.

This guide looks at what TCM tradition says about each ingredient, why the combination is considered so effective, who may benefit most, and exactly how to make the soup — with a full ingredient list, step-by-step method, variations, serving rhythm, and precautions.

Lamb in TCM: The Most Warming of Meats

In TCM food therapy, lamb is classified as warm to hot in nature (in older texts, even great warm), with a sweet flavor. It is said to enter the Spleen, Stomach, Kidney, and Heart meridians. Its defining qualities are that it warms, it builds, and it moves upward and outward — which is precisely what a cold, depleted body is said to need in midwinter.

Three traditional uses stand out:

  • Warming the middle burner. Lamb is believed to warm the Spleen and Stomach, the digestive core. People who feel perpetually cold, whose hands and feet never warm up, or who feel a dull ache in the belly after eating cold food are the classic audience for lamb in TCM food therapy.
  • Supporting Yang and Kidney energy. Because it enters the Kidney meridian, lamb is traditionally linked to the deep warming energy that TCM associates with vitality, lower-back strength, and the ability to withstand cold. This is why lamb is the winter meat of choice across northern China.
  • Nourishing Blood. Lamb is also considered a blood-building meat, traditionally used for pale complexion, fatigue, and the tiredness that follows illness or heavy menstrual cycles.

The classical literature supports this. The Compendium of Materia Medica (Bencao Gangmu, 《本草纲目》), compiled by Li Shizhen in the 16th century, records lamb as warming the middle, replenishing deficiency, and supporting Qi and Blood. Even earlier, the Essential Prescriptions of the Golden Cabinet (Jin Gui Yao Lue, 《金匮要略》) by Zhang Zhongjing contains one of the most famous recipes in Chinese medicine: Danggui, ginger, and lamb soup (当归生姜羊肉汤) — a warming, blood-nourishing broth that is still cooked in Chinese homes today, almost two thousand years later.

Daikon: The Cooling Counterweight

If lamb is the fire, daikon is the valve. In TCM, daikon (bái luó bo, 白萝卜) is classified as cool in nature with a sweet and pungent flavor, entering the Lung, Spleen, and Stomach meridians. Its traditional actions are to move Qi downward, resolve stagnation, ease bloating, and transform phlegm.

This is the key to the pairing. Rich, warming meats can feel heavy — they may build energy, but they can also leave a person feeling stuffed, sluggish, or vaguely overheated. Daikon is believed to offset exactly that: it descends, it disperses, and it keeps the digestive system moving so that the nourishment in the lamb can actually be absorbed rather than sitting like a stone. Many Chinese cooks describe the soup as bǔ ér bù zào (补而不燥) — nourishing without being drying or irritating.

There is even a folk saying that captures the logic: "Eat radish in winter, eat ginger in summer" (冬吃萝卜夏吃姜). Winter is the season of rich, warming food and indoor living; radish is the vegetable that keeps everything flowing.

Why This Pairing Works So Well

TCM food therapy is built on balance rather than on single "superfoods," and lamb with daikon is a textbook example. The lamb is warm, sweet, and building; the daikon is cool, sweet, and moving. The ginger, red dates, and white peppercorns in the recipe push gently in the same direction as the lamb — warming the digestion and helping the rich meat go down easily — while the daikon keeps the whole pot from tipping into heaviness.

Seasonally, the dish also makes sense. Winter in TCM is governed by the Water element and the Kidneys, a season of conservation, storage, and depth. It is the time of year when warming, building foods are traditionally emphasized and cooling, light foods are used to keep the body from becoming congested. Lamb and daikon soup sits exactly on that line.

Who May Benefit Most

This is a substantial, warming soup. TCM food therapy suggests it may be especially well suited to people who recognize several of these patterns:

  • Hands and feet that are cold most of the day, even indoors
  • A feeling of deep cold in the lower back, belly, or knees
  • A pale complexion, easy fatigue, or a sense of being run down after illness
  • Weak appetite and slow digestion, especially after cold or raw food
  • Frequent colds in winter, or a body that never quite warms up after being outdoors
  • Menstrual cycles that leave a person feeling drained and cold

Conversely, people who tend to run hot — with a red face, a dry mouth in the afternoon, night sweats, or a tendency to feel irritable and overheated — are usually guided toward lighter, cooling winter dishes instead.

Ingredients

This recipe makes about 6 servings, or a large pot that keeps for three days in the refrigerator.

  • 800 g bone-in lamb shoulder or lamb neck, cut into 4 cm chunks (bone-in gives a deeper broth)
  • 1 large daikon (about 700 g), peeled and cut into thick 2 cm rounds
  • 30 g fresh old ginger, sliced — more if you feel the cold easily
  • 5 dried red dates (jujube), pitted
  • 1 small piece dried tangerine peel (chen pi), about 4 g
  • 2 scallions, white parts only
  • 1 tablespoon Shaoxing cooking wine
  • 1 teaspoon white peppercorns
  • 2.5 liters cold water
  • Salt to taste
  • 1 tablespoon goji berries (optional, added at the very end)

Optional classical addition: 10 g of angelica root (dang gui) turns this into a close cousin of the traditional Danggui, ginger, and lamb soup from the Jin Gui Yao Lue. Dang gui is traditionally used to nourish Blood; it can be added with the aromatics and left in for the whole simmer, then discarded before serving.

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Blanch the lamb. Put the lamb chunks in a pot of cold water, bring to a boil, and blanch for 3-5 minutes, until the water turns cloudy and grey foam rises. Drain, rinse the lamb under warm water, and discard the blanching liquid. This single step is what keeps the finished broth clear and clean-tasting rather than gamey and murky — do not skip it.
  2. Build the broth. Return the lamb to a clean, heavy pot with the ginger, red dates, tangerine peel, scallion whites, white peppercorns, and Shaoxing wine. Add 2.5 liters of cold water and bring to a boil over high heat.
  3. Skim and simmer. Skim off any foam that rises to the surface, then lower the heat to a bare simmer. Cover with the lid slightly ajar and cook for 1 hour, until the lamb is beginning to soften.
  4. Add the daikon. Add the daikon rounds and continue simmering for another 40-50 minutes, until the lamb is tender enough to pull from the bone and the daikon is translucent, sweet, and yielding but still holding its shape. Adding the daikon only now keeps it from dissolving into the broth.
  5. Finish and season. In the final 5 minutes, stir in the goji berries if using. Season with salt, ladle off any excess fat from the surface, and serve hot in deep bowls with broth, lamb, and daikon together.

Total time: about 20 minutes of preparation and 1 hour 50 minutes of cooking. A wide, heavy pot or clay casserole gives the best result; a thin pot will reduce too quickly, so top up with a little hot water if the broth drops below the ingredients.

Variations

  • Classical blood-nourishing version. Add 10 g of angelica root (dang gui) and increase the ginger to 40 g. This is the closest home version of the Jin Gui Yao Lue formula, traditionally eaten after menstrual cycles or during recovery.
  • Extra warming northern style. Keep the white peppercorns generous and add 3-4 slices of fresh ginger extra at the end of cooking. This is the version many families in Beijing and the northeast cook in January.
  • Slow cooker. After blanching, put the lamb and aromatics in a slow cooker with cold water and cook on low for 6-8 hours, adding the daikon in the final 1.5 hours.
  • Pressure cooker. Cook the lamb and aromatics under pressure for 25 minutes, release naturally, then add the daikon and simmer uncovered for 20 minutes so it keeps its texture.
  • Lighter version. Use lamb loin chops instead of shoulder, skim the fat thoroughly, and add an extra handful of daikon. The soup becomes noticeably less rich and better suited to early winter rather than deep winter.
  • Noodle finish. Cook thin wheat noodles separately and pour the soup over them for a complete winter meal — a common way to serve this dish in northern China.

When to Eat It and How Often

Lamb and daikon soup is emphatically a cold-season dish. In TCM thinking, it belongs to deep winter — the weeks around the solstice through late February — when the body genuinely benefits from warming, building food. A common rhythm is once a week, served as the centerpiece of a midday or early evening meal, when digestion is at its strongest.

It is best eaten warm and not too late at night. Because lamb is rich, a large bowl right before sleep can feel heavy for some people. Leftovers keep for three days in the refrigerator and the broth freezes well for up to a month; store the broth and meat together and add fresh daikon when reheating if you have any left over.

Precautions and Who Should Be Cautious

Lamb and daikon soup is a traditional, well-balanced dish, but it is warming and substantial — which means it is not right for everyone at every time. Keep these points in mind:

  • Heat patterns. If you tend to feel hot, have a red face, a dry mouth and throat, night sweats, constipation with dryness, or feel irritable and overheated, lamb is traditionally considered too warming. Enjoy it rarely, in small amounts, or choose a cooler winter soup instead.
  • Acute illness or fever. Rich, warming soups are generally not recommended during an active fever or acute infection. Wait until the acute phase has passed.
  • Damp-heat or inflammatory skin flare-ups. People with a TCM pattern of damp-heat — heaviness, a bitter taste, a greasy yellow tongue coating, or active hot, weeping skin conditions — are often advised to limit lamb.
  • Gout or high uric acid. Lamb is a purine-rich meat and long-simmered broths are concentrated. If you manage gout or hyperuricemia, enjoy this dish in moderation and avoid drinking large amounts of the broth.
  • High blood pressure or heart conditions. The broth is naturally fatty and salty. Skim the fat carefully, season lightly, and keep portions moderate.
  • Pregnancy. Lamb is commonly eaten during pregnancy in Chinese food culture, and this soup is gentle by traditional standards. Because every pregnancy is different — and because herbal additions such as dang gui are traditionally avoided during pregnancy unless prescribed — check with your healthcare provider before making it part of your routine, and leave out the dang gui.
  • Digestive weakness. If heavy meat meals tend to sit badly, start with a smaller bowl, keep the ginger and daikon generous, and chew slowly.

As with all food therapy, this soup is a complement to — never a replacement for — professional medical care. If you have persistent fatigue, unexplained weight loss, or ongoing pain, please see a doctor.
