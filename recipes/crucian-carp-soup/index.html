Crucian Carp Soup — The Traditional Lactation-Supporting Broth

🥘 Recipe · 7 min read
Quick answer

Quick answer: Crucian carp soup is a light, savory fish broth that appears in some Chinese postpartum food traditions. It can be a pleasant way to add fluid, protein, and a warm meal, but it has not been reliably established as a way to change milk supply. If you try it, use a fresh, fully cooked fish, keep the seasoning simple, and notice how your body and appetite respond.

Is this guide for you?

This recipe suits a reader who wants a mild soup after a busy day, is curious about traditional postpartum cooking, or wants an alternative to a heavy meat broth. It is especially useful when you prefer a clean ginger-and-scallion flavor and can buy fish from a reliable source. It is not the best first choice if you dislike bones, need a fast pantry meal, or have a fish allergy. For a soft, low-effort grain-based meal, compare it with our congee recipe guide.

Breastfeeding deserves a realistic frame: traditional foodways may pair nourishing soups with rest and regular meals, but milk production is individual. If a baby is not feeding well, wet diapers change, or you are worried about supply or pain, contact a qualified lactation professional or clinician rather than relying on soup.

The decision: which bowl makes sense?

  • Choose crucian carp soup if: you want a warm, lightly seasoned fish meal and can eat fish safely.
  • Consider another option if: you need a boneless meal, are avoiding fish, or your household has a relevant allergy or fish-consumption advisory.
  • Start with: one small bowl, about 250–350 ml, alongside rice and vegetables. Treat it as food, not a concentrated tonic.

The TCM lens: a traditional interpretation

In TCM food therapy, crucian carp (鲫鱼, jì yú) is traditionally described as sweet in flavor and neutral to mildly warm in nature. Classical food-therapy language commonly associates it with the Spleen and Stomach systems. In this framework, “Spleen” and “Stomach” are traditional functional concepts, not direct descriptions of organs or anatomy.

Traditional cooks may choose crucian carp in a postpartum bowl because the soup is warm, moist, and easy to pair with ginger. Some interpretations describe it as supporting qi, fluids, or the movement of milk; these are TCM concepts and should not be read as proven physiological effects. The ginger is traditionally considered warming, while scallion adds aroma and helps keep the broth lively.

The modern food lens: what is known and what is not

Fish can provide protein and, depending on the species and portion, nutrients such as vitamin B12, selenium, and fats. The exact nutrition of crucian carp soup varies with the fish, how much flesh is eaten, and how much broth is served. A soup is also a practical way to make a meal more appealing when appetite is low.

What remains uncertain is whether crucian carp itself reliably changes milk production. There is no good reason to promise that it does. Hydration, sufficient overall food, feeding or pumping patterns, sleep, stress, and individual health all matter, and a single recipe cannot stand in for assessment. Use current local fish guidance because contamination advice depends on the water and species.

Try it once: simple crucian carp soup

Ingredients for 2 small servings

  • 1 fresh crucian carp, about 350–450 g, scaled and gutted
  • 6 thin slices fresh ginger
  • 2 scallions, white and green parts separated
  • 1 teaspoon neutral cooking oil
  • 900 ml water or unsalted stock
  • 1 small pinch of salt, added at the end; optional white pepper
  • Optional: a handful of napa cabbage or soft tofu for a fuller meal

Method and timing

  1. Ask the fish seller to scale and gut the fish. At home, rinse briefly, remove the gills and any dark lining, then pat it very dry. Check for an odor that seems sour or strongly ammonia-like; if present, do not use the fish.
  2. Warm the oil in a pot over medium heat. Add the fish and sear each side for 2–3 minutes until lightly golden. This step adds flavor and can reduce a muddy taste.
  3. Add the ginger, scallion whites, and water. Bring to a boil, then lower to a steady simmer for 20–25 minutes, skimming foam if needed. The flesh should be opaque and separate easily from the bone.
  4. Add cabbage or tofu during the last 5–8 minutes if using. Season lightly with salt and scatter scallion greens over the bowl.
  5. Serve immediately while hot. Pick the flesh from the bones carefully; do not assume blending or straining removes every fine bone.

One safe experiment: begin with half a serving at lunch, not as a replacement for a full meal. Observe taste, fullness, thirst, digestion, and any signs of an allergic reaction over the rest of the day. If all is comfortable, make it again as part of a varied diet rather than increasing the amount because of an expected effect.

Troubleshooting and personalization

  • Fishy aroma: improve freshness, remove the gills and dark lining, dry the fish thoroughly, and sear before adding water. Ginger and scallion should support the flavor, not hide spoiled fish.
  • Too many bones: serve the fish whole to an attentive adult, or use a boneless white-fish fillet next time. Do not serve fine-boned fish to a young child without appropriate care.
  • Too bland: add a little salt, napa cabbage, tofu, or cooked rice at the table. Avoid turning it into a salty “tonic.”
  • Too rich or uncomfortable: skim the surface, eat a smaller portion, and switch to congee or a vegetable soup if that sits better.

Specific safety boundaries

Do not eat raw or undercooked fish. Follow local advice about freshwater fish, especially if the fish was caught rather than purchased. Check carefully for bones. Avoid the recipe if you have a fish allergy, and seek urgent help for trouble breathing, facial swelling, or widespread hives after eating. If pregnant, breastfeeding, immunocompromised, managing kidney or liver disease, taking medicines that affect diet, or unsure about local fish safety, ask a qualified healthcare professional for individualized advice. Food allergies and infant feeding concerns deserve professional attention; soup is not a substitute for it.

Frequently asked questions

Can this soup reliably increase milk supply?

That has not been established. It is traditionally used in some postpartum foodways, but a single soup should not be expected to change milk supply. Get feeding concerns assessed by a lactation professional or clinician.

Can I make it with a fillet?

Yes. A boneless white-fish fillet is a practical substitute. Reduce the simmering time and cook until the center is opaque and flakes easily.

How often can I eat it?

There is no universal TCM serving schedule. A small bowl as part of a varied diet is a more sensible approach than using it repeatedly as a concentrated remedy. Follow local fish-consumption guidance.
