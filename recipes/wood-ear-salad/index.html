Wood Ear Mushroom Salad — A Cooling Vessel-Nourishing Side Dish

🥘 Recipe · 7 min read
Quick answer

On hot days and humid evenings, Chinese kitchens reach for a humble ingredient that does quiet, powerful work: the wood ear mushroom (mù ěr, 木耳). Deep black, delicately crunchy, and almost neutral on its own, it transforms into something special when dressed with vinegar, garlic, and sesame oil. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), this unassuming fungus has been valued for centuries as a food that cools the body, nourishes the Blood, and supports the health of the blood vessels — which is why this simple salad earns the description vessel-nourishing.

Wood ear salad is one of the most popular cold dishes in Chinese home cooking, served as a refreshing starter in summer and as a cleansing side dish all year round. This guide covers the TCM story of wood ear, what makes it a naturally "cooling" food, a complete step-by-step salad recipe with variations, the benefits it is traditionally associated with, and the precautions you should keep in mind.

What Is Wood Ear Mushroom in TCM?

Wood ear (also called black fungus, cloud ear, or tree ear) is an edible jelly fungus that grows on the bark of deciduous trees. Dried wood ear has been a pantry staple in China for over a thousand years. In TCM food therapy, its energetic profile is described as:

  • Nature (性, xìng): Neutral to slightly cooling — gentle enough for daily meals, yet useful for clearing heat.
  • Flavor (味, wèi): Sweet (甘, gān).
  • Meridians entered (归经): Lungs, Stomach, and Liver.

Because of this profile, wood ear is traditionally considered to moisten Dryness, nourish the Blood, and harmonize the Stomach. Its slightly cooling quality is why it shines in cold dressed salads — a way of literally "cooling the plate" during the warmest months of the year.

Traditional History and Classical References

Wood ear appears throughout Chinese food-therapy literature. In the Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》), Li Shizhen recorded wood ear as sweet and neutral, traditionally used to benefit Qi, nourish Blood, and moisten the Lungs and Intestines. Earlier Tang-dynasty dietary texts also mention black fungus as a food that "nourishes the middle and benefits Qi" — a gentle tonic suited to regular eating rather than strong medicinal intervention.

In folk tradition, wood ear earned nicknames that point to its vessel-supporting reputation. Because of its dark, glossy appearance and its springy texture, it was long associated with healthy, supple blood flow — an association modern cooks still echo when they pair wood ear with Blood-nourishing companions like goji berries, red dates, and spinach.

Why "Cooling" and "Vessel-Nourishing"?

The two ideas in this salad's name come straight from TCM logic. First, cooling: summer heat and rich, greasy food can create what TCM calls heat and Damp-Heat in the body. Cooling, light dishes help the body feel clear and comfortable. Wood ear's neutral-to-slightly-cool nature makes it an ideal base for such dishes.

Second, vessel-nourishing: in TCM theory, Blood needs to move smoothly through the vessels for the whole body to be nourished. Wood ear is traditionally believed to nourish Blood and support its smooth circulation. Modern nutrition research has found that wood ear is rich in dietary fiber, plant polyphenols, and iron — nutrients that are studied for their role in healthy blood and cardiovascular function. As always, we present this as tradition and emerging science, not a medical promise.

Complete Recipe: Wood Ear Mushroom Salad

This is the classic home-style 凉拌木耳 (liáng bàn mù ěr). It takes about 20 minutes once the wood ear is soaked.

Ingredients

  • 30 g (about 1 oz / ½ cup) dried black wood ear mushrooms
  • Cool water, for soaking
  • 2 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 small knob ginger, finely julienned (optional)
  • 2 tbsp black vinegar (Chinkiang vinegar) — or rice vinegar for a milder taste
  • 1 tbsp light soy sauce
  • 1 tsp sesame oil (toasted)
  • ½ tsp sugar (optional, to balance the vinegar)
  • 1–2 tsp chili oil or fresh red chili slices (optional, for heat)
  • 2 tbsp chopped scallions and cilantro
  • 1 tbsp toasted white sesame seeds

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Soak: Place the dried wood ear in a bowl of cool water and soak for 1–2 hours, until fully soft and springy. If you are short on time, warm (not boiling) water works in about 30 minutes. Do not soak overnight at room temperature.
  2. Clean: Rinse the rehydrated wood ear thoroughly. Trim away any hard, woody stems and tear the fungus into bite-sized pieces.
  3. Blanch: Bring a pot of water to a rolling boil. Add the wood ear and blanch for 2–3 minutes. This step is essential — wood ear must never be eaten raw.
  4. Chill: Drain immediately and plunge the wood ear into cold water (ice water is ideal) for 1–2 minutes. This stops the cooking and keeps the signature crunchy texture.
  5. Drain well: Shake off excess water thoroughly — a dry salad absorbs dressing better.
  6. Make the dressing: In a small bowl, whisk together the vinegar, soy sauce, sesame oil, sugar (if using), minced garlic, and ginger.
  7. Toss: Combine the wood ear with the dressing. Add chili oil if you like heat. Toss well and let it rest for 10 minutes so the flavors marry.
  8. Garnish and serve: Top with scallions, cilantro, and toasted sesame seeds. Serve cool or at room temperature as a starter or side dish.

Flavor Variations

  • Cucumber wood ear salad: Add 1 small julienned cucumber for extra crunch and even more cooling refreshment — a classic summer pairing.
  • Goji berry version: Soak 1 tbsp goji berries in warm water for 5 minutes and toss them in. In TCM, goji berries reinforce wood ear's Blood-nourishing quality. (Learn more in our goji berries guide.)
  • Wood ear and celery: Add blanched celery ribbons — celery is another traditionally cooling vegetable that pairs well with the fungus.
  • Warm version: In cooler months, stir-fry the blanched wood ear briefly with garlic and a splash of Shaoxing wine instead of dressing it cold.

Health Benefits — TCM and Modern Nutrition Perspectives

Blood Nourishment and Circulation

In TCM, wood ear is traditionally believed to nourish the Blood and support smooth circulation through the vessels. It is often recommended as part of a diet for people who feel tired, pale, or easily fatigued — signs TCM associates with Blood deficiency. Modern analysis shows wood ear contains iron, which is needed for healthy red blood cells, making it a sensible plant-based addition to a varied diet.

Cooling and Clearing Heat

Because of its neutral-to-cool nature, wood ear is traditionally used to help the body cope with hot weather, greasy meals, and the mild irritability that heat can bring. A cold wood ear salad is a simple, delicious example of eating with the seasons — cooling foods for the warm months.

Moistening Dryness and Supporting Digestion

TCM texts describe wood ear as moistening the Lungs and Intestines. Its high soluble-fiber content gives it a gentle, slippery quality that is traditionally believed to support regular bowel movements and comfortable digestion — one reason it is so often served alongside rich, savory dishes.

Modern Nutritional Highlights

Wood ear is low in calories yet rich in plant fiber, polyphenols, and trace minerals. Some studies suggest that fungal polysaccharides like those found in wood ear may support healthy cholesterol levels already within a normal range. These are areas of ongoing research, and we share them only as context for an already nourishing food.

Precautions and Considerations

  • Never eat raw or undercooked wood ear. Always soak dried wood ear and blanch or cook it thoroughly before eating.
  • Do not over-soak. Soaking for many hours or overnight at room temperature encourages bacterial growth. Soak for 1–2 hours in cool water and cook promptly. Discard any wood ear that smells sour or feels slimy.
  • Fresh wood ear caution: In China, fresh (un-dried) wood ear is generally avoided because it contains photosensitizing compounds that may cause skin reactions after sun exposure. Stick to dried wood ear, which is safe once properly soaked and cooked.
  • Blood-thinning medication and bleeding disorders: Because wood ear is traditionally believed to support circulation, people taking anticoagulant medication or living with bleeding disorders should consult a healthcare provider before eating it regularly.
  • Surgery: If you have upcoming surgery, discuss your diet with your doctor, as with any food that may influence clotting.
  • Allergies: Anyone with a known mushroom allergy should avoid wood ear.
  • Portion size: Enjoy wood ear as part of a balanced meal — a small bowl of salad is a reasonable serving.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is wood ear mushroom good for in TCM?

In TCM food therapy, wood ear is considered sweet and neutral, and is traditionally believed to nourish Blood, moisten the Lungs and Intestines, and support healthy blood circulation. It is a popular cooling ingredient for warm-weather dishes and is often paired with Blood-nourishing foods such as goji berries and red dates.

Can I eat raw wood ear mushroom in a salad?

No. Wood ear must always be fully rehydrated and cooked — usually blanched for 2–3 minutes — before eating. Raw or undercooked wood ear can be difficult to digest and may cause discomfort. Always use dried wood ear that has been properly soaked, rinsed, and cooked.

How long should I soak dried wood ear mushrooms?

Soak dried wood ear in cool water for 1–2 hours until soft and springy, or use warm water for about 30 minutes. Do not soak for many hours or overnight at room temperature, as this encourages bacterial growth. Once soaked, rinse well, trim any hard stems, and cook promptly.

Who should be cautious with wood ear mushroom?

People taking blood-thinning medication, those with bleeding disorders, and anyone preparing for surgery should consult a healthcare provider before eating wood ear regularly. Pregnant women and people with mushroom allergies should also seek professional advice. Always eat fully cooked wood ear in moderate portions.
