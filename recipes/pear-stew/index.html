Pear and Rock Sugar Stew — Soothing Dry Coughs

🥘 Recipe · 6 min read
Quick answer

In Chinese food therapy, a gently simmered pear stew is a familiar autumn comfort. This version pairs ripe pear with a modest amount of rock sugar and bulb (chuan bei mu, 母), an herb traditionally used in TCM formulas for dry, delicate cough patterns. The warm bowl is soothing and easy to eat; it is not a treatment for cough or any respiratory illness.

TCM perspective: nature, flavor, and tradition

In traditional Chinese dietary thought, pear is generally described as cool in nature and sweet in flavor, with an association with the Lung and Stomach systems. bulb is also traditionally regarded as cool and bitter-sweet. Practitioners have used it in particular formula contexts when dryness is prominent. Rock sugar contributes sweetness and rounds the flavor, but it is still added sugar, so a little is usually enough.

These descriptions belong to TCM theory and are not biomedical explanations. The classic materia medica tradition records foods and herbs according to their flavor, nature, and traditional applications. Individual constitutions and patterns differ, so the combination is best understood as a traditional food preparation rather than a one-size-fits-all remedy.

Pear and rock sugar stew

Ingredients (serves 2)

  • 1 firm-ripe Asian pear, washed
  • 3–5 g dried bulb, purchased from a reputable herbal supplier and prepared as directed on its label
  • 1–2 teaspoons rock sugar, to taste
  • 250–350 ml water
  • Optional: 2–3 goji berries for color and a mild fruity note

Method

  1. Rinse the pear. Cut a lid from the stem end and scoop out the core, keeping the pear shell intact, or peel and cut it into bite-size pieces for a simpler stovetop version.
  2. For the whole-pear presentation, place the pear in a heatproof bowl. Add the and rock sugar to the cavity, then pour water into the bowl. For the stovetop version, add all ingredients to a small covered saucepan.
  3. Steam the bowl over gently simmering water for about 45–60 minutes, or simmer the saucepan on low for 25–35 minutes, until the pear is tender. Add a splash of water if the pan becomes dry.
  4. Cool until comfortably warm. Eat the pear and sip the cooking liquid. If using goji berries, add them for the final 5 minutes.

Choosing and serving

Choose an unbruised pear that feels heavy for its size. Dried should come from a clearly labeled, reputable source; do not substitute an unidentified wild bulb or assume that more is better. Follow the supplier’s preparation and serving directions. If you cannot obtain a reliably identified product, make the simple pear-and-rock-sugar version without the herb.

Serve one portion warm as an occasional seasonal dessert or light snack. In TCM food therapy, preparations like this are often chosen according to the season and a person’s overall pattern, not just a single symptom. There is no universally established food-therapy dose or schedule for this recipe.

Variations

  • Less sweet: omit the rock sugar or use just a small piece; ripe pear already provides sweetness.
  • Without steaming equipment: peel and dice the pear, then simmer it covered in a small saucepan with water until soft.
  • For a simple family dessert: leave out and add a few goji berries near the end. This changes the traditional herbal combination, so describe it simply as pear stew rather than an herbal formula.

Frequently asked questions

Can I eat pear and rock sugar stew every day?

This recipe is best treated as an occasional food, not a daily prescription. TCM dietary choices are traditionally adjusted to the person and season; there is no standard evidence-based daily dose for this stew.

Can I make it without ?

Yes. Pear gently cooked with water and a little or no rock sugar makes a simple dessert. The herbal ingredient is optional and should only be used when correctly identified and suitable for you.

Is it suitable during individual health circumstances or for young readers?

Do not add medicinal herbs for individual health circumstances, individual health circumstances, or a young reader without advice from a qualified healthcare professional. The plain cooked pear version may still need adjustment for individual dietary needs.

Does it treat a cough?

No food can be promised to treat or cure a cough. This dish is traditionally enjoyed as a soothing food in some TCM contexts, but persistent or concerning symptoms need appropriate medical evaluation.
