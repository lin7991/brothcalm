Winter Melon Soup — A Light Broth for Damp-Heat Days

🥘 Recipe · 6 min read

Every Chinese kitchen has a soup for the sticky days — the ones when the air feels thick, the skin feels clammy, and appetite quietly disappears. Winter melon soup is that soup. It is pale, almost translucent, faintly sweet, and it goes from cutting board to table in well under an hour. In the language of Chinese food therapy (食疗, shí liáo), this is the bowl you reach for when the body is carrying too much heat and too much dampness at once.

The star ingredient is winter melon (冬瓜, dōng guā), the enormous waxy green gourd that shows up in Asian markets in sizes that defy the shopping basket. Despite the name, it is a warm-season crop, harvested in late summer and autumn, and it keeps for months in a cool room. That shelf life is the reason the name stuck: it was the gourd that carried a household through the winter.

What makes it remarkable in the pot is how neutral it tastes on its own and how much it gives to a broth. Cooked, it turns silky and sweet, and it absorbs whatever aromatics you put beside it. The result is a soup that is cooling without being bitter, light without being thin, and genuinely refreshing on a day when nothing heavier sounds appealing.

Winter Melon in TCM: Cold, Sweet, and Gently Draining

In TCM theory, winter melon is classified as cold in thermal nature with a sweet and bland flavor profile — the combination TCM calls sweet-bland (甘淡, gān dàn). The bland flavor is significant: it is traditionally associated with the ability to promote urination and move fluids downward and outward, which is exactly the quality that makes winter melon a summer and damp-season favorite.

It is believed to enter the Lung, Small Intestine, and Bladder meridians. Through those channels it is traditionally used to:

  • Clear heat — especially the low-grade, muggy kind that shows up as a warm chest, thirst, or a restless feeling on humid afternoons.
  • Promote the movement of fluids — encouraging the body to release what it is holding rather than storing it.
  • Gently drain dampness — the reason it appears in so many traditional dishes for puffiness, heaviness, and sluggish digestion.
  • Dissolve phlegm — a classical use that ties winter melon to the throat and the lungs rather than to the stomach alone.

Two parts of the plant are used in Chinese kitchens and pharmacies. The flesh is the food, mild and hydrating. The green rind — winter melon peel (冬瓜皮, dōng guā pí) — is considered the more strongly draining portion and is traditionally simmered into soups and teas when swelling or water retention is the concern. Home cooks often simmer a few strips of the rind in the pot and lift them out before serving, which captures the effect without adding a chewy texture to the bowl.

Modern nutrition tells a consistent story from a different angle. Winter melon is roughly 96 percent water, very low in calories, and supplies vitamin C, potassium, and dietary fibre. The Chinese character for winter melon contains the character for melon, but you will sometimes see it described in English as ash gourd or wax gourd — all three names point to the same pale-fleshed gourd.

What the Classics Say

Winter melon has been recorded in Chinese materia medica for well over a thousand years. The Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》) describes it among foods that clear heat and benefit the water passages, and later texts repeat its traditional association with the bladder and the small intestine. The folk saying that winter melon is the food for the swelling of summer reflects an observation that anyone who cooks with it will recognise: a bowl of this soup leaves a person feeling lighter, less puffed, and noticeably less thirsty.

What the tradition also notes — and what modern cooks easily miss — is that winter melon is meant to be eaten with something warming in cold weather. Ginger, scallion, and white pepper are not decoration in this recipe. In TCM terms they keep a cold food from chilling the digestion, and they are the reason the same soup can be adapted from the heat of August to a winter evening.

Ingredients (Serves 4)

  • 900 g winter melon, peeled, seeded, and cut into 2 cm cubes (about half a medium gourd)
  • 1.2 litres water or light unsalted chicken stock
  • 4 thin slices fresh ginger
  • 2 scallions, white parts bruised with the flat of a knife, green parts thinly sliced
  • 1 teaspoon light soy sauce (optional, for a savoury edge)
  • 1 teaspoon toasted sesame oil
  • ¼ teaspoon white pepper
  • Salt to taste
  • Optional for depth: 150 g pork ribs (blanched) or 20 g dried scallop
  • Optional for dampness: 30 g Job's tears (coix seed), soaked 2 hours, or a handful of adzuki beans

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Prepare the melon. Halve the winter melon, scoop out the seeds and soft fibre, and cut away the hard green skin. Cut the pale flesh into 2 cm cubes — uniform pieces cook evenly and keep their shape. If you want the traditional draining effect, wash two strips of rind and tie them in a small muslin or cheesecloth bag to simmer with the broth.
  2. Build the base. Pour the water or stock into a medium pot, add the ginger and the bruised scallion whites, and bring to a gentle simmer over medium heat. Let it infuse for 5 minutes. This short step is what keeps the finished soup from tasting like hot water with vegetables in it.
  3. Add any meat now, not later. If you are using pork ribs, blanch them in a separate pot of boiling water for 3 minutes, drain, then add them to the base and simmer for 25 minutes before the melon goes in. Dried scallop can go straight into the base and needs the same 25 minutes to release its flavour.
  4. Add the melon. Stir in the winter melon cubes and the soaked Job's tears if using. Simmer uncovered for 15–20 minutes. You are looking for cubes that have turned translucent and yield easily to a spoon but have not collapsed into the broth.
  5. Season off the heat. Turn off the burner, then add the soy sauce, sesame oil, and white pepper. Taste before salting — stock and dried scallop both bring their own sodium, and the melon adds a faint natural sweetness that salt can easily overwhelm.
  6. Serve. Fish out the ginger slices and the rind bag if you used one. Ladle into bowls, scatter the green scallion over the top, and serve warm. It works as a light first course, or alongside steamed rice as a simple supper.

Variations Worth Trying

  • Hearty pork rib version: Keep the blanched ribs in the pot and serve them with the soup. This turns a light broth into a full meal and is the version most often eaten as a family dinner in southern China.
  • Dampness-clearing version: Add 30 g of Job's tears or a small handful of adzuki beans along with the melon. Both are traditionally used to support the body in resolving dampness, and they make the soup more substantial without making it rich.
  • Seafood version: Use 20 g of dried scallop or a handful of dried shrimp in the base. The seafood gives the broth an unmistakable savoury depth that pairs beautifully with the melon's sweetness.
  • Vegetarian version: Replace the stock with water plus four dried shiitake mushrooms and a small piece of kombu simmered for 20 minutes. Strain, then proceed with the recipe. Shiitake is traditionally believed to support Qi, which balances the cooling melon.
  • Warming version for cold constitutions: Increase the ginger to 6 slices, add two cloves of crushed garlic and a few white peppercorns, and finish with a drop of chilli oil. This is the version to make if you feel the cold easily but still want the soup's lightening effect.
  • Winter melon and barley style: Simmer the melon with a spoonful of pearl barley for 30 minutes for a thicker, more porridge-like soup. It is filling and mild — a good choice for anyone with a delicate appetite.

When to Eat Winter Melon Soup

This is a warm-weather soup first and foremost. Late summer and early autumn, when the melon is at its best and the humidity is at its worst, is when the dish feels most obviously right. It is also a practical reset after a stretch of rich, oily, or celebratory meals — the kind of food that leaves a person feeling puffed and slow the next morning.

In TCM food therapy, timing matters as much as ingredients. A cooling soup eaten in the heat of July supports the body; the same soup eaten in the depths of January without warming aromatics can leave a person feeling chilled. If you want to enjoy this dish year-round, keep the ginger and white pepper generous in the cold months, and keep the plain version as a summer specialty. For more on how season and climate shape the table, see our guide to eating with the seasons.

As for frequency, two to four bowls a week through the humid months is a comfortable traditional rhythm. Served as a first course with rice and a steamed vegetable, it fits the TCM habit of building balance across a whole meal rather than asking one dish to do everything. It also makes a natural companion to lighter summer eating, such as the dishes in our summer cooling foods guide.

Who Should Be Careful

Winter melon's cooling, draining nature is the whole point of the dish — and also the thing to watch. People with a cold or deficient constitution, who tend to feel chilly, have loose stools, a weak appetite, or low energy, may find that a large bowl of plain winter melon soup leaves them feeling cold and unsettled. If that sounds like you, either enjoy it in smaller portions, always cook it with ginger and white pepper, or choose a warming soup instead, such as our silkie chicken soup in the colder months.

Because winter melon is very high in water and very low in calories, it should be part of a meal rather than a meal in itself for anyone who is underweight, recovering from illness, or supporting a growing family. It is also traditionally eaten less often by people who already wake frequently at night to urinate.

If you are pregnant or nursing, or if you take prescription medication — particularly diuretics or blood pressure medication — speak with a qualified healthcare provider before making winter melon a regular habit. As with all TCM food therapy, this soup is a complementary practice believed to support general wellbeing. It is not a substitute for diagnosis or treatment, and anyone with persistent swelling, unexplained weight change, or ongoing digestive symptoms should seek medical care.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is winter melon soup used for in TCM?

Winter melon is classified as cold and sweet-bland, and is believed to enter the Lung, Small Intestine, and Bladder meridians. Traditionally it is used to clear heat, move fluids, and gently drain dampness — which is why the soup is a classic on hot, humid days or whenever a person feels puffy and overheated.

Should winter melon be peeled for soup?

Everyday cooking usually peels it because the rind is hard. In TCM the green rind is considered the most strongly draining part, so a practical approach is to simmer two or three strips in a muslin bag with the broth and remove it before serving.

How often can I eat winter melon soup?

Two to four times a week through warm and humid weather is a comfortable traditional rhythm, and less often in cold months unless it is balanced with ginger and white pepper. TCM food therapy adjusts frequency to the season and the person rather than following a fixed rule.

Who should avoid or limit winter melon soup?

Those with a cold or deficient constitution — feeling chilly often, loose stools, weak appetite — are usually advised to limit it or cook it with generous ginger. Anyone who wakes frequently at night to urinate may also want to enjoy it less often.

Can winter melon soup be made vegetarian?

Yes. Use water or light vegetable stock with dried shiitake and a small piece of kombu for savoury depth, and finish with light soy sauce and sesame oil. It is one of the easiest Chinese soups to make meat-free.

Final Thoughts

Winter melon soup is proof that Chinese food therapy does not need rare herbs or long simmering to be useful. A gourd, a litre of water, a few slices of ginger, and half an hour will produce a bowl that cools without bitterness and lightens without stripping flavour. It is the kind of dish that teaches you to notice what your body is asking for — and in the middle of a humid week, it usually asks for exactly this.

Try it once plain, then once with ginger pushed a little further and a handful of Job's tears in the pot. Between those two versions you will have a soup for almost every warm day of the year.
