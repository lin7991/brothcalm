Eating by Constitution — The TCM Body-Type Diet Guide

📖 TCM Theory · 9 min read

Two people can eat the exact same meal — one thrives on it, the other feels heavy, bloated, or restless. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the reason is body constitution (体质, tǐzhì): the inherited and developed pattern of your body's energy, temperature, and moisture. Food is not good or bad in isolation; it is good for a particular body. Eating by constitution is the ancient art of matching what is on your plate to who your body is.

What Is a "Body Constitution" in TCM?

In TCM theory, every person is born with a constitutional tendency shaped by genetics, birth season, and family history — then continuously molded by diet, climate, sleep, emotions, and lifestyle. Your constitution describes how your body tends to behave when in balance and where it tends to tip first when out of balance. Some people naturally run warm; others run cold. Some digest almost anything; others must be careful with rich or raw foods.

This is why TCM food therapy rarely prescribes one universal "healthy diet." Instead, it asks a simple question first: what kind of body is eating this food? Two classics of the tradition — the Huangdi Neijing (Yellow Emperor's Classic of Internal Medicine) and later works on food energetics — teach that the same ingredient can support one person while aggravating another. Constitutional eating is personalized by design, and it has been practiced for over two thousand years.

A useful, widely taught framework organizes bodies into nine constitution types. The list below introduces each one with its typical signals and its food affinities, so you can begin to recognize your own pattern.

The Nine Constitutions at a Glance

Most people are a blend of two or three patterns rather than a pure match to one. Read each description and notice which ones feel familiar — especially under stress or in a difficult season. Your dominant pattern is the best place to start.

1. Balanced Constitution (Píng Hé)

People with a balanced constitution have steady energy, sound sleep, good appetite, and a calm emotional baseline. They tolerate most foods and recover quickly from minor upsets. In TCM theory this is the ideal state to protect, not correct.

Eat more: a wide rotation of seasonal whole foods, warm cooked grains, and colorful vegetables.

Keep doing: regular meals, moderate portions, and eating with the seasons — see our seasonal eating guide.

2. Qi Deficiency (Qì Xū)

Qi (vital energy) deficiency tends to show up as low energy, a soft voice, easy fatigue after activity, poor appetite, and loose stools. People may feel tired even after a full night's sleep and catch every cold that passes through the office. In TCM theory the spleen produces qi from food, so this pattern is closely linked to digestion.

Favor: gently cooked, easily digested meals — congee, chicken soup, steamed vegetables — plus qi-supporting foods like astragalus, Chinese yam, and jujube dates.

Limit: large raw salads, iced drinks, and very pungent spices, which are believed to burden the digestive system and use up qi.

3. Yang Deficiency (Yáng Xū)

Yang is the warming, activating side of the body. Yang deficiency classically shows up as cold hands and feet, a preference for warm rooms, a pale complexion, low motivation, and a tendency to feel chilly when others are comfortable. Digestion may be slow, with loose stools and little thirst.

Favor: warming foods and drinks — ginger, cinnamon, longan, scallions, and slow-simmered soups such as herbal chicken soup. A cup of ginger tea in the morning is a classic choice.

Limit: raw and cold foods, iced beverages, and excessive tropical fruit, which are considered cooling in food energetics.

You may also enjoy our guide to foods for cold hands and feet.

4. Yin Deficiency (Yīn Xū)

Yin is the moistening, cooling, and resting side of the body. Yin deficiency tends to appear as dryness — dry skin, dry throat, scanty sleep, a warm feeling in the palms and soles, and a tendency to feel restless or warm in the evening. It is often compared to a pot of water simmering too low over a fire that is too high.

Favor: moistening, cooling-nourishing foods such as snow fungus, goji berries, lotus seeds, and pears; gentle teas like goji chrysanthemum tea.

Limit: heavy spice, alcohol, late nights, and overly hot, drying foods.

Our foods for dry skin guide covers this pattern in more depth.

5. Phlegm-Dampness Constitution (Tán Shī)

Phlegm-dampness describes a body that tends to accumulate moisture: a feeling of heaviness, easy weight gain around the middle, a sticky or sweet taste in the mouth, thick phlegm, and a foggy head. In TCM theory this often traces back to a spleen that struggles to transform food and fluids — the same mechanism explained in our article on dampness in TCM.

Favor: light, drying, spleen-supporting foods — poria, chen pi (tangerine peel), adzuki beans, barley, daikon radish, and Chinese yam.

Limit: dairy, fried food, sweets, and excessive beer, which are traditionally considered damp-producing.

6. Damp-Heat Constitution (Shī Rè)

When dampness combines with heat, the pattern shifts: oily or acne-prone skin, a bitter taste, strong body odor, heavy limbs, irritability, and sometimes yellowed eyes or urine. The body feels both sticky and hot, like a humid summer afternoon.

Favor: clearing, cooling foods — mung beans, bitter melon, cucumber, celery, and teas like honeysuckle tea, chrysanthemum tea, or lotus leaf tea.

Limit: alcohol, fried and greasy food, chili-heavy dishes, and late-night eating.

7. Blood Stasis Constitution (Xuè Yū)

Blood stasis refers to blood that does not move smoothly. Typical signals include easy bruising, dark circles under the eyes, a dusky or dull complexion, dry skin patches, and fixed, stabbing pains. In TCM theory moving blood requires warmth, movement, and qi.

Favor: foods traditionally used to promote circulation — hawthorn berries, black beans, scallions, walnuts, and a little ginger in daily cooking.

Limit: a sedentary routine, excessive cold foods, and emotional suppression — daily gentle movement matters as much as diet.

8. Qi Stagnation Constitution (Qì Yù)

Qi stagnation is primarily an emotional-movement pattern: frequent sighing, mood swings, a feeling of a lump in the throat, bloating, and chest or rib-side tightness that shifts with stress. In TCM theory the liver governs the smooth flow of qi, so this pattern is sometimes called liver qi stagnation.

Favor: aromatic, uplifting foods and teas — mint, rose tea, jasmine tea, chen pi, and scallions.

Limit: heavy greasy meals and excess alcohol; pair dietary care with stress practices. See foods that calm anxiety and the liver qi stagnation guide.

9. Special Constitution (Tè Bǐng)

The special, or allergic, constitution covers people with strong sensitivities — seasonal allergies, food sensitivities, asthma, or skin reactions. In TCM theory this is often discussed as an inherited vulnerability that can be supported by strengthening the body's defensive qi (wèi qì) and protecting the lungs and spleen.

Favor: simple, whole, cooked foods and traditionally supportive ingredients such as shiitake and mushrooms; keep meals regular and the digestive tract calm.

Limit: known personal triggers, heavy processed foods, and excess cold drinks; work with a qualified practitioner for individualized guidance.

How to Find Your Own Pattern

You do not need a formal diagnosis to begin eating more constitutionally. Try this gentle self-observation for one week:

  • Temperature: do you run warm or cold? Do your hands and feet stay cold easily?
  • Energy: do you feel steady through the day, or crash after meals and activity?
  • Digestion: how do you react to raw food, dairy, rich food, or iced drinks?
  • Moisture: is your skin dry, oily, or easily damp and sticky? Any phlegm or a sticky mouth?
  • Emotions: are you more prone to irritability, worry, low mood, or sighing?
  • Sleep: do you sleep deeply, or feel hot, restless, or wakeful at night?

Keep a simple note of what you eat and how you feel two hours later. Patterns usually appear quickly — for example, if cold drinks reliably leave you bloated and tired, your body is telling you it prefers warmth. For a precise reading, a TCM practitioner uses pulse and tongue diagnosis to confirm your pattern, but self-observation is a valid starting point.

Eat for Your Body, Not Against It

Once you recognize your dominant tendency, the practical rule is simple: favor foods whose energetics balance your pattern, and limit foods that push it further. The warmer foods are not "better" than cooling foods — they are better for a cold body. The same logic runs through every five flavors discussion and food energetics guide on BrothCalm.

  • Cold and yang-deficient bodies → lean toward warm, cooked, gently spiced meals.
  • Dry and yin-deficient bodies → lean toward moistening, nourishing soups and stews.
  • Heavy, damp bodies → lean toward light grains, vegetables, and drying aromatics.
  • Hot, irritable bodies → lean toward cooling teas and lightly cooked greens.

Constitutions also shift with the seasons — the same person may need warming food in winter and lighter fare in summer. Our seasonal eating guide shows how to layer the two.

A Gentle Way to Start (Two Weeks, No Extremes)

Constitutional eating is not a strict elimination diet. It works best as a gradual rebalancing:

  • Week 1 — observe. Keep the food diary above and identify your strongest pattern.
  • Week 1 — one warm meal a day. If you are cold, tired, or prone to dampness, start the day with congee or a warm soup; if you run hot, choose lighter cooked greens and a cooling tea.
  • Week 2 — swap, don't remove. Replace one daily trigger (iced drink, heavy fried snack) with a constitutional alternative such as goji jujube tea for energy or calming tea in the evening.
  • Week 2 — notice. Track energy, digestion, sleep, and mood. Small shifts in the right direction usually appear within days.

Common Questions About Eating by Constitution

Is one constitution "better" than another?

No. In TCM theory every constitution has strengths and tendencies; the goal is balance, not conversion to a single ideal type. A person with a yang-deficient pattern who eats warmly and rests well can be just as healthy as a balanced-constitution type who does the same.

Can my constitution change over time?

Yes. While you are born with an inherited tendency, TCM theory holds that diet, seasons, life stage, and habits continuously shape your pattern. Many people find their dominant tendency shifts — for example, becoming more yin-deficient after years of late nights, or more damp in humid climates. Re-checking your pattern seasonally keeps your food choices relevant.

Do I need to avoid my "limit" foods forever?

Not necessarily. Constitutional eating is about tendency, not prohibition. Once your body feels more balanced, most people can enjoy small amounts of less-aligned foods occasionally — the key is noticing how your body responds and returning to supportive choices when needed.

Final Thoughts

Eating by constitution is one of the most practical gifts of TCM food therapy: it replaces one-size-fits-all diet rules with a personalized lens that respects your body's unique design. Start with observation, make gentle swaps, and let your energy, digestion, and mood be your guide. For a deeper foundation, explore how food energetics, the five flavors, and the five elements fit together — and remember that constitutional eating complements, never replaces, professional medical care.
