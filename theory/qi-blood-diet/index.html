What Are Qi and Blood in TCM?

In Western medicine, "blood" is a measurable fluid pumped by the heart. In TCM, Blood (with a capital B) is a broader idea: it is the dense, moist substance that physically nourishes the body, but it is also understood to carry the mind and spirit, moisten the skin and hair, and keep the sinews flexible. Qi, by contrast, is the energetic side of life — the invisible force behind movement, warmth, digestion, and immunity.

Think of it this way: Qi is the electricity that makes a lamp glow, and Blood is the current that carries it. They are inseparable partners. TCM has two famous sayings about their relationship:

  • Qi is the commander of Blood. Qi pushes Blood through the vessels, holds it in place, and "generates" it from the food we eat.
  • Blood is the mother of Qi. Blood gives Qi a physical home and a source of material support. When Blood is thin, Qi has nothing to anchor to.

This is why a qi and blood diet almost never focuses on one at the expense of the other. Foods that are good for one usually support both, and traditional meals are designed around that partnership.

Signs That a Qi-Blood Diet May Be Relevant

Only a trained TCM practitioner can diagnose a pattern, and the signs below are not a checklist for self-diagnosis. But they are the classic descriptions you will find in TCM texts, and they can help you notice whether your eating habits deserve attention.

Signs often associated with Qi deficiency:

  • Feeling tired even after a full night of sleep
  • A soft or weak voice, and shortness of breath after light activity
  • Poor appetite, loose stools, or a feeling of heaviness after meals
  • Easy sweating, especially with minimal exertion
  • A pale tongue with a thin white coating

Signs often associated with Blood deficiency:

  • A pale complexion, pale lips, and pale nail beds
  • Dry skin, dry hair, or brittle nails
  • Dizziness, poor memory, or difficulty concentrating
  • Scanty or irregular periods, or cold hands and feet
  • Poor sleep or a tendency to wake in the night

Because Qi and Blood deficiency frequently travel together, many people recognize a mix of both columns. That is exactly the situation a balanced qi-blood diet is designed for.

The TCM Logic of Eating for Qi and Blood

In TCM, the Spleen is described as the "source of Qi and Blood" — the organ system responsible for transforming food into usable energy and nourishment. If digestion is weak, even the best tonic foods will pass through without doing much good. This explains two core habits in Chinese food therapy:

1. Cook your food gently and thoroughly. Warm, slow-cooked meals — soups, stews, congee, and braises — are believed to pre-digest food and make it easier for the Spleen to transform. This is why the classic congee, a simple rice porridge, is treated almost like a medicine for people recovering their strength.

2. Prefer sweet, mild, and neutral foods. In the energetics of food, the sweet flavor is said to enter the Spleen, and foods with a neutral or slightly warm nature are considered gentle, long-term builders. Think jujube dates, yam, millet, chicken, and mushrooms — foods that feel like a warm blanket rather than a sudden jolt.

Qi-Tonic Foods: The Everyday Builders

Qi-tonic foods are traditionally described as foods that support energy, digestion, and resistance. They are usually mild enough to eat often, and they work best cooked into broths and grains.

Astragalus (Huang Qi)

Astragalus root is one of the most famous Qi tonics in TCM, traditionally added to soups and stews to support vitality and the body's defenses. A few slices simmered with chicken or mushrooms give a broth a gentle, earthy sweetness. See our astragalus guide for how to choose and cook with it.

Codonopsis (Dang Shen)

Codonopsis is often called the "poor man's ginseng" — a milder, more affordable Qi tonic that is believed to support the Spleen and appetite. It pairs beautifully with astragalus in a simple two-herb tea or soup base. Read more in our codonopsis guide.

Chinese Yam (Huai Shan)

Chinese yam is a food-first tonic: it is eaten as a vegetable, added to congee, or simmered in soups. In TCM it is traditionally used to support the Spleen, digestion, and steady energy. Our Chinese yam guide covers the many ways to cook it.

Jujube Dates (Hong Zao)

Jujube dates are the all-purpose sweetener of the Chinese medicinal kitchen — believed to support both Qi and Blood while gently harmonizing other herbs. They are cheap, easy to find, and delicious in tea, congee, and soup. Start with our jujube guide.

Also worth a place at your table

  • Chicken and bone broth — the classic base for Qi-supporting soups; try herbal chicken soup or a slow bone broth.
  • Millet — a small, warming grain traditionally used to support the Spleen; it makes a comforting morning millet congee.
  • Shiitake and other mushrooms — valued in TCM for grounded, building energy.

Blood-Nourishing Foods: The Deep Moisture

Blood-nourishing foods are traditionally described as foods that build the "material" side of the body — rich, dark, and moistening. Many of them are naturally sweet and deep in color, which is why TCM folk wisdom says "red foods feed the blood."

Goji Berries (Gou Qi Zi)

Goji berries are the most famous blood-nourishing food in the Chinese pantry. They are believed to support the eyes, the liver, and the "essence" of the body, and they are sweet enough to snack on by the handful. Learn how to choose and brew them in our goji berry guide.

Longan (Dragon Eye)

Dried longan is a warming, sweet fruit traditionally used to support Blood, calm the spirit, and encourage restful sleep. It is lovely in tea or a simple longan-date soup. See our longan guide for more.

Dang Gui (Angelica Root)

Dang gui is the classic "blood tonic" herb of TCM, traditionally added to soups for women's health and general nourishment. It has a distinctive aroma and is usually used in small amounts alongside food ingredients. Our dang gui guide explains how to use it safely.

Ejiao (Donkey-Hide Gelatin)

Ejiao is a rich gelatin traditionally prepared from donkey hide and valued as a deep blood nourisher in TCM. It is usually dissolved into warm soups or taken as a prepared supplement. Because it is rich, it is traditionally used in small amounts and with care. Read our ejiao guide before experimenting.

Also worth a place at your table

  • Dark leafy greens — spinach, watercress, and Chinese greens are everyday, food-first ways to support healthy blood.
  • Black sesame and red beans — staples in traditional blood-supporting desserts and porridges.
  • Red date + goji tea — the easiest daily habit; brew a pot with our goji-jujube tea recipe or the deeper longan-jujube tea.

Building a Qi-Blood Day: A Gentle Eating Pattern

You do not need a cabinet full of herbs to eat for Qi and Blood. The traditional pattern is simple, repetitive, and kind to the digestion. Here is what a day might look like:

Breakfast — start warm. A bowl of congee or millet porridge cooked with a few jujube dates and goji berries gives the Spleen an easy first task. If you prefer eggs, a steamed egg custard is another gentle, nourishing start.

Lunch or dinner — soup at least a few times a week. Slow-simmered soups are the heart of qi-blood eating. Rotate between herbal chicken soup with astragalus and codonopsis, a chicken and yam soup for digestion, and a simple bone broth as a cooking base for vegetables and grains.

Mid-afternoon — a small cup of tea. A warm goji-jujube tea is a pleasant, low-effort way to include blood-nourishing foods every single day.

The cooking mindset — low and slow. Whether it is a soup, a congee, or a braise, gentle heat and enough time are part of the recipe. TCM texts describe this kind of cooking as "transforming" food — turning raw ingredients into the easily absorbed nourishment the body can actually use.

What to Keep in Mind

Qi- and Blood-tonic foods are gentle by design, but a few cautions keep them useful rather than heavy:

  • Watch for Dampness. If you feel bloated, heavy, or unusually sticky after rich foods, your body may be telling you to lighten up. Sweet, sticky tonics can feed Dampness, so balance them with vegetables, light broths, and movement.
  • Not every tonic suits every person. Strong Qi herbs like ginseng are traditionally used with care when there are signs of excess heat or high blood pressure, and Dang Gui is traditionally avoided during pregnancy unless a practitioner advises otherwise.
  • Start small and listen. Add one or two foods, not ten. Food therapy works through steady, repeated nourishment — not through occasional feasts of expensive herbs.
  • See a professional for real patterns. These foods are complements, not replacements. If fatigue, pallor, or heavy periods are affecting your life, speak to a healthcare provider and a qualified TCM practitioner.

In the end, the qi and blood diet is less a prescription than a return to an older way of eating: warm food, cooked slowly, shared regularly, and chosen with intention.
