Tea as Medicine — The TCM Roots of Your Daily Brew

📖 TCM Theory · 9 min read

Your morning cup of tea is more than a caffeine ritual. In China, tea has been called yào chá (药茶) — literally "medicine tea" — for more than a thousand years, and the idea that a simple brew can gently support the body is one of the oldest continuous traditions in East Asian wellness. This article explores the TCM roots of tea drinking: where the "tea as medicine" idea came from, how different teas are understood energetically, and how to choose a brew that suits your own body.

Where the Idea of Tea as Medicine Began

The origin story of tea is a medical one. According to Chinese legend, the mythical ruler Shennong (神农), the "Divine Farmer" who is credited with tasting hundreds of herbs, discovered tea when leaves drifted into his pot of boiling water. The Shennong Bencao Jing (《神农本草经》), one of the oldest Chinese pharmacopoeias, records that tea "tastes bitter and cold, and is traditionally used to help the body expel what it does not need and to clear the mind."

By the Tang dynasty (618–907), tea drinking had grown into a refined art. The celebrated writer Lu Yu (陆羽) composed the Classic of Tea (《茶经》), the first book in the world devoted entirely to tea, in which he described tea not only as a pleasure but as a companion to a well-ordered life. In later centuries, physicians such as Li Shizhen, author of the Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》, 1596), included tea among the everyday substances that could be used to harmonize the body. Tea in China has therefore never been purely a drink — it has always occupied the middle ground between food, tonic, and ritual.

How TCM Understands Tea: Nature, Flavor, and Movement

In TCM theory, every food and drink has an energetic signature made up of its nature (性), its flavor (味), and the organs or pathways it is believed to influence. Tea is no exception.

  • Nature: Plain tea leaves are traditionally classified as slightly cool or cold in nature, although processing changes this — a theme we return to below.
  • Flavor: Tea is described as bitter (苦) and sweet (甘). In the framework of the five flavors, bitterness is said to drain and dry, while sweetness harmonizes and nourishes.
  • Movement: Tea is traditionally said to have an upward, lightening movement — it is believed to help "clear the head," lift the spirits, and support mental alertness.

Because tea is classified as slightly cooling, traditional guidance has long warned that people who feel cold easily, have weak digestion, or are recovering from illness should drink tea in moderation or choose processed, warmer varieties. This is not a modern diet rule — it appears in classical Chinese medical commentaries written centuries ago.

The Same Leaf, Different Medicines: Tea Processing in TCM

One of the most elegant ideas in Chinese tea culture is that all tea comes from the same plant, Camellia sinensis, and that the way the leaves are processed changes their energetic character. This is why TCM-minded tea drinkers do not simply ask "do you like tea?" — they ask which kind suits the season and the person.

Green tea (绿茶) — cool and clearing

Unfermented and lightly processed, green tea is believed to keep the most of the leaf's original cool, clearing nature. In TCM terms it is traditionally used to help clear heat, refresh the mind, and support the body in warm weather. If you feel hot, dry, or restless, a cup of green tea in the daytime may feel naturally balancing.

White tea (白茶) — gently mild

White tea is only slightly withered and dried, and is traditionally considered mild and gentle, cooler than black tea but less harsh than a strong green. Aged white tea is particularly treasured, with some TCM practitioners believing that years of resting soften its nature further.

Oolong tea (乌龙茶) — the middle path

Partially oxidized, oolong sits between green and black tea. In folk tradition it is often described as more balanced — not too cold, not too hot — and is popularly paired with rich meals to support comfortable digestion.

Black tea (红茶) and ripe pu-erh (熟普洱) — warm and earthy

Fully oxidized black tea and fermented ripe pu-erh are traditionally regarded as the warmest teas. In TCM language, their processing is said to transform the leaf's cool nature into a warm, settling one that is gentler on the stomach. In Chinese homes, black tea with a little honey or jujube is a classic cold-season drink, while ripe pu-erh is a common companion to heavy food.

You can feel this spectrum in your own body. A large glass of cold-brewed green tea leaves many people feeling refreshed but slightly chilled; a cup of aged ripe pu-erh tends to feel grounding and warming. The tradition simply asks you to notice the difference.

Tea and the Seasons: A Living Calendar

Chinese dietary therapy pays close attention to the seasons, and tea drinking has always followed the annual cycle. In the framework of seasonal eating, the traditional pattern goes something like this:

  • Spring: Fresh green teas and light floral brews are favored as the year turns toward growth and renewal.
  • Summer: Cooling teas — green tea, white tea, and classic summer drinks like chrysanthemum tea — are believed to help the body cope with heat.
  • Autumn: As the air turns dry, many people shift toward mildly moistening brews and begin cutting back on cold tea.
  • Winter: Black tea, ripe pu-erh, oolong, and warming blends with ginger or jujube become the teas of choice.

Notice that this is not a rulebook but an observation of what generations of tea drinkers found comfortable. The same logic explains why your local tea shop sells chrysanthemum tea in summer and ginger-black tea in winter without ever reading a TCM textbook.

Herbal Teas: Where "Tea" Becomes a Tonic

Strictly speaking, TCM's most famous "teas" are often not made from tea leaves at all. Chinese herbal teas combine flowers, fruits, roots, and grains, and each has its own traditional purpose. Popular examples on BrothCalm include:

  • Chrysanthemum tea — traditionally used to help clear heat and support eye comfort for people who spend long hours looking at screens.
  • Calming tea — a soothing blend traditionally used to help quiet an overactive mind in the evening.
  • Goji-jujube tea — a sweet, nourishing brew traditionally used to support energy and blood.

Because herbal teas are caffeine-free and chosen for a specific effect, they are the closest everyday parallel to the old ideal of tea as a gentle, personalized remedy. You can think of plain tea as the daily baseline and herbal teas as the targeted tune-up.

Practical Guidance: Drinking Tea the Traditional Way

How you brew and drink tea matters as much as which tea you choose. Here are practical habits drawn from Chinese tea culture that anyone can adopt:

Match the tea to your body and the clock

If you tend to feel cold and tired, favor warm, processed teas and drink them mid-morning or early afternoon. If you run hot, a cool green or white tea in the daytime may suit you better. Most traditional guidance suggests avoiding strong tea late at night, as its lifting quality may interfere with sleep.

Do not drink tea on a completely empty stomach

Classical Chinese advice repeatedly warns against very strong tea first thing in the morning on an empty stomach, especially for people with sensitive digestion. If you like an early cup, have a little food first or choose a gentler, warmer tea.

Brew it properly

Use water that has just stopped boiling rather than violently boiling water for delicate green and white teas. Let black tea and pu-erh steep a little longer for a fuller, warmer cup. In Chinese practice, the second infusion is often considered the best — the leaves have opened and the flavor has softened.

Make tea a small ritual

TCM has always valued the mind as much as the meal. Pouring a cup, watching the leaves unfurl, and taking three slow breaths before the first sip is itself a calming practice — one reason tea rooms have been places of contemplation in China for over a thousand years.

Precautions and Who Should Be Mindful

Tea is one of the safest beverages in the world, but a few sensible precautions keep it in balance:

  • Cold-sensitive people: If you often have cold hands and feet, loose stools, or weak digestion, go easy on strong green tea and prefer black, oolong, or ripe pu-erh.
  • Pregnancy: Pregnant women are usually advised to limit caffeine, including tea, and to choose caffeine-free herbal blends after checking with a healthcare provider.
  • Sleep: If you are sensitive to caffeine, stop tea by mid-afternoon or choose naturally caffeine-free herbal brews in the evening.
  • Medications: Tea compounds can interact with some medications, particularly iron supplements and certain heart medications. Leave a gap of at least two hours between tea and medicine.
  • Overconsumption: Drinking very large amounts of very strong tea may cause dizziness, palpitations, or stomach discomfort — the Chinese phrase for this is zui cha (醉茶), or "tea drunkenness." If you feel it, slow down and have a small snack.

FAQ

Is tea considered medicine in traditional Chinese medicine?

Yes. In TCM, tea is traditionally viewed as both a beverage and a gentle remedy. Ancient texts such as the Shennong Bencao Jing describe tea as clearing heat and lifting the spirit, and it has been used for centuries as a daily tonic rather than a strong drug.

Which tea is best for a cold or tired body in TCM?

In TCM theory, people who often feel cold, tired, or have weak digestion are traditionally encouraged to choose warmer teas such as black tea, ripe pu-erh, or oolong, and to limit cooling green tea, especially on an empty stomach.

How does green tea differ from black tea in TCM energetics?

Green tea is unfermented and is believed to retain a cooler, more clearing nature, while black tea is fully oxidized and is traditionally regarded as warmer and more gentle on the stomach. The same leaf can behave differently depending on how it is processed.

When is the best time to drink tea according to Chinese tradition?

Many Chinese tea drinkers enjoy tea in the morning and early afternoon, when its gently lifting quality is traditionally thought to support alertness and digestion. Drinking strong tea late at night is usually discouraged because it may disturb sleep.
