Angelica Sinensis (Dang Gui) — The Female Ginseng

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 8 min read

Walk through any traditional Chinese medicine shop and you will notice a distinctive, earthy, slightly sweet aroma hanging in the air. More often than not, that fragrance comes from bundles of golden-brown root slices labelled dang gui (当归). In Chinese food therapy, this humble root holds a position of rare honour: it is the herb most closely associated with nourishing blood, calming the cycle of women's health, and warming the body from within.

Western herbalists know it as Angelica sinensis, a member of the same botanical family as carrots, celery, and parsley. Traditional healers know it by a friendlier name — "the female ginseng." This guide explores what TCM tradition says about dang gui, how to cook with it safely and deliciously, and the precautions everyone should know before adding it to the pantry.

What Is Dang Gui? A TCM Profile

In traditional Chinese medicine, every food and herb is described by its nature (性), its flavour (味), and the organ networks it is said to enter (归经). Dang gui's profile explains exactly why it earned its reputation:

  • Nature: warm (温)
  • Flavours: sweet, pungent, and slightly bitter
  • Meridians: traditionally said to enter the Liver, Heart, and Spleen
  • Traditional actions: nourishes and vitalises blood, regulates menstruation, moistens the intestines, and is believed to help move blood smoothly without leaving it stagnant

The warm nature is the key. In TCM theory, blood that is cold and sluggish is thought to pool and stagnate, which may contribute to discomfort, cramps, and dull complexions. Dang gui's warmth is traditionally believed to keep blood flowing the way a gentle stream keeps flowing in early spring — not too fast, not too slow.

Traditional Uses in TCM

The "Female Ginseng"

Dang gui has been written about for over two thousand years. It appears in the Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing (神农本草经), one of the oldest surviving materia medica texts, where it was classified as a "superior" herb — one considered safe for long-term use as part of a balanced lifestyle. Ancient texts describe it as a remedy for blood deficiency and for the complaints that often accompany it: fatigue, a pale complexion, dizziness, scanty menstruation, and a sensation of dryness.

The nickname "female ginseng" grew from its central role in gynaecological formulas. The most famous of these is Si Wu Tang (四物汤, Four Substances Decoction), a classical blend of dang gui, rehmannia, white peony, and chuanxiong that has been used for centuries as a foundational tonic for women's blood. TCM practitioners traditionally prescribe it for menstrual irregularities, painful periods, and recovery after childbirth.

Blood and Qi Together

In TCM theory, blood and qi (vital energy) support each other: qi moves the blood, and blood anchors the qi. For this reason, dang gui is rarely used alone. It is classically paired with astragalus (huang qi) in a combination known as Dang Gui Bu Xue Tang (当归补血汤), a formula traditionally said to generate both qi and blood. In the kitchen, this translates into soups that combine dang gui with astragalus, jujube dates, and goji berries — a warming, nourishing bowl for cooler months.

Moistening the Intestines

Because of its traditionally described moistening quality, dang gui has also been used for dryness-related constipation, particularly in older adults or people recovering from illness whose fluids are considered depleted. The oily texture of the root mirrors this function — in fact, quality dang gui is prized precisely for its rich, fatty, almost resinous interior.

How to Use Dang Gui in Food Therapy

Choosing and Storing Dang Gui

Selecting good dang gui is straightforward once you know what to look for:

  • Whole roots vs. slices: Whole roots keep their aroma longer; slices are more convenient for cooking. Both are widely available in Chinese grocery stores and online.
  • Appearance: Good slices are pale yellowish-brown with a dense, oily core and a strong, sweet, celery-like fragrance. Thin, dry, or crumbly slices have usually lost their potency.
  • Origin: The most famous growing region is Min County (岷县) in Gansu, China, which has supplied high-quality dang gui for centuries.
  • Storage: Keep dang gui in an airtight glass jar in a cool, dark, dry place, away from sunlight and strong odours. It will keep for up to a year; slices stored this way retain most of their aroma for several months.

Traditional Dosage in Food

In food therapy, dang gui is treated as a seasoning with benefits rather than a concentrated medicine. A traditional dose is 3 to 9 grams of dried root (roughly 5 to 15 thin slices) per pot of soup for 2 to 4 people. Simmer it slowly with the other ingredients, then remove the softened slices before serving or leave them in for texture — they are edible but fibrous. For a first try, start at the lower end of the range.

Recipe: Dang Gui & Astragalus Chicken Soup

This classic warming soup is the most popular way to enjoy dang gui at home:

Ingredients:

  • 1 small free-range chicken or 4 chicken drumsticks
  • 6-9 g dried dang gui slices (about 8-12 slices)
  • 10-15 g dried astragalus slices
  • 6-8 red jujube dates, pitted
  • 3-4 slices fresh ginger
  • 1 tablespoon goji berries
  • 1.5 litres water, plus salt to taste

Method:

  1. Blanch the chicken in boiling water for 3 minutes, then rinse to remove impurities.
  2. Rinse the dang gui, astragalus, jujube, and ginger under cold water.
  3. Place everything except the goji berries in a large pot with the water.
  4. Bring to a boil, skim off any foam, then reduce to a gentle simmer.
  5. Simmer covered for 1.5 to 2 hours until the chicken is very tender.
  6. Add the goji berries in the last 10 minutes, season with salt, and serve warm.

Enjoy one bowl as part of a balanced meal. Leftovers keep for 2-3 days refrigerated and can be gently reheated — though the broth is best savoured fresh.

A Simple Dang Gui Tea

For a lighter daily option, simmer 3-5 slices of dang gui with 2 sliced red dates and a few goji berries in 500 ml of water for 15 minutes. Strain and sip warm, ideally in the morning or early afternoon. It is traditionally recommended to avoid drinking this tea late at night, as its warming nature may keep some people alert.

Precautions and Who Should Avoid It

Dang gui is a beloved ingredient, but it is not for everyone. Respect these traditional cautions:

  • Pregnancy: Dang gui is traditionally avoided during pregnancy, particularly in the first trimester, because it is believed to stimulate blood circulation. If you are pregnant or planning to conceive, do not use it without professional guidance.
  • Heavy menstrual bleeding: Because dang gui is traditionally said to move blood, it may not suit people who already experience heavy or prolonged periods.
  • Loose stools or weak digestion: Its moistening, oily nature can aggravate diarrhoea or a "damp" digestive state in TCM terms.
  • Heat signs: People with signs of excess heat — red face, dry mouth, irritability, sore throat — are traditionally advised to use it cautiously.
  • Blood-thinning medication: Dang gui may interact with anticoagulants such as warfarin. Always tell your doctor about any herbal ingredients you are using.

As with all herbs, more is not better. Large amounts of concentrated dang gui extract are not the same as food-therapy use and should only be taken under the guidance of a qualified TCM practitioner.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is dang gui and why is it called the female ginseng?

Dang gui is the root of Angelica sinensis. In TCM it is traditionally regarded as a primary herb for nourishing and moving blood, which is why it has long been associated with women's health and earned the nickname "female ginseng."

Is dang gui safe during pregnancy?

Traditionally, dang gui is avoided during pregnancy, especially in the first trimester. If you are pregnant, planning to conceive, or breastfeeding, speak with a qualified healthcare provider before using it in any form.

How much dang gui should I use in cooking?

A traditional food-therapy dose is roughly 3 to 9 grams of dried root per pot of soup, shared over several servings. Start low, simmer it with food, and stop if you notice any discomfort.

Can men take dang gui?

Yes. Although it is famous as a women's tonic, TCM theory describes it as a blood-nourishing herb that may support anyone with signs of blood deficiency, such as fatigue or a pale complexion.

Bringing Dang Gui into Your Kitchen

Dang gui rewards patience. It is not a dramatic ingredient; it is a steady one — a warm, fragrant root that has quietly supported kitchens and families for two millennia. Whether you simmer it into a Sunday chicken soup, sip it as a simple tea, or simply keep a jar of slices in the pantry for colder months, you are continuing a tradition that TCM texts have honoured since the age of the Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing.

Start small, listen to your body, and always pair food therapy with the advice of a qualified healthcare professional — especially if you are pregnant, on medication, or managing a chronic condition.
