Snow Fungus (Yin Er) — The Collagen-Building Yin Tonic in TCM

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 7 min read

In Chinese food therapy, few ingredients carry as much beauty folklore as snow fungus. Known in Mandarin as yin er (银耳) or xue er (雪耳), this pale, jelly-like mushroom has been simmered into sweet soups for centuries and is often called the "poor man's bird's nest" because it was believed to offer similar nourishing qualities at a fraction of the price. Today it appears in skincare aisles and wellness blogs alike, but its roots run deep in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) as a gentle yin tonic for the lungs, skin, and digestive tract. In this guide, we explore the traditional properties of snow fungus, why it earned its collagen-building reputation, and how to turn it into simple, nourishing soups at home.

What Is Snow Fungus?

Snow fungus is the common name for Tremella fuciformis, an edible jelly fungus that grows on hardwood trees, most famously in the mountain forests of Fujian and Sichuan provinces in China. In its dried form it looks like a crumpled pale-golden flower; once soaked and simmered it expands into translucent, gelatinous ribbons with a delicate, almost neutral taste. Unlike many medicinal mushrooms that taste bitter or earthy, snow fungus is prized precisely because it absorbs the flavors around it while contributing a luxurious, silky texture. The same species is also sold under the name white fungus, and you may find both labels on the same product.

TCM Properties: Nature, Flavor, and Meridians

In TCM food energetics, every food is described by its nature (hot, warm, neutral, cool, or cold), its flavor, and the organ systems it is believed to influence. Snow fungus is traditionally classified as:

  • Nature: Neutral to slightly cool — gentle enough for daily use and for people with heat signs.
  • Flavor: Sweet — sweet foods in TCM are believed to harmonize the middle and nourish the body gently.
  • Meridians: Lung and Stomach — the organ systems responsible for respiration, moisture, and digestion.

Because it enters the Lung and Stomach meridians, snow fungus is traditionally used to nourish yin (the moistening, cooling aspect of the body), generate fluids, and moisten dryness. In TCM theory, lung yin is closely connected to the condition of the skin — the phrase "the lungs govern the skin and body hair" is a cornerstone of Chinese dermatological thinking. This is why snow fungus appears so often in food therapy for dry skin, dry cough, and autumn dryness.

Traditional Uses: The "Poor Man's Bird's Nest"

Chinese herbal literature has long regarded bird's nest — the edible nest of the swiftlet — as one of the most precious yin-nourishing delicacies. Snow fungus became known as its humble cousin because it was believed to share similar moistening and skin-supporting qualities for a small fraction of the cost. Historical records describe it being simmered slowly into soups for the elderly, for people recovering from illness, and for women seeking to maintain youthful, hydrated skin. In classic TCM food therapy texts, snow fungus is recommended for symptoms associated with dryness: a dry, tickling cough, parched throat, dry skin, and constipation related to insufficient fluids. It is not a strong medicine; it is considered a restorative, everyday food that supports the body's moisture over time.

Why Snow Fungus Is Called a Collagen-Building Food

Snow fungus contains no animal collagen, but the comparison is not accidental. When cooked, it releases large amounts of plant polysaccharides — complex carbohydrates that form a thick, viscous gel very similar in texture to gelatin. These polysaccharides are the reason a well-simmered snow fungus soup turns glossy and almost custard-like. In TCM theory, this gelatinous, fluid-nourishing quality is exactly what "nourishing yin" looks like in a bowl: it is believed to help the body retain moisture and support the skin's natural hydration. Some modern studies on Tremella polysaccharides have explored their effects on skin moisture and immune response, and while results are promising, food therapy is best understood as traditional wisdom rather than a proven medical treatment. What is certain is that a bowl of snow fungus soup is a low-calorie, hydrating way to end a meal.

How to Select and Store Snow Fungus

Good-quality dried snow fungus should look pale golden or cream colored, with tightly curled, flower-like pieces and a clean, faintly sweet scent. Avoid pieces that are dark, brittle, or have an off smell, which can indicate age or poor storage. When buying, choose whole flower heads rather than broken fragments when possible — they tend to be better quality. Store dried snow fungus in an airtight jar or resealable bag in a cool, dry place, away from strong spices and moisture. Stored properly, it keeps for many months. Note that dried snow fungus expands enormously — a single small flower can produce several servings, so a little goes a long way.

How to Prepare Snow Fungus

Preparation is simple but requires a little patience:

  1. Soak: Place the dried fungus in a bowl of cold water and soak for 2–4 hours, or overnight. It should become soft, translucent, and fully expanded.
  2. Clean: Trim off the hard, pale-yellow base with scissors, then rinse the flower well. Tear it into small bite-sized pieces — smaller pieces release more of the gel-like texture and cook faster.
  3. Simmer: Add the pieces to a pot with fresh water, bring to a gentle boil, then reduce to a low simmer. Cook for 1–2 hours, until the liquid becomes thick, glossy, and slightly jelly-like. A slow cooker works beautifully for this.
  4. Sweeten: Traditionally, snow fungus is sweetened with rock sugar or jujube dates at the end of cooking, then served warm or chilled.

For a quick version, a pressure cooker can reduce the simmering time to about 30 minutes, though many traditional cooks feel the slow method develops a silkier texture.

Classic Snow Fungus Tonic Recipes

Snow Fungus, Red Date, and Goji Soup

This is the classic Chinese dessert soup, balancing the moistening fungus with the blood-nourishing sweetness of red dates and goji berries. It pairs naturally with our snow fungus dessert recipe for a step-by-step guide.

Ingredients:

  • 1 dried snow fungus flower (about 15 g)
  • 6 red dates (jujube), pitted
  • 1 small handful of goji berries (about 10 g)
  • Rock sugar to taste (optional)
  • 1.2 liters of water

Steps:

  1. Soak the snow fungus in cold water for 2–4 hours, then trim the hard base and tear into small pieces.
  2. Place the fungus and water in a pot. Bring to a boil, then lower to a gentle simmer for 60–90 minutes.
  3. Add the red dates and simmer for another 20 minutes.
  4. Add the goji berries and rock sugar in the last 5 minutes, stirring until the sugar dissolves.
  5. Serve warm, or chill and serve as a refreshing dessert.

Snow Fungus and Pear Tonic

For autumn dryness or a dry, tickly throat, TCM food therapy often combines snow fungus with pear — another classic lung-moistening food. Slice one fresh Asian pear (skin on), and simmer it with a small soaked snow fungus flower and a few goji berries in water for about 40 minutes. Sweeten lightly with rock sugar if desired. The result is a light, cooling tonic traditionally believed to moisten the lungs and soothe a dry cough. You can also add a little honey after cooling, but not during boiling, to preserve its properties.

Who Should Be Cautious

Snow fungus is gentle, but TCM tradition notes a few cautions. Because it nourishes yin and generates fluids, people with a cold-damp constitution — those who feel cold easily, have loose stools, or a heavy, bloated feeling after eating — are traditionally advised to enjoy it in moderation rather than daily. The sweetened versions are high in sugar, so people managing blood sugar should use very little rock sugar or rely on the natural sweetness of dates. Anyone with a known mushroom allergy should avoid snow fungus entirely. As always, if you are pregnant, nursing, or managing a chronic condition, speak with a qualified healthcare provider before making snow fungus a regular part of your diet.
