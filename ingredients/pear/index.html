Pear — The Cooling Fruit That Moistens the Lungs and Throat

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 6 min read

A Fruit That Cools the Fire of Dryness

Few foods look as ordinary as a pear — and few are as quietly useful in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM). Known as Li (梨), and most prized in its pale, crisp form as Xue Li (雪梨, "snow pear"), this humble autumn fruit has been part of Chinese food therapy for well over two thousand years.

Where ginger warms and goji berries nourish, the pear does something subtler: it cools, softens, and moistens. It is the fruit TCM practitioners traditionally reach for when the body has become a little too dry and a little too warm — a scratchy throat as the seasons turn, a dry cough that will not settle, a voice worn thin by too much talking, or a thirst that plain water does not seem to touch.

This guide covers what makes pear so valued in Chinese food therapy, how it was described in the classical materia medica, and the practical preparations — steamed, stewed, and juiced — that turn a simple fruit into real food medicine.

Pear in Traditional Chinese Medicine: Properties

Every ingredient in the TCM pantry carries a specific energetic signature: a temperature, a flavour, and a set of meridian affinities that determine how it behaves in the body. Here is the traditional profile of pear:

Chinese NameLi (梨), Xue Li (雪梨)
NatureCool (raw pear borders on cold; cooking makes it markedly milder)
FlavourSweet, slightly sour
Meridians EnteredLung, Stomach, Large Intestine
Key ActionsClears Heat, Generates Fluids, Moistens Dryness, Moistens the Lungs, Soothes the Throat

The sweet flavour is said to build and moisten, while the faint sour note constrains fluids so they are not lost — a combination that makes pear particularly good at "engendering fluids," the TCM term for prompting the body to produce its own healthy moisture. Its cool nature clears low-grade heat, and its affinity for the Lung and Stomach meridians explains why it acts on the upper body: the throat, the voice, the respiratory tract, and the delicate lining of the stomach.

Key Traditional Benefits of Pear

🫁 Moistens the Lungs and Eases Dry Cough

In TCM theory, the Lungs are described as "delicate" organs that dislike both dryness and heat. When dryness takes hold, the cough that follows is distinctive: dry and hacking, with little or no phlegm, often worse at night or in heated indoor air, sometimes accompanied by a tickle at the back of the throat that simply will not go away.

Pear has traditionally been used for exactly this pattern. Its moistening, cooling nature is believed to replenish the fluids that the Lungs rely on, softening the irritation that drives the cough reflex. This is why pear shows up again and again in classical formulas for autumn dryness (秋燥) and for the later stages of a cough that has dried out the throat.

💧 Generates Fluids and Quenches Thirst

One of TCM's central insights is that health depends on adequate body fluids — the moistening matrix that keeps the skin supple, the throat comfortable, and the tissues hydrated. When fluids run low, the signs are unmistakable: a dry mouth, a parched throat, cracked lips, scanty urine, and a thirst that returns even after drinking.

Pear is famously juicy, and in TCM that juiciness is treated as a therapeutic quality rather than an accident. Fresh pear juice was traditionally recommended for thirst and dryness, including the intense thirst that follows feverish illness or accompanies wasting-thirst patterns. The physician Sun Simiao is said to have favoured pear juice for this purpose, and pear juice appears as one of the five components of the classic Five Juice Drink described in the Qing dynasty text Systematic Differentiation of Warm Diseases (温病条辨).

🔥 Clears Heat and Soothes the Throat

Sore, raw, hoarse, and tired throats are commonly read in TCM as heat or dryness affecting the throat channel. Pear's cool nature and downward-moving quality are traditionally used to clear that heat and restore ease — a reputation reflected in the famous northern Chinese preparation Autumn Pear Syrup (秋梨膏), long made for singers, teachers, and anyone whose voice carries their livelihood.

Modern readers may find the practical version familiar: warm pear tea, sipped slowly, feels soothing on an irritated throat in much the same way that TCM has described for centuries.

🌿 Moistens the Intestines and Supports Regularity

Dryness does not stop at the throat. When fluids are depleted, the Large Intestine can become dry as well, producing dry, difficult stools — a pattern especially common in older adults, in people on long courses of drying medication, and in those recovering from illness.

Pear is traditionally believed to moisten the Intestines gently, encouraging regularity without the gripping discomfort sometimes associated with stronger laxative foods. Eaten whole with the skin, it also delivers a meaningful amount of dietary fibre, which many people find supports this traditional observation.

🍶 A Traditional Support After Rich Food and Alcohol

Chinese food therapy has long paired pear with heavy meals and drinking occasions. Pear was traditionally taken after rich food or wine, where its cooling, moistening and slightly dispersing nature was believed to ease the heat and congestion that follow indulgence. It is a small, pleasant habit — a few slices of pear or a glass of pear juice to close out a long dinner.

Raw or Cooked? The Distinction That Matters Most

If there is one thing to remember about pear in TCM, it is this: raw and cooked pear behave differently. A later Chinese materia medica, the Bencao Tongxuan (本草通玄), sums it up in a single line that practitioners still quote — raw pear clears heat from the six bowels, while cooked pear nourishes the yin of the five organs.

In everyday terms, that means raw pear is best reserved for clear heat signs: a hot, dry, irritated throat, thirst, a sense of internal warmth, and a constitution that runs on the warm side. Cooked pear, by contrast, becomes gentler and more nourishing — the right choice for dryness accompanied by weak digestion, for chronic dry cough, and for anyone whose stomach turns easily at cold food.

If you enjoy raw pear but tend to feel cold or bloated afterwards, the traditional remedy is simple: cook it, or pair it with a few slices of fresh ginger to balance the coolness.

How to Choose, Store, and Prepare Pear

🛒 Choosing a Good Pear

Look for fruit that feels heavy for its size, which indicates plenty of juice. The skin should be firm and unblemished, with no soft brown patches at the stem end. Asian varieties — snow pear, nashi, and the small fragrant ya pear — are the ones most often named in Chinese food therapy, but Bosc, Anjou, and Conference pears work perfectly well for the preparations below. For cooking, slightly under-ripe fruit holds its shape better; for juicing and eating raw, choose fruit that gives slightly at the neck.

🧊 Storing Pears

Ripe pears keep for two to three days at room temperature or up to a fortnight in the crisper drawer of the refrigerator. Store them away from strong-smelling foods, since pears readily absorb neighbouring odours. Cut pear browns quickly; a squeeze of lemon juice will slow that considerably.

Four Classic Pear Preparations in Chinese Food Therapy

🍵 Steamed Pear with Chuan Bei (川贝蒸雪梨)

This is the best-known pear preparation in Chinese households and the one most often made when a dry cough lingers. Cut the top off one snow pear and hollow out the core with a spoon, leaving the walls intact. Place 3–5 grams of Chuan Bei (Fritillaria) powder and a small piece of rock sugar inside, replace the lid, and steam for 30–40 minutes until the flesh is soft and translucent. Eat the flesh and drink the liquid that collects in the hollow. Traditionally the whole pear is eaten in one sitting, once daily for three to five days. For a simpler everyday version, steam the pear with rock sugar alone. Chuan Bei is a medicinal herb, so check with a qualified practitioner if you are pregnant, nursing, or taking medication.

🥤 Five Juice Drink (五汁饮)

Recorded in the classic Systematic Differentiation of Warm Diseases, this cooling blend was traditionally used for thirst, dryness, and recovery after feverish illness. Juice equal parts fresh pear, water chestnut, lotus root, and sugarcane, then add a smaller quantity of fresh asparagus root (or substitute a little rehmannia or bamboo leaf infusion if unavailable). Combine, warm gently if desired, and sip slowly through the day. It is light, sweet, and remarkably refreshing — a traditional alternative to sports drinks when the body simply feels dried out.

🍯 Autumn Pear Syrup (秋梨膏)

Made in northern China for centuries and famously prepared for the imperial court, this thick syrup is the traditional answer to a hoarse voice and a chronically dry throat. Roughly chop two kilograms of fresh pears — skin, core and all — and simmer gently with a handful of jujube dates, three slices of ginger, and 10 grams of dried lily bulb in just enough water to cover. After 60–90 minutes, strain the pulp through muslin, pressing well, then return the liquid to the pan and reduce over low heat for another hour until it coats the back of a spoon. Stir in honey to taste and store in a sterilised jar in the refrigerator. Take one teaspoon in warm water once or twice daily.

🥣 Pear and Snow Fungus Soup (雪梨银耳羹)

Soak 15 grams of dried white fungus (snow fungus) overnight, trim any hard base, and simmer with 3 cups of water and a piece of rock sugar for 45 minutes until the fungus turns gelatinous. Add one peeled, cubed pear, a tablespoon of goji berries, and a few dried jujube dates, then simmer ten minutes more. This classic sweet soup is one of the most popular moistening desserts in Chinese home cooking, enjoyed warm or chilled.

What Modern Nutrition Says

Pears are modest-looking but nutritionally respectable, and several of their nutrients line up neatly with the traditional description:

  • Fibre — A medium pear with the skin supplies roughly 5–6 grams of fibre, much of it the soluble fibre pectin, which may support digestive comfort and healthy cholesterol levels.
  • Sorbitol and fructose — These naturally occurring sugars and sugar alcohols draw water into the intestine, a mechanism that some studies suggest may explain pear's longstanding reputation for gently encouraging regularity.
  • Vitamin C and polyphenols — Pears contain vitamin C along with quercetin and other antioxidant polyphenols, concentrated mainly in the skin.
  • Potassium and water content — With around 84% water and a useful amount of potassium, pear is a genuinely hydrating fruit — a plausible modern echo of the traditional idea of "generating fluids."

None of this "proves" the TCM framework, and it is not meant to. What it does show is that a fruit long described as cooling, moistening and soothing happens to be well suited to exactly those roles from a nutritional standpoint as well.

Who May Benefit from Pear?

Pear is a common food that most people can enjoy freely. It is traditionally considered particularly well matched to:

  • People with dryness signs: dry cough with little phlegm, dry mouth and throat, cracked lips, dry skin, hoarseness
  • Anyone in autumn or in very dry, heated indoor environments, where dryness patterns tend to arise
  • Those who run warm or experience thirst easily, especially in summer or after spicy food
  • People prone to dry, difficult stools, particularly older adults or those recovering from illness
  • Anyone looking for a naturally sweet, hydrating snack in place of sugary drinks

Cautions and Considerations

As with any food in Chinese food therapy, a little context goes a long way. A few points worth remembering:

  • Cold and weak digestion — Raw pear is cool, and eaten in quantity it may sit heavily in people with cold, damp or deficient patterns: cold hands and feet, loose stools, bloating, a sensation of heaviness, or an easily upset stomach. Cook the pear, or pair it with ginger or a pinch of cinnamon, and keep raw pear to small portions.
  • Diarrhoea — Because pear has a mild moistening and laxative tendency, it is traditionally avoided during loose stools or active diarrhoea.
  • Blood sugar — Pears contain natural sugars. If you monitor your blood sugar, account for pear within your usual carbohydrate planning.
  • Traditional food pairings — Chinese food therapy traditionally cautions against eating large amounts of pear together with crab, on the reasoning that both are cold-natured. There is no need for alarm, but the pairing is worth avoiding in quantity.
  • Seeds — Pear seeds contain amygdalin, as do apple and apricot seeds. Do not chew or eat the seeds in quantity; ordinary amounts of fruit flesh are not a concern.
  • Pregnancy, nursing, and medication — Pear as an ordinary food is fine. Therapeutic preparations containing herbs such as Chuan Bei or rehmannia should only be used under the guidance of a qualified practitioner during pregnancy, nursing, or alongside prescription medication.

Final Thoughts

Pear is one of the quiet heroes of Chinese food therapy — inexpensive, widely available, and easy to use, yet remarkably well matched to the dryness and low-grade heat of modern life. Heated offices, long hours of talking, late nights, travel, and spicy restaurant meals all nudge the body in the same direction, and a simple pear meets it with cooling moisture.

Start where it is easiest. Steam a pear with a piece of rock sugar on a dry autumn evening. Stew one with snow fungus when your throat feels raw. Or simply slice one, skin on, and enjoy it after a heavy dinner. Ancient wisdom rarely asks for more than that.
