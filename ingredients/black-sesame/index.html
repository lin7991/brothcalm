Black Sesame (Hei Zhi Ma) — The Kidney and Hair Nourishing Seed

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 7 min read

A Tiny Black Seed with a Big Reputation

Walk into any traditional Chinese apothecary and you will find jars of seeds, roots, barks and dried fruits — but few of them are as quietly beloved as Hei Zhi Ma (黑芝麻), the black sesame seed.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), black sesame is not treated as a garnish or a baking ingredient. It is a food-therapy staple: a gentle, nourishing seed traditionally used to support the Kidneys, brighten the hair, lubricate a dry system, and rebuild strength in someone who has been running on empty for too long.

Its reputation is old. Black sesame appears in the classic materia medica of Chinese herbal medicine, where it was praised as a food that could be eaten daily and over long periods — a rare quality in a tradition that mostly reserved its strongest herbs for short courses. That everyday-ness is exactly what makes it so useful in a modern kitchen.

What Is Hei Zhi Ma? The TCM Properties

Every ingredient in TCM carries an energetic profile — a temperature, a flavour, and a set of meridians it is said to enter. That profile explains why black sesame is used the way it is.

Chinese NameHei Zhi Ma (黑芝麻)
Also Known AsBlack sesame seed, Hu Ma (胡麻)
NatureNeutral (slightly warm in many traditional preparations)
FlavourSweet
Meridians EnteredLiver, Kidney, Large Intestine
Key ActionsTonifies Liver and Kidney Essence, Nourishes Blood, Moistens the Intestines, Supports the Hair and Bones

The sweet flavour is important. In TCM, sweet is the flavour of nourishment — it builds and tonifies rather than disperses or clears. The neutral-to-warm temperature means black sesame is not harsh or cooling, so it can be eaten through the colder months and by people with a tendency toward cold. And the affinity with the Liver, Kidney and Large Intestine tells you almost everything about its traditional uses: hair and eyes, bones and lower back, and dry, difficult bowel movements.

Key Traditional Benefits of Black Sesame

🌰 Tonifies Liver and Kidney Essence (Jing)

In TCM theory, the Kidneys store Essence — the deep reserve of vitality that governs growth, reproduction, bone strength and ageing. The Liver stores Blood, and together the Liver and Kidney are considered the root of the body's yin and its structural substance.

Black sesame is traditionally classified as a seed that tonifies both. This is why it shows up so often in food-therapy guidance for people who feel depleted, who tire easily, whose lower back and knees ache, or who are moving through a life stage where their reserves naturally run lower — recovery from illness, long periods of overwork, or the later decades of life. It is not a stimulating tonic. It rebuilds slowly, which is exactly the point.

💇 Traditionally Associated with Healthy Hair and Delayed Greying

Ask anyone raised with Chinese food wisdom what black sesame is good for, and the answer will come immediately: hair.

The logic is elegant. TCM holds that the Kidneys' condition is reflected in the hair, and that Liver Blood nourishes it. When Kidney Essence and Liver Blood are abundant, hair is traditionally described as thick, glossy and slow to grey. When they are depleted, hair may thin, dry out, or turn grey early.

Because black sesame is believed to nourish both systems, it has been a cornerstone of traditional hair-support eating for centuries. The colour matters too — in TCM's correspondences, black is the colour of the Kidneys and of Water, so dark-coloured foods are traditionally considered to reach the Kidney system. This is why black sesame, black beans, walnuts and mulberry are so often combined in the same formula.

💧 Moistens the Intestines and Eases Dryness

Black sesame contains a great deal of oil, and TCM recognises this. Its moistening quality is traditionally used for what practitioners call dry constipation — the kind where stools are hard and difficult to pass, often alongside a dry throat, dry skin, or a generally dry constitution.

This is especially common in older adults, in people who have recently recovered from a feverish illness, and in anyone living in a dry climate or spending long hours in air-conditioning. A daily spoonful of toasted, ground black sesame stirred into warm congee or milk is a gentle, food-based approach to a very common complaint.

🦴 Supports Bones and Joints

TCM ties bone health to Kidney Essence, and black sesame is one of the traditional foods used to support it. From a modern nutrition standpoint this is one of the more tangible claims: sesame seeds are notably rich in calcium, magnesium, phosphorus and copper — all minerals involved in maintaining bone density. Grinding the seeds matters here, because whole sesame seeds can pass through the digestive tract largely intact.

🩸 Nourishes Blood

As a sweet, nourishing seed, black sesame is also used in food therapy for signs of Blood and yin deficiency — a pale complexion, dry eyes, brittle nails, dizziness, and a general feeling of thinness or depletion. It is rarely used alone; it is paired with ingredients such as goji berries, jujube dates or Chinese yam to build a more complete tonic.

How to Use Black Sesame in Daily Life

Black sesame is one of the easiest tonic foods to work with, because it tastes good and behaves like any ordinary pantry ingredient. Two preparation details matter more than anything else.

Step 1: Toast It

Place the seeds in a dry pan over low to medium heat. Stir constantly for two to three minutes, until the seeds smell warm and nutty and you begin to hear the faint popping of them jumping. Stop the moment they start to pop in earnest — sesame burns quickly. Traditional practitioners believe toasting makes the seed warmer, more fragrant and easier for the Spleen to digest.

Step 2: Grind It (or at least crush it)

Whole sesame seeds are famously hard to digest. Grinding them into a coarse powder, or crushing them lightly, exposes the oils and minerals inside, so the body can actually use them. A small electric grinder, a mortar and pestle, or even the blunt end of a knife and some patience will do the job. Store the powder in a sealed jar in the fridge and use it within two to three weeks.

🥣 Black Sesame Congee

Add two tablespoons of toasted, ground black sesame to a pot of rice congee in the final five minutes of cooking. Sweeten lightly with a jujube date or a little honey. This is a classic tonic breakfast — warming, gentle on the digestion, and easy to eat daily. See our full Black Sesame Porridge recipe.

🥛 Black Sesame and Walnut Drink

Blend two tablespoons of toasted black sesame powder with one tablespoon of ground walnuts and a cup of warm milk or unsweetened soy milk. This pairing is one of the most traditional combinations in Chinese food therapy, used together for hair, bones and Kidney support. Drink it warm in the morning or early evening.

🫙 Black Sesame Paste

Black sesame paste — made by grinding toasted seeds with a little honey or rock sugar and a neutral oil — is a traditional sweet spread. One teaspoon a day stirred into warm water makes a simple daily tonic, and the paste can also be spread on toast or used as a filling for sweet buns and glutinous rice balls.

🍵 Blended Sesame Tonic Powder

Many Chinese households keep a jar of a blended tonic powder: black sesame, walnuts, black beans and sometimes mulberry, all toasted and ground together. One tablespoon in hot water each morning is a very common daily habit, and a practical way to make the whole combination a routine rather than an occasional treat.

Buying and Storing

Black sesame is inexpensive and widely available — look for it in Asian groceries, health food shops and well-stocked supermarkets.

  • Look for small, uniform, glossy black seeds with no dusty residue. Fresh seeds smell nutty and sweet.
  • Avoid seeds that smell bitter, musty or like old oil. Sesame is high in fat and goes rancid over time.
  • Store whole seeds in an airtight jar in a cool, dark cupboard for up to six months. Once toasted or ground, refrigerate the powder and use within two to three weeks.
  • Check labels on ready-made pastes and powders for added sugar, which can be very high.

What Modern Research Says

Black sesame has been studied far less than it has been eaten, but the existing research is interesting:

  • Calcium and minerals — Sesame seeds are among the richest plant sources of calcium, along with magnesium, copper, zinc and iron. This gives real substance to the traditional association with bones and Blood.
  • Healthy fats — Roughly half the seed is oil, predominantly unsaturated, including omega-6 fatty acids and a small amount of omega-3.
  • Lignans and antioxidants — Sesame contains sesamin and sesamol, lignans that some studies suggest may support antioxidant defences and healthy lipid levels.
  • Vitamin E and B vitamins — Present in meaningful amounts, supporting the seed's reputation as a nourishing, skin-friendly food.

As always, this does not "prove" the TCM framework — the two systems describe the body in different languages. But it does suggest that the traditional regard for black sesame as a nutrient-dense, deeply nourishing food rests on a solid foundation.

Who May Benefit Most

Black sesame is a food, and it is suitable for most healthy adults. It is traditionally recommended for:

  • People with dry constitutions — dry skin, dry hair, dry eyes, dry stools
  • Those showing signs of Liver and Kidney depletion: fatigue, aching lower back and knees, dizziness, early greying
  • Older adults looking for gentle, long-term support for bones and bowel regularity
  • Anyone recovering from illness or a long stretch of overwork and poor sleep
  • People wanting a calcium-rich plant food that is easy to add to breakfast

Cautions and Considerations

Black sesame is a gentle food, but a few points are worth keeping in mind:

  • Spleen weakness with loose stools — Because black sesame is oily and moistening, those with weak digestion and frequent loose stools should use it in moderation. Pairing it with a warming ingredient such as ginger or a little cinnamon may help balance its nature.
  • Sesame allergy — Sesame is a recognised allergen. Anyone with a sesame allergy should avoid it entirely, including in pastes, oils and baked goods.
  • Calorie density — These are energy-dense seeds. A tablespoon or two a day is a food-therapy portion; a whole jar is not. This matters for anyone managing their weight.
  • Digestive discomfort — Eating large amounts of untoasted, unground seeds is a common cause of bloating and can make stools loose. Toast and grind.
  • Pregnancy and medication — Black sesame is a common food and generally considered safe in normal dietary amounts. Anyone using concentrated powders or pastes therapeutically during pregnancy, nursing, or alongside medication should check with a qualified healthcare provider first.

Final Thoughts

Black sesame is one of the quiet heroes of the Chinese food-therapy pantry. It does not promise anything dramatic. It simply nourishes — gently, consistently, and in a way that fits naturally into a bowl of congee or a warm morning drink.

Toast a batch this week, grind it, and keep it in the fridge. One spoonful a day, taken patiently, is exactly how this tradition has always worked: not as a cure, but as a habit of eating well for a very long time.
