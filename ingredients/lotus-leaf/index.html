Lotus Leaf (He Ye): Cooling Dampness and Clearing Summer Heat

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 6 min read

Few images capture the Chinese summer quite like a pond of lotus in full bloom. The broad, waxy leaves rise above the water on slender stalks, holding morning dew in perfect beads, while pink and white flowers open in the heat of the day. The plant is admired for its beauty, its roots and seeds are eaten at the family table — and its enormous green leaves, known as hé yè (荷叶), have been folded into Chinese kitchens and apothecaries for well over a thousand years.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), lotus leaf occupies a distinctive place among summer ingredients. It is not a rich tonic like ginseng or goji berries, and it is not a sharp medicinal herb. Instead, it is a cooling, clearing, drying food — traditionally used when the weather turns hot and humid, and the body feels heavy, sluggish, and overheated. In the language of TCM, lotus leaf clears summer heat and transforms dampness.

For anyone living through a muggy summer, or anyone who recognizes that familiar end-of-August feeling of bloating, lethargy, and sticky discomfort, lotus leaf is one of the most practical ingredients in the entire food therapy repertoire. Let's look at what it is, how it works in TCM theory, and how to actually use it in the kitchen.

What Is Lotus Leaf?

Lotus leaf is the large, umbrella-shaped leaf of Nelumbo nucifera, the sacred lotus — an aquatic perennial native to Asia and widely cultivated across China, India, and Southeast Asia. The leaf can grow more than 60 centimeters across, and its surface is famously self-cleaning: water rolls off in beads, carrying dust with it. This water-repellent quality is the same characteristic that makes the leaf a superb wrapping material for steaming foods.

The leaf is harvested in late summer and early autumn, when it is fully formed but still tender enough to be useful. It is most commonly used dried, folded into packages and sold in Chinese herbal shops and Asian groceries. You will also find it fresh in season at markets in southern China, where it is sold by the bundle or by the single enormous leaf. In TCM texts, the dried leaf is called hé yè (荷叶); its stem, the leaf stalk, is a separate substance known as hé gěng (荷梗), and the seed pod receptacle is lián fáng (莲房). This article focuses on the leaf.

Lotus is one of the oldest cultivated plants in human history, with evidence of use stretching back thousands of years. Every part of it appears in the Chinese materia medica: the root (lotus root), the seed (lotus seed), the plumule inside the seed, the flower, the stamen, the receptacle, and the leaf. Few plants are so completely woven into a single culinary and medical tradition.

Lotus Leaf in TCM: Nature, Flavor, and Meridians

As with any ingredient in food therapy, we begin with the three-part profile that TCM uses to describe how a food behaves in the body: thermal nature, flavor, and the organ meridians it enters.

  • Nature: Cool, sometimes described as cool to cold (凉 / liáng)
  • Flavor: Bitter and sweet, with a slight astringency (苦甘 / kǔ gān)
  • Meridians entered: Heart (心), Liver (肝), Spleen (脾), and Stomach (胃)
  • Primary TCM actions: Clears summer heat (清暑 / qīng shǔ), transforms dampness (化湿 / huà shī), raises clear yang, and cools the blood

The cool nature of lotus leaf is the key to understanding its traditional role. In TCM, summer heat is not just a temperature — it is a pattern that can disturb the body from the inside, producing thirst, irritability, restlessness, a red face, and a feeling of being overheated from within. A cool, slightly bitter leaf is the classic antidote in food therapy: it disperses that accumulated heat without being harsh.

The bitter flavor carries a specific meaning in TCM. Bitter substances are said to "dry" and to "descend" — they drain dampness downward and out, and they reduce the cloying, heavy quality that dampness imposes on the body. Because lotus leaf is bitter and light rather than cloying, TCM holds that it can dry dampness without injuring the Spleen, an unusual and valuable combination.

Its sweetness tempers the bitterness, making it pleasant to drink and gentle on the digestive system. And its slight astringency gives rise to another traditional use: stopping abnormal bleeding, particularly blood in the stool or nosebleeds associated with heat.

The meridian affinity tells us where the leaf's influence is traditionally thought to concentrate. Entering the Heart, it is believed to help calm restlessness and clear heat that disturbs the spirit. Entering the Liver, it supports the smooth flow of qi and helps clear damp-heat. Entering the Spleen and Stomach, it addresses the very source of dampness — the digestive system that TCM holds responsible for transforming food and fluids.

Traditional Uses of Lotus Leaf

Clearing Summer Heat (Qing Shu)

The most classic use of lotus leaf is for what TCM calls summerheat (暑 / shǔ) — the cluster of symptoms that appear during hot, humid weather: thirst, a heavy head, irritability, restlessness, low appetite, and a sensation of heat that no amount of shade seems to relieve. Lotus leaf is traditionally brewed as a light tea in the hottest weeks of the year, often alongside mung bean or chrysanthemum, to gently disperse that heat.

In Chinese folk practice, a pot of lotus leaf tea is a summer kitchen staple — something a grandmother makes in the afternoon and leaves on the table for the family to drink cool. It is not considered medicine so much as a seasonal adjustment, a way of eating and drinking in harmony with the weather.

Transforming Dampness (Hua Shi)

Where summer heat is dry and burning, dampness is heavy and sticky. In TCM, dampness describes a pattern of sluggishness and accumulated fluid: a sensation of heaviness in the limbs, a foggy head, bloating after meals, loose or sticky stools, a coated tongue, and a feeling of being "waterlogged." Dampness is especially common in humid climates, in late summer, and in people whose digestion is already weak.

Lotus leaf's bitter, light nature is traditionally believed to reach into this stagnation and help move it. Because it enters the Spleen, it works on the organ TCM holds responsible for fluid transformation — the idea being that if the Spleen's transforming function is supported, dampness stops accumulating in the first place. For this reason you will find lotus leaf in the classic pairing with job's tears (薏苡仁 / yì yǐ rén) and barley, two other grains traditionally used to drain dampness.

Supporting Fluid Balance and a Feeling of Lightness

TCM links damp accumulation with a sensation of puffiness and water retention, and lotus leaf is traditionally described as mildly diuretic — supporting the natural elimination of excess fluid. This is the root of its long association with a feeling of lightness in the body, and with the modern interest in lotus leaf as a supportive ingredient for weight management.

It is worth being precise here. Lotus leaf is not a slimming remedy, and it is not traditionally used that way. What TCM describes is a pattern: when dampness clears, the body feels lighter and less congested. Some modern laboratory studies have suggested that compounds in lotus leaf may influence fat metabolism, but this research is preliminary and should not be read as a promise. Lotus leaf is best understood as a gentle, seasonal support for the body's own fluid balance — see our summer food therapy guide for how it fits into a wider seasonal approach.

Calming Restlessness and Supporting the Spirit

Because lotus leaf enters the Heart meridian, it is traditionally included in summer formulas for restlessness, irritability, and disturbed sleep during hot weather. In TCM, heat has a natural tendency to rise and agitate the shén (神), the spirit or consciousness housed in the Heart. A cooling, calming leaf helps settle that agitation. It is a familiar pairing principle: lotus leaf for the heat, and a soothing ingredient such as lotus root or lotus leaf tea blended with jujube for the spirit.

Cooling the Blood and Stopping Bleeding

A less common but well-documented traditional use is for bleeding patterns attributed to heat — nosebleeds, blood in the stool, or heavy menstrual bleeding. Lotus leaf's astringent quality is believed to help "hold" blood in place while its cool nature reduces the heat driving it out. This is a medicinal application rather than a kitchen one, and it belongs in the hands of a qualified practitioner.

How to Choose, Store, and Prepare Lotus Leaf

Buying

Dried lotus leaf is sold in Asian groceries and Chinese herbal shops, usually folded into a flat package. Look for leaves with an even olive-green to brownish-green color, a clean grassy aroma, and no signs of mold or mustiness. Very pale, brittle leaves have usually lost their aroma; very dark or damp leaves may have been stored poorly. Whole leaves are preferable to pre-crushed pieces, as you can see exactly what you are getting. If you buy fresh leaves in season, choose ones that are firm, evenly colored, and free of brown spots.

Storing

Keep dried lotus leaf in an airtight container away from light and moisture. A glass jar in a cool cupboard is ideal. Properly stored, it keeps its aroma for about a year; after that it is not harmful, simply weaker. If your leaf came in a plastic bag, transfer it — plastic traps moisture and encourages mold in humid climates. Fresh leaves should be used within a few days, or blanched briefly and frozen.

Preparing

Dried lotus leaf should be rinsed under cool running water to remove dust, then soaked in warm water for about 10 to 15 minutes until pliable. The soaking water can be discarded or, if the leaf is clean and fragrant, reserved for the tea. For cooking, trim away the tough central vein and any woody edges with scissors before use.

Fresh leaves need a quick blanch — about 30 seconds in boiling water — to soften them and remove any bitterness before wrapping food. Pat them dry before use, since excess water will steam rather than flavor your dish.

Lotus Leaf Tea: A Simple Everyday Brew

The easiest way to use lotus leaf is as a light tea. This is the version most often drunk in Chinese homes during the hot months.

Ingredients

  • 3–6 grams dried lotus leaf (roughly one-quarter of a large leaf), torn into pieces
  • 500 ml (about 2 cups) water
  • Optional: 5–6 dried chrysanthemum flowers, or a small piece of dried tangerine peel
  • Optional: a few rock sugar crystals or a teaspoon of honey, to taste

Method

  1. Rinse the lotus leaf briefly under cool water.
  2. Place the leaf in a teapot or small saucepan and add the water.
  3. Bring to a gentle boil, then reduce the heat and simmer, covered, for 5 to 8 minutes.
  4. Turn off the heat and let it steep for a further 5 minutes. The longer you steep, the more bitter it becomes — start short and adjust.
  5. Strain and drink warm, or chill for a refreshing summer drink over ice.

A single batch can be re-steeped once with fresh hot water, though the second infusion will be milder. Drink it in the afternoon, when summer heat tends to peak.

Variations

  • Lotus leaf and chrysanthemum — add dried chrysanthemum flowers for a more cooling brew traditionally used when the eyes feel hot and tired.
  • Lotus leaf and job's tears — simmer 15 grams of job's tears (yì yǐ rén) with the leaf for a damp-draining summer drink. This is our most recommended pairing for humid weather.
  • Lotus leaf and tangerine peel — a small piece of chen pi (dried tangerine peel) softens the bitterness and is traditionally believed to protect the Stomach while dampness is being resolved.
  • Lotus leaf and hawthorn — a traditional combination after heavy, greasy meals, when the stomach feels full and sluggish.

Lotus Leaf in the Kitchen

Beyond tea, lotus leaf is a genuine cooking ingredient — and one of the most elegant in the Chinese repertoire. Its fragrance is subtle, grassy, and faintly sweet, and it perfumes whatever it wraps.

Lotus Leaf Wrapped Rice (He Ye Fan)

The most famous preparation is glutinous rice steamed inside a lotus leaf. The leaf becomes a fragrant wrapper that steams the rice gently, imparting a green, tea-like aroma while keeping the grains moist. To make it at home: soak a dried leaf until pliable, blanch it briefly, line a steamer basket with it, add seasoned glutinous rice plus optional fillings such as shiitake mushroom, chestnut, or diced chicken, then fold the leaf over the top and steam for 40 to 50 minutes. The leaf is not eaten — it is the vessel and the seasoning.

Lotus Leaf Congee

A gentler, everyday option: simmer a strip of dried lotus leaf in water for 10 minutes, remove it, and cook rice in that infused water to make a light congee. The result is a subtly green, faintly bitter porridge traditionally eaten in hot weather when appetite is poor. Pair it with a small amount of Chinese yam if your digestion tends to be weak. See our congee recipe guide for the base technique.

Steaming and Wrapping

Lotus leaf can wrap almost anything: fish, chicken, sticky rice, or vegetables. The technique is always the same — soften the leaf, blanch, pat dry, wrap the filling, and steam. The leaf traps steam and aroma, so the food inside stays moist and takes on a delicate grassy perfume. It also makes an attractive serving vessel, which is why the dish appears so often at Chinese banquets.

Practical Guidance: How Much, and When

Lotus leaf is a light substance, so quantities in food therapy are modest.

  • Tea: 3–6 grams of dried leaf per 500 ml of water, once daily.
  • Damp-draining brews: 3–6 grams of leaf combined with 15–30 grams of job's tears or barley.
  • Congee and cooking: one strip or a quarter leaf to infuse a pot for a family.
  • Best season: late spring through late summer, and especially during humid weather.
  • Best time of day: afternoon, or after a heavy meal.
  • Duration: traditionally used in short seasonal stretches — a few days to two weeks — rather than indefinitely.

If you are new to lotus leaf, begin with a mild infusion — a small amount of leaf, a short steep — and notice how your body responds. Food therapy is always a conversation with your own constitution, not a fixed prescription.

Cautions and Who Should Be Careful

Lotus leaf is generally regarded as a safe food when used in culinary amounts, but it is a cooling, drying, mildly diuretic substance, and that matters for certain people.

  • Cold or deficient constitution. If you frequently feel chilly, have cold hands and feet, or tend toward loose stools and low energy, a cooling, drying leaf may aggravate that pattern. TCM traditionally advises caution here.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding. Lotus leaf has traditionally been used to move blood and is not considered suitable during pregnancy. Avoid it, and consult your healthcare provider.
  • Low blood pressure. Because it is mildly diuretic, people with low blood pressure are traditionally advised to be cautious.
  • Medication interactions. If you take diuretics, blood-pressure medication, or diabetes medication, speak to your doctor before regular use, as the combined effect on fluid balance may be significant.
  • Not for prolonged heavy use. Long-term, high-dose use of a drying substance may deplete fluids in TCM theory. Keep it seasonal and moderate.
  • Children and the elderly. Use smaller amounts, and only occasionally, as their constitutions are more easily influenced.

Stop using lotus leaf if you notice unusual fatigue, dizziness, or digestive discomfort, and consult a qualified healthcare provider if symptoms persist.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is lotus leaf used for in TCM?

Lotus leaf (He Ye) is traditionally used to clear summer heat, transform dampness, and support fluid balance. It is most often taken as a tea during hot, humid weather, and is believed to ease the heaviness, sluggishness, and poor appetite that TCM associates with damp-heat patterns.

Can I drink lotus leaf tea every day?

Traditionally it is a seasonal brew rather than a year-round daily tonic. It is usually drunk during hot or humid weather, or for short stretches of one to two weeks, using about 3 to 6 grams of dried leaf per day. Because it is cooling and mildly diuretic, long-term daily use is not traditionally recommended for people with a cold or deficient constitution.

Who should avoid lotus leaf?

People with a cold or deficient constitution, those with low blood pressure, and anyone taking diuretic or blood-pressure medication without medical guidance should be cautious. Pregnant and breastfeeding women are advised to avoid it.

Does lotus leaf help with weight management?

In TCM theory, lotus leaf is believed to resolve dampness, and damp accumulation is one pattern traditionally linked with heaviness and water retention. Some preliminary studies suggest compounds in lotus leaf may influence fat metabolism, but the evidence is not conclusive. Treat it as a supportive seasonal food, not a weight-loss remedy.

The Takeaway

Lotus leaf is one of the quiet heroes of Chinese food therapy: unglamorous, inexpensive, and easy to overlook next to more famous tonics. Yet for a hot, humid summer — or for anyone who recognizes the heavy, bloated, sluggish feeling that dampness brings — it is a remarkably practical ingredient. A simple infusion in the afternoon, a wrap of glutinous rice, a pot of congee cooked in lotus-infused water: these are small, pleasurable ways of eating in step with the season.

Used moderately, and with attention to your own constitution, lotus leaf is a beautiful example of what food therapy is really about — not chasing remedies, but adjusting the way we eat as the weather changes around us.
