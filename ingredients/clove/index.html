Cloves (Ding Xiang): The Aromatic Bud That Warms the Stomach

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 6 min read

Push a single dried clove into an orange at Christmastime, or bite into one by accident in a braised dish, and you feel the same thing: a sudden, sweet-sharp wave of warmth that seems to travel all the way down to your stomach. Few spices announce themselves so completely. In China, that unmistakable heat is not just a flavor note — it is the reason cloves, known as ding xiang (丁香), have held a place in Chinese food therapy for well over a thousand years.

This guide looks at cloves through the lens of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM): what they are, their warming nature and where they act in the body, the traditional uses that made them a household remedy for nausea and cold, and how to bring them into your cooking and tea practice in safe, practical amounts.

What Are Cloves?

A clove is the dried, unopened flower bud of the Syzygium aromaticum tree, an evergreen native to the Moluccas — the "Spice Islands" of Indonesia. Each bud looks like a tiny nail, which is exactly what its Chinese name evokes and what the Latin word clavus ("nail") gave the English word. The bud is harvested before it opens, then sun-dried until it turns the deep, oily brown-black that signals good quality.

Cloves reached China along the maritime spice routes, and their intense fragrance made them valuable far beyond the kitchen. Three-quarters of a clove's aroma comes from a single compound, eugenol — a warm, almost numbing aromatic that is also mildly analgesic. In TCM, that numbing warmth is not a curiosity; it is the signature of the herb's action.

In Chinese kitchens, cloves rarely stand alone. They appear in small numbers in braising spice blends, warming broths, and medicinal soups, where they are treated much like a medicine measured in pinches rather than a seasoning poured by the spoonful. That instinct — use a little — is itself good TCM practice.

TCM Properties of Ding Xiang

In the framework of Chinese food therapy, every ingredient carries a thermal nature, a flavor, and a set of organ channels it influences. Cloves are among the most warming and most concentrated items in the spice cabinet:

  • Nature (性): Warm — some classical texts describe it as hot
  • Flavor (味): Pungent (acrid)
  • Channels (归经): Spleen, Stomach, and Kidney
  • Direction (趋向): Descending — traditionally said to direct rebellious qi downward

Pungent flavors are understood to move and disperse, while a warm nature adds heat and drives out cold. When you combine the two in a spice as concentrated as clove, you get an ingredient that acts on the middle burner — the stomach and spleen — with unusual force. Classical food therapy describes ding xiang as warming the middle, descending rebellious qi, and supporting kidney yang.

That phrase, "descending rebellious qi," is worth unpacking. In TCM, nausea and vomiting are often described as qi moving the wrong way — upwards, when it should settle downwards. Cloves are traditionally understood to help move that qi back in the right direction, which is why they appear so often in traditional formulas for an unsettled stomach.

Traditional Uses in Chinese Medicine

Cloves appear in classical Chinese materia medica texts, where they are consistently described as warming, aromatic, and descending — a combination that suits the digestive system. Over the centuries, they have been traditionally used in the following ways.

Warming the Stomach and Easing Cold Pain

A stomach that feels cold is an easy pattern to recognize: a dull, cramping ache that eases when you press a warm hand to your belly, a dislike of cold drinks, a poor appetite in the morning, and loose stools. Warming aromatics like cloves, ginger, and cinnamon are the traditional response to this pattern. A few cloves simmered in a light broth or congee is a gentle, familiar way to bring warmth back to the middle.

Easing Nausea and Vomiting

Cloves are probably best known in Western households — rightly — as a remedy for queasiness. In TCM terms, their descending quality and their warmth are believed to help settle a cold, churning stomach, whether the discomfort comes from rich food, travel, or simply feeling chilled. Traditional practice favors a small cup of clove infusion sipped slowly rather than gulped.

Relieving Cold-Type Abdominal Discomfort

For cold-type digestive cramping with gas and a heavy, uncomfortable feeling after eating cold or raw food, cloves are traditionally combined with warming aromatics such as dried ginger, fennel, and a touch of cinnamon. The aroma itself is part of the therapy — in Chinese food therapy, the pungent fragrance of a warming spice is understood to lift sluggish digestion before you have even swallowed.

Supporting Yang and Warming the Kidneys

Because cloves are said to enter the Kidney channel, they are traditionally used in small amounts in warming tonic soups, especially in winter. This is not the place for heavy spicing — a clove or two alongside black beans, walnuts, or lamb is the traditional style. They are also included in some traditional warming blends intended to support the lower back and knees during cold months.

Easing Toothache (Traditional Topical Use)

One of the oldest folk uses of cloves is purely practical: a single clove held against an aching tooth. Eugenol is a genuine mild local anaesthetic, and this is one of the few traditional uses with a clear modern explanation. It is a stopgap for discomfort, not a substitute for dental care — if a tooth hurts, see a dentist.

Freshening Breath

Chewing a clove to sweeten the breath is an ancient habit across Asia and Europe alike, and it doubles as a pleasant way to carry a hint of the spice's warmth into the day. In TCM the aromatic quality is understood to "open" the mouth and stomach and clear a stale, heavy feeling.

Cloves in the Kitchen

Clove is one of the most potent spices in the pantry, measured in pieces, not spoonfuls. Here are the most useful ways to use it well.

Whole or Ground?

Whole cloves hold their aroma far longer and are easier to control — you can count them and fish them out before serving. Ground clove, on the other hand, releases its heat instantly and can easily overwhelm a dish. For teas, broths, and braises, buy whole and use them as they are. Reserve ground clove for baking and spice blends, where a light hand is essential.

A Simple Clove Tea

This is the most approachable way to bring cloves into a daily routine.

  • You will need: 2–3 whole cloves, 1 slice of fresh ginger, and a 2 cm piece of cinnamon bark (optional but traditional)
  • Method: Place everything in a cup or small pot and pour over 250 ml of just-boiled water. Cover and steep for 5–8 minutes.
  • When to drink: Sip slowly after a heavy meal, on a cold morning, or when you feel chilled and unsettled. One small cup is usually enough.
  • Variation: Simmer the same ingredients gently for 10 minutes with a slice of orange peel for a deeper, sweeter infusion.

In Broths, Braises, and Congee

Add 2–4 whole cloves to a pot of bone broth, chicken soup, or braised beef serving four people, along with ginger, star anise, and cinnamon. Cloves disappear into the background in small numbers and give a slow, deep warmth. For a soothing rice congee, add 2 cloves and a slice of ginger while the rice cooks, then remove the cloves before eating.

Classic Pairings

  • Ginger: the most natural partner — both warm the middle burner and settle the stomach
  • Cinnamon bark: together they form the backbone of Chinese warming spice blends
  • Fennel and star anise: the aromatic trio that appears in braising mixtures
  • Orange peel (chen pi): adds a gentle citrus note and supports digestion in TCM

Buying and Storing Cloves

Choose whole cloves that are plump, oily, and heavy for their size, with a strong, sweet-sharp scent when you crush one. Good cloves should be dark brown with an intact rounded head; avoid pale, dry, or brittle buds, which have lost most of their volatile oil. You can test quality by pressing a clove with your fingernail — a little oil should appear.

Store cloves in a small airtight glass jar away from light, heat, and moisture. Whole cloves keep their potency for one to two years; ground clove fades within about six months. Do not keep them near the stove, where heat will quietly strip the aroma.

Why Clove Adds So Much to a Warming Diet

Chinese food therapy rarely treats any ingredient as a solo act. Clove earns its place by working in the smallest possible amount — a pinch that shifts the whole character of a dish from neutral to warming. In the traditional view, this reflects a broader principle: some foods are nourishing in quantity, while others act through their aroma and their direction of movement. Clove belongs firmly to the second group.

For anyone exploring a warming, digestion-friendly way of eating — the same approach behind our winter warming foods guide and our herbal chicken soup — a jar of good whole cloves is one of the most useful small investments you can make.

Who Should Be Careful?

Cloves are warming and drying, which makes them a poor fit for people who already run hot. In TCM, signs of excess heat include:

  • A red face, a dry mouth, and constant thirst
  • A yellow or thick tongue coating
  • Acne, mouth ulcers, or a feeling of heat in the chest
  • Constipation with hard, dry stools

People with stomach ulcers, acid reflux, or a sensitive digestive system may find the pungent spice irritating and should use it sparingly. Pregnant women are traditionally advised to keep clove to ordinary culinary amounts, not concentrated tea or supplement doses. Clove may interact with blood-thinning medication and can affect blood sugar, so anyone taking such medication or managing diabetes should speak with a healthcare provider before using it regularly. Essential clove oil is extremely concentrated and should never be taken undiluted.

As with all food therapy, the guiding principle is moderation and attention. A clove or two in a pot of soup is food. A large daily dose of concentrated extract is not.
