Chrysanthemum (菊花) — The Cooling Flower

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 6 min read

Introduction

Few flowers are as woven into daily Chinese life as the chrysanthemum. In the late summer and autumn, golden and white blossoms appear in markets, dried and ready for the teapot. For thousands of years, jú huā (菊花) has been more than an ornament — it is one of the most beloved cooling herbs in Chinese food therapy, prized for its gentle action on the eyes, the head, and the spirit.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), chrysanthemum belongs to the group of "cooling and clearing" ingredients. It is traditionally used when heat rises, whether that shows up as tired, red eyes after a long day of screens, a stuffy head, or a short temper. Modern wellness culture has embraced it too — chrysanthemum tea is now a common sight in health-food aisles — but the wisdom behind the flower is genuinely ancient. This guide explores its TCM properties, traditional uses, and how to make it part of your everyday routine.

TCM Properties of Chrysanthemum

Nature and Taste

In TCM theory, chrysanthemum has a cool (寒凉) nature with a sweet and slightly bitter taste. Coolness gives it the ability to clear heat, while the bitter note helps it descend and direct energy downward — exactly what is needed when heat rises to the head and eyes.

Meridian Affinity

Chrysanthemum is said to enter the Lung and Liver meridians. The Lung meridian connection supports its use for wind-heat conditions like a scratchy throat or mild feverish feeling, while the Liver meridian connection explains its traditional reputation for soothing the eyes and calming irritability — the Liver is the organ associated with smooth flow of qi and with the eyes in TCM.

Traditional Uses in TCM

Clearing Wind-Heat and Cooling the Head

One of the oldest roles of chrysanthemum in TCM is to disperse wind-heat. In classical formulas, it appears alongside cooling partners for early-stage colds where the heat is more prominent than the chill — think of a slightly feverish face, a dry throat, and a headache that feels worse in the wind. The flower's light, ascending quality helps it reach the head, where it cools and soothes.

Soothing Liver Fire and Supporting the Eyes

Perhaps chrysanthemum's most famous use is for the eyes. The classic pairing of chrysanthemum with goji berries — the tea you find in nearly every Chinese home and every TCM clinic — is designed to address "liver fire rising" (肝火上升). In TCM, this pattern is associated with red, dry, or tired eyes, blurred vision, headaches, and irritability. Chrysanthemum cools the liver and clears the head; goji berries nourish the liver and replenish the eyes. Together they are believed to support comfortable, rested eyes during long working days.

Calming a Restless Mind

Because heat in the Liver can disturb the mind, chrysanthemum has a gentle reputation as a calming flower. It is sometimes included in tea blends for people who feel irritable, restless, or easily frustrated. In TCM food therapy, cooling the excess heat is seen as one way to help the mind settle back into balance.

Practical Guide: Choosing and Brewing Chrysanthemum

Common Varieties

Not all chrysanthemum is the same. In Chinese apothecaries you will usually find three types:

  • Hang Ju (杭菊) — the white or yellow blossoms from Hangzhou, considered the classic tea grade with a mild, sweet taste.
  • Gong Ju (贡菊) — a "tribute" grade from Anhui, prized for purity and used widely for eye health.
  • Hua Ju (花菊) or wild chrysanthemum (野菊) — smaller and more bitter, with a stronger cooling action, usually reserved for medicinal use.

How to Choose Quality Flowers

Good dried chrysanthemum should have intact petals, a clear floral aroma, and a natural pale yellow or white color. Avoid flowers that look dusty, browned, or smell musty. Store them in an airtight container away from light and moisture — they keep well for about a year.

How to Brew the Perfect Cup

The classic brew is simple:

  1. Place 4–6 dried chrysanthemum flowers (about 1–2 grams) in a cup or glass teapot.
  2. Pour in hot water just off the boil (about 85–90°C / 185–195°F).
  3. Let it steep for 3–5 minutes, until the water turns pale gold.
  4. Enjoy plain, or add a few goji berries and a touch of rock sugar or honey.

For a stronger eye-care blend, try 5 flowers with 8–10 goji berries and a small slice of licorice. For a cooling summer drink, brew it with a few mint leaves and serve over ice.

Dosage Guidelines

In TCM food therapy, 1–2 cups of chrysanthemum tea per day is considered a gentle, sustainable amount. Herbal infusions made for short-term relief, such as during a hot, headachy day, can be stronger and more frequent, but long-term daily use is best kept light.

Cautions and Contraindications

Chrysanthemum is cooling, so people with a cold constitution — those who often feel cold, have weak digestion, or loose stools — are traditionally advised to use it in moderation or pair it with warming ingredients. Some people are sensitive to flowers from the daisy family (Asteraceae); if you have known allergies to ragweed, marigolds, or daisies, test chrysanthemum with care. Pregnant or nursing women and anyone managing a chronic condition should check with a qualified healthcare provider before making herbal teas part of a daily routine.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is chrysanthemum tea good for in TCM?

Traditionally, chrysanthemum is used to clear heat, soothe liver fire, and support eye health. It is a classic tea for headaches, tired eyes, and irritability associated with heat rising to the head.

Can I drink chrysanthemum tea every day?

One or two cups a day is generally considered gentle and safe in TCM food therapy. People with cold constitution or weak digestion are often advised to drink it in moderation or balance it with goji berries or a little honey.

What does chrysanthemum pair well with?

Goji berries are the classic partner for eye support, honeysuckle adds stronger heat-clearing action, and mint is a good match for headaches. Rock sugar or honey rounds out the taste.

Who should avoid chrysanthemum tea?

Those with cold hands and feet, weak digestion, or yang deficiency should use it sparingly, and pregnant women should consult a healthcare provider before drinking herbal teas regularly.
