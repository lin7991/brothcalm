Ejiao (Donkey-Hide Gelatin) — TCM's Blood-Building Essence

🌿 Ingredient Deep Dive · 8 min read

Walk through any traditional Chinese pharmacy and you will see ejiao displayed like jewelry — glossy black squares wrapped in red silk, priced far above the herbs around them. In Chinese food therapy, ejiao (阿胶, donkey-hide gelatin) has been treasured for over two thousand years as one of the most prized substances for building blood and nourishing the body's deepest reserves.

For Western readers meeting ejiao for the first time, it can sound unusual: gelatin made from donkey hide. Yet in TCM tradition it is anything but exotic — it is a classic ingredient in soups, tonics, and postpartum recovery meals, and it appears in some of the most famous formulas in Chinese medicine. This guide explains what ejiao is, what TCM tradition says it does, how to use it in everyday cooking, and who should be cautious.

What Is Ejiao?

Ejiao is a refined gelatin produced by simmering donkey hide for many hours until the liquid concentrates into a thick extract, which is then cooled and cut into dark blocks. The name comes from Dong'e County (东阿) in Shandong Province, China, where the traditional production method uses the mineral-rich water of the local A River — a detail that TCM practitioners have considered essential for quality for centuries.

The finished product is hard, glossy, and almost black, with a faint animal aroma. Before use, it is typically crushed or melted so it can dissolve into warm liquids. In the Chinese pharmacopeia it is classified as a tonifying substance — something that helps restore what the body has lost, rather than a remedy aimed at a single symptom.

Ejiao is listed in the Shennong Bencao Jing (《神农本草经》), the ancient Chinese herbal classic compiled around the Han dynasty, where it is ranked among the top grade (上品) substances — those considered safe for long-term use and beneficial for general vitality. It remains one of the most respected items in TCM food therapy today.

The TCM Properties of Ejiao

In TCM theory, every food carries a nature (性), a flavor (味), and a set of channels it enters (归经). Understanding these helps explain when ejiao is traditionally chosen:

  • Nature: Neutral to slightly warm (平)
  • Flavor: Sweet (甘)
  • Channels: Lung (肺), Liver (肝), and Kidney (肾)

The sweet flavor is considered nourishing and harmonizing in TCM — it "builds up" rather than disperses. Entering the Liver channel links ejiao to the blood (the Liver is said to store blood), while its Lung channel connection ties it to moisture and the respiratory system, and the Kidney connection reaches the body's constitutional essence (jing, 精).

Traditional Uses in Chinese Medicine

Nourishing Blood (Bu Xue)

Ejiao's signature role in TCM tradition is nourishing blood (补血). It is traditionally used for the signs TCM associates with blood deficiency: pale complexion, dizziness, fatigue, dry hair, scanty or irregular menstruation, and poor sleep. In modern terms, these patterns often overlap with what Western medicine calls anemia, which is why ejiao is frequently mentioned in TCM conversations about anemia and recovery.

It is rarely used alone. A classic pairing is ejiao with dang gui (Angelica sinensis) and cooked rehmannia — a combination that appears in formulas such as Ejiao Siwu Tang, which builds on the famous Four Substances formula. In home cooking, the same idea appears as soups combining ejiao with goji berries, jujube dates, and longan, all of which are traditionally considered blood-supporting foods.

Nourishing Yin and Moistening the Lungs

Because ejiao is believed to nourish yin (the body's cooling, moistening aspect) and enter the Lung channel, TCM tradition uses it for dry conditions — dry cough with little phlegm, a parched throat, and skin that feels dry or brittle. It is often combined with moisture-supporting ingredients such as lily bulb or pear in tonics aimed at autumn dryness.

This moistening quality is also why ejiao shows up in beauty-focused TCM food therapy. The idea is not that ejiao "fixes" skin directly, but that nourishing blood and yin from the inside is believed to support a healthy complexion. Traditional texts describe a face with adequate blood as "bright and lustrous" (面色红润).

Supporting Hemostasis (Stopping Bleeding)

In classical TCM, ejiao was also used for bleeding patterns — persistent menstrual bleeding, blood in the stool, or bleeding that accompanies a yin deficiency. Formulas such as Jiao Ai Tang (胶艾汤), recorded in the Jingui Yaolue (《金匮要略》), combine ejiao with mugwort and other herbs for this purpose. This is a strong-action use that belongs firmly in the hands of a trained practitioner — in daily food therapy, the focus is on the gentle blood-nourishing role.

How to Use Ejiao in Daily Cooking

Ejiao is not chewed or eaten raw. The traditional way is to dissolve it into a warm liquid, or to simmer it into soups and congee. Because it is potent, small amounts are the norm — roughly 3 to 9 grams per serving (about the size of half a walnut), and not every day unless a practitioner advises otherwise.

Ejiao and Goji Berry Tea

A gentle everyday tonic:

  • Crush 3–5 g of ejiao into small pieces.
  • Warm 1 cup of milk or water; do not boil aggressively.
  • Stir in the ejiao until fully dissolved.
  • Add 1 tablespoon of goji berries and 2–3 red jujube dates (sliced).
  • Steep for 5 minutes, then sip warm — traditionally recommended in the morning or early evening.

Ejiao in Soup or Congee

Ejiao pairs well with slow-simmered dishes. Add crushed ejiao in the last 10 minutes of cooking so the gelatin melts into the broth without being overheated:

  • Chicken and goji soup: simmer chicken, goji berries, jujube, and a slice of ginger; add ejiao at the end.
  • Black sesame congee: stir dissolved ejiao into warm congee with a little brown sugar.
  • Longan egg sweet soup: a Cantonese-style dessert where ejiao, longan, and a poached egg are gently cooked together.

For anyone who finds the aroma strong, mixing ejiao into warm milk with honey or into red date tea is the classic way to soften it.

Who Should Be Cautious

Ejiao is rich, sticky, and heavy to digest, so TCM tradition lists several groups who should avoid it or use it only with guidance:

  • Weak digestion: people with a sluggish spleen and stomach, loose stools, or a tendency to bloating are traditionally told to avoid ejiao, because it is considered "cloying" (滋腻) and hard to break down.
  • Dampness or phlegm: if you feel heavy, have a thick tongue coating, or produce excess mucus, ejiao is usually not recommended.
  • Excess heat: during acute fever, infection, or strong inflammation, tonics like ejiao are traditionally paused.
  • Pregnancy: ejiao appears in classical formulas for threatened miscarriage, but this is practitioner territory. Pregnant women should never self-dose with ejiao.
  • Allergies: anyone allergic to animal-derived gelatin should avoid it entirely.

Because ejiao is an animal product with a real biological effect, it is also worth mentioning that its use is restricted or banned in some countries — for example, the European Union does not permit the import of donkey-hide gelatin. If you live outside Asia, buy from reputable specialty suppliers and check local regulations.

Buying Authentic Ejiao

Genuine ejiao is expensive, and counterfeit products are a well-documented problem. Quality cues used by TCM practitioners include:

  • Appearance: authentic blocks are dark brown to black, glossy, and break with a clean edge; fakes often look dull or crumble.
  • Smell: real ejiao has a faint, smoky, slightly animal note — a strong chemical or plastic smell is a red flag.
  • Brand and paperwork: reputable Chinese brands print batch numbers and carry inspection certificates; buy from sources you trust.
  • Dissolving: authentic ejiao dissolves smoothly into warm liquid with little residue.

FAQ

Is ejiao safe to take every day?

In TCM tradition, ejiao is usually taken in small amounts (3–9 g) in short courses rather than daily. People with weak digestion, loose stools, or dampness are traditionally advised to avoid it. Check with a qualified practitioner before regular use.

Is ejiao suitable for vegetarians or vegans?

No — ejiao is made from donkey hide. Plant-based alternatives traditionally used for similar blood-nourishing goals include goji berries, jujube dates, longan, and cooked Chinese yam.

What does ejiao taste like?

Authentic ejiao has a mild, slightly sweet taste with a faint animal-like aroma. When dissolved it is gelatinous and smooth, which is why it is often mixed into warm milk, soups, or congee.

Can ejiao help with anemia?

In TCM tradition, ejiao is one of the most famous blood-nourishing substances, and it is commonly discussed for the patterns associated with anemia — fatigue, pale complexion, and dizziness. It is best used as part of a broader approach that includes iron-rich foods and professional medical care if anemia is diagnosed.
